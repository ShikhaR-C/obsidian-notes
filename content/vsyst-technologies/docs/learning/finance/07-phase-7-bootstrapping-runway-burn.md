# Phase 7 — Bootstrapping: Burn, Runway & Staying Alive Pre-Revenue

> Level: Intermediate | Time: ~1 hr | Outcome: you can compute VSYST's burn and runway to the day, name every lever that extends it, and run the cash discipline that keeps a self-funded company alive long enough to find its business model. This phase is the accounting engine under the [[startup-operations-plan|ops plan]]'s "weekly cash check".

---

## 1. Bootstrapped Means *You Are the Investor*

"Bootstrapping" = growing the company on **founder money + customer revenue**, without outside investors. Every rupee VSYST spends comes from your capital, your director's loans (Phase 6), or what DZZLO earns. That has one liberating consequence and one brutal one:

- **Liberating:** you own ~100% and answer to no one. No board, no dilution, no "grow-or-die" pressure.
- **Brutal:** there is **no cavalry**. When cash runs out, no investor wires a rescue. Your survival metric isn't profit or growth — it's **runway**, and protecting it is literally your only non-negotiable job as CFO.

The whole [[startup-operations-plan|ops plan]] Part 1 §2 said this in business terms ("cash, burn, runway — ten minutes every week, forever"). This phase gives you the *accounting mechanics* so those ten minutes are precise, not vibes.

## 2. Burn Rate — How Fast the Tank Empties

**Burn rate** = how much cash the company loses per month. Two flavours, and the difference matters:

| Term | Formula | Means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Gross burn** | Total monthly cash *out* | Everything you spend — the raw cost of existing |
| **Net burn** | Cash out − cash in | The *actual* monthly hole after any revenue |

**Pre-revenue, gross ≈ net** (revenue ≈ 0). As DZZLO earns, net burn shrinks below gross — and the day **net burn goes negative, you're cash-flow positive** (the business funds itself; Phase 10's target). Compute burn from *cash*, not the accrual P&L — remember the two clocks (Phase 3). Your net burn is essentially the **Operating Cash Flow** line, made monthly.

VSYST's likely monthly burn today:

| Cash out | ₹/month (illustrative) |
| --- | --- |
| Your founder salary | 50,000 |
| AWS / hosting | 8,000 |
| Software (Figma, tools, GitHub…) | 6,000 |
| Internet, phone | 2,000 |
| CA / compliance retainer | 5,000 |
| Domains, misc | 3,000 |
| **Gross burn** | **~74,000** |
| less: DZZLO revenue | − (near 0 today) |
| **Net burn** | **~74,000/month** |

## 3. Runway — How Many Months Until Zero

> **Runway (months) = Cash in bank ÷ Net monthly burn**

If VSYST has ₹6,00,000 in the bank and burns ₹74,000/month: **runway ≈ 8 months.** That's the single most important number you own. The rule from the ops plan, now with a why:

- **> 12 months:** comfortable — focus on building and finding the model.
- **6–12 months:** start planning your next move (revenue push, or raise).
- **< 6 months:** **this is a red alert. Fixing runway becomes your *only* job** — everything else waits. Below 3 months, options collapse (you negotiate from desperation, take bad terms, bad clients). Never let it get there quietly.

Runway isn't static — it moves every time burn or cash changes. A CFO watches the *trend*: is runway growing (good) or shrinking (act)? Track it weekly as a single line on a chart.

## 4. Where Bootstrap Money Comes From (in order of preference)

Not all funding is equal. Best sources first — the higher up, the less it costs you:

| Source | Cost to you | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Customer revenue** | **Free — it's the goal** | The only *sustainable* source. Everything else buys time to reach this |
| **Customer prepayments** | Free + positive | **DZZLO's superpower** — annual plans paid upfront fund you (§5) |
| **Founder capital / director's loan** | Your personal risk | Phase 6. Flexible, but finite and personal |
| **Services income** (agency work) | Your time | Fund the product from consulting — the classic path (ops plan's two models) |
| **Grants / govt schemes** | Free but slow | Startup India, state Chhattisgarh schemes, Startup grants — worth exploring |
| **Revenue-based financing** | Medium | Repay as a % of revenue; newer in India (GetVantage, etc.) |
| **Bank loan / credit line** | Interest + risk | Phase 6. Hard to get pre-revenue without collateral/guarantee |
| **Investor equity (VC/angel)** | **Ownership + control** | Phase 8. The most expensive money there is — use last, not first |

**The ops plan's stance, which is correct:** *you likely don't need investors yet.* A software company can fund itself from services + customer prepayments far longer than founders assume. Raising is a tool for *pouring fuel on a proven fire*, not for *lighting one* (Phase 8).

## 5. DZZLO's Cash Superpower: Annual Prepayments

This deserves its own section because it's specific to *your* product. A SaaS company that collects **annually, upfront** gets a massive cash advantage:

- A dealer paying ₹18,000 for a year *today* gives you ₹18,000 of cash *now*, though you'll *earn* it over 12 months (the unearned-revenue mechanic, Phase 6 §8).
- That upfront cash **funds your burn** — customers become your cheapest, non-dilutive "investors."
- 20 dealers on annual plans at ₹18,000 = ₹3,60,000 of cash that extends runway by ~5 months, while costing you 0% and no ownership.

This is why SaaS pricing (Phase 9) should *nudge toward annual* (a discount for paying yearly). It's the difference between a company that constantly scrambles for cash and one that funds its own growth. The accrual clock says you haven't "earned" it; the cash clock says it's paying your salary. Both are true — and for survival, cash wins.

## 6. The Cash-Discipline System (a CFO's daily hygiene)

Turn the Survival Law into habits and simple account structure:

1. **Separate accounts.** At minimum: an **operating account** and a **tax-reserve account**. The moment any revenue with GST/tax implications arrives, **sweep ~20–25% to the tax reserve** (the ops plan's rule — never spend money that's owed to the government). Consider a third "profit/runway" account (the *Profit First* system from the ops plan's reading list).
2. **Never touch committed money.** A customer's prepayment is partly *future cost*; tax collected is the *government's*. Mentally (and literally) ring-fence both.
3. **Weekly cash check, forever.** Cash balance, this week's net burn, updated runway. Ten minutes. Chart the runway line.
4. **13-week cash forecast.** Beyond the single runway number, keep a rolling 13-week (one quarter) week-by-week projection of cash in and out. It catches the "we're fine on average but broke in week 6 when the AWS annual bill and salary collide" problem that a single average hides.
5. **Collect fast, pay slow (ethically).** Invoice the day you deliver; chase the 60+ ageing bucket (Phase 2); use vendor credit terms fully. Every day you shorten collection extends runway.

## 7. Extending Runway — The Levers, Ranked

When runway gets tight, these are your moves, from least to most painful:

| Lever | How | Speed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Collect receivables** | Chase the 60+ bucket; offer a small early-pay discount | Days |
| **Push annual prepay** | Discount for yearly DZZLO plans → cash now | Days–weeks |
| **Cut discretionary spend** | Pause unused subs (ops plan: quarterly review), defer nice-to-haves | Immediate |
| **Raise prices** | On new customers first — highest-leverage, least painful | Weeks |
| **Add services revenue** | Take a consulting project to fund the product | Weeks |
| **Defer founder salary** | Convert to a director's-loan claim (Phase 6) you recover later | Immediate, but personal cost |
| **Cut variable/contractor cost** | Reduce the contractor spend before any core commitment | Weeks |
| **Raise external money** | Last resort pre-model — you'll get bad terms when desperate | Months |

Notice **raising money is *last*, not first.** Founders reach for dilution when the cheaper levers — collecting, pricing, trimming — do more, faster, and cost no ownership.

## 8. When Bootstrapping Stops Being Right

Bootstrapping is the correct default for VSYST *right now* (pre-revenue, no proven model — investors won't fund that on good terms anyway). You *consider* raising (Phase 8) only when **all** of these are true:

- You have a **proven, repeatable business model** (Phase 9) — customers pay, you know your unit economics.
- **Money is the *only* thing** between you and much faster growth (not "we're not sure what works but cash would help").
- The **market rewards speed** — a land-grab where a competitor with capital could lock you out.

Until then, capital wouldn't accelerate you; it would just let you burn faster while still guessing. **First find the model (Phase 9); then decide whether to pour fuel on it (Phase 8).** Raising to *discover* a model is how founders give away ownership for nothing.

## 9. A Note on Your Own Salary

Counterintuitive but important: **pay yourself a modest, real salary from day one** (the ops plan says this too). Why a CFO insists on it:
- It keeps the entity boundary clean (Phase 1) — no ad-hoc withdrawals muddying the books.
- It makes your burn *honest* — a company that "works" only because the founder eats ₹0 isn't actually viable, and you'll misjudge the model.
- It protects *you* — desperate, unpaid founders make bad decisions (bad clients, bad terms).
Set it low enough to respect runway, high enough to live. Book it as salary (with TDS if above the limit), or, when cash is truly tight, accrue it as a director's-loan claim you recover later.

## 10. Exercises

**10.1 — Build your runway model (25 min).** In `finance-workbook/phase7-runway.xlsx`: list every real monthly cash-out line → gross burn; subtract realistic monthly revenue → net burn; divide current bank balance by net burn → **runway in months**. This is *the* number. Put it in a big cell at the top.

**10.2 — Three scenarios (15 min).** Copy the model into three columns: **Base** (today), **Lean** (cut discretionary spend + defer half your salary), **Growth** (add 15 DZZLO annual prepays at ₹18k). Compare the three runway numbers. Feel how much each lever from §7 actually buys you.

**10.3 — 13-week cash forecast (20 min).** Lay out the next 13 weeks as columns. Drop in expected cash-in (any revenue, prepayments) and cash-out (salary dates, AWS, the quarterly/annual bills, CA fees). Find the *lowest* weekly balance — that trough, not the average, is your real constraint. Note the week it happens and why.

**10.4 — Set your alert (5 min).** Decide VSYST's runway red line (e.g. "below 5 months, finding cash becomes my only job") and write it at the top of the runway model. Pre-committing to the line now stops a future emotional you from ignoring it.
