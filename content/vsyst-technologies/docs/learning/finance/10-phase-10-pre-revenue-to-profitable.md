# Phase 10 — Pre-Revenue to Profitable: The Founder-CFO Playbook

> Level: Advanced | Time: ~1.5 hr | Outcome: you can run VSYST's finance function on a real cadence — the metrics to watch at each stage, the monthly/quarterly rhythm, budget-vs-actual discipline — and, above all, decide *when and how* to flip VSYST from burning cash to making money. This is where everything from Phases 1–9 becomes a job you *do*, not just know.

---

## 1. What a CFO Actually Does (the job you're taking)

Strip away the title and a startup CFO does four things, in order of maturity:

| Hat | The work | Where it lives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Steward** | Keep the books clean, stay compliant, don't run out of cash | Phases 1–7 |
| **Operator** | Run the monthly close, report numbers, manage collections & spend | This phase |
| **Strategist** | Model decisions, price, decide raise-vs-profit | Phases 8–9 |
| **Storyteller** | Explain the numbers to investors, banks, co-founders | Phases 3, 8 |

As founder-CFO you wear all four. Early on you're 80% steward; as DZZLO earns, you shift toward strategist. The mistake is staying *only* a bookkeeper (numbers with no decisions) or *only* a dreamer (decisions with no numbers). The rhythm in §3 keeps you balanced.

## 2. The Metrics That Matter — By Stage

Watching the *wrong* metric for your stage wastes attention. The right dashboard changes as VSYST grows:

| Stage | Watch obsessively | Ignore (for now) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Pre-revenue** (you, today) | **Runway, net burn**, first paying customers, cash low-point | Profit margins, valuation, Rule of 40 |
| **Early revenue** | **MRR growth, churn, CAC payback**, unit economics (Phase 9) | Vanity metrics (signups, downloads) |
| **Scaling** | **Net Revenue Retention, LTV:CAC trend, magic number**, gross margin | — |
| **Profitability** | **Operating margin, cash conversion, Rule of 40** | Growth-at-all-costs |

**Your dashboard *right now* is small and brutal:** cash in bank, net burn, runway, and "number of paying dealers." That's it. Everything else is a distraction until you have revenue. When you have revenue, add MRR + churn. Don't build a 30-metric dashboard for a company with 3 customers — it's procrastination dressed as rigour.

**Two later metrics worth pre-loading:**
- **Rule of 40** — `revenue growth % + profit margin % ≥ 40`. A healthy SaaS can trade growth for margin and vice-versa, but the *sum* should clear 40. It's how you'll later judge "are we growing efficiently or just burning?"
- **Magic number** — new ARR ÷ prior-period sales & marketing spend. > ~0.75 means sales spend pays back efficiently — the green light to spend *more* on growth.

## 3. The Founder-CFO Operating Rhythm

Finance isn't a year-end panic; it's a **cadence**. Steal this calendar:

| Cadence | Do this | Time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Weekly** | Cash balance, net burn, runway (update the chart). Chase the 60+ receivables bucket. | 15 min |
| **Monthly** | **Close the books**: reconcile bank (Phase 2), review P&L + cash flow, update the model's actuals, budget-vs-actual (§4). Produce a 1-page "management account". | 2–3 hrs |
| **Quarterly** | Review unit economics & the model. Re-forecast the rest of the year. Cancel unused subscriptions (ops plan). Check compliance calendar (Phase 5) for the quarter's filings. | Half day |
| **Annually** | Statutory audit + all ROC/tax filings (Phase 5). Set next year's budget. Review pricing. The big strategic "raise vs profit" call (§5). | Multi-day, with CA |

> **The monthly close is the keystone habit.** A founder who closes the books every month — reconciles, reviews, compares to plan — is *never surprised*. One who "checks in when there's time" gets ambushed by a cash crunch or a tax bill. The close is where you catch the AWS charge you forgot, the customer who silently churned, the vendor you double-paid. Two hours a month buys you the right to sleep.

## 4. Budgeting, Forecasting & Variance (managing on purpose)

Three related habits that turn the model (Phase 9) into control:

- **Budget** — at year start, decide what you *plan* to earn and spend, month by month. It's a commitment, not a wish.
- **Forecast** — a *rolling* re-estimate as reality unfolds (a 12-month rolling forecast beats a static annual budget you ignore by March).
- **Variance analysis** — each month, compare **actual vs budget** and ask *why* on every meaningful gap. "Marketing was ₹40k over — was it worth it? AWS doubled — what changed?"

```
              Budget    Actual   Variance   Why?
  Revenue     50,000    38,000   −12,000    2 dealers slipped to next month
  Salaries    50,000    50,000        0     —
  AWS          8,000    14,000   − 6,000    forgot annual reserved-instance bill
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Net burn   −60,000   −78,000   −18,000    ← runway shrinking faster than planned → act
```

Variance is your **feedback loop** — the difference between driving with your eyes open vs. hoping. The ops plan's "judge on 90-day trends, not this week's mood" is variance analysis in disguise.

## 5. When (and How) to Flip From Burning to Profitable — *the* question you asked

This is the strategic heart of being a founder-CFO. Two framings make it decidable.

### 5a. Default-Alive vs Default-Dead (compute this)

Paul Graham's test, and the single most clarifying question for a pre-revenue founder:

> **At your current growth rate and current burn, will you reach profitability *before* the money runs out?**
> - **Yes → "default alive."** You control your destiny; you can choose to grow faster *or* coast to profit.
> - **No → "default dead."** Without a change (more revenue, less burn, or a raise), you die. **Knowing this early is everything.**

Compute it from your Phase-9 model: project revenue growth and burn forward — does the cash line cross zero *after* the profit line crosses zero, or before? Most founders *feel* alive while being *default dead*, because optimism hides the crossover. Run the numbers, not the vibe.

### 5b. Growth vs Profitability — the deliberate trade

There are two valid strategies, and they suit different companies:

| | **Optimise growth** (burn to grow) | **Optimise profitability** (grow efficiently) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who it suits | VC-backed, land-grab markets, winner-take-all | **Bootstrapped, niche-leader, control-focused** |
| Cash | Burn now for market share | Self-funding, durable |
| Risk | Depends on the next raise | Depends on execution |
| **Fit for VSYST** | Only *after* a proven model + a real land-grab reason | **The natural default** — bootstrapped, sticky niche (fuel dealers), no need to torch cash |

**VSYST's honest default is profitability/efficiency**, not growth-at-all-costs — you're bootstrapped, in a defensible niche, and control-focused. That means: **flip toward profitable as soon as the model is validated**, rather than manufacturing a burn you don't need.

### 5c. The signals it's time to flip toward profit

Flip when **most** of these are true:
- **The model is validated** (Phase 9): customers pay a real price, unit economics clear the bars, churn is low.
- **Runway pressure** without an *attractive* raise available (raising from weakness is worse than trimming to profit).
- **The market isn't a land-grab** — no competitor can lock you out by outspending you (true for a relationship-driven, sticky niche like fuel-dealer credit management).
- **You value control/optionality** over hyper-scale — a profitable company that owns itself has every option; a burning one is on a clock.

### 5d. How to flip (the levers)

Flipping to profit = making `revenue ≥ operating cost` durably. The moves, most to least valuable:
1. **Raise prices** (new customers first) — highest leverage, since software margin is ~100% on each extra rupee.
2. **Push annual prepay & retention** — cash now + lower churn lifts LTV (Phases 7, 9).
3. **Focus spend on what acquires paying customers**; kill everything else (variance analysis flags it).
4. **Hold headcount** until revenue justifies each hire (model it first — Phase 9).
5. **Cut the burn** you added on optimism.

The target is the moment from Phase 3: **Operating Cash Flow turns positive.** That's the day VSYST stops needing your money to survive — the definition of "profitable" that actually matters for a bootstrapper.

## 6. Being Board- & Audit-Ready (even as a solo founder-CFO)

Run the finance function as if someone's watching — because eventually someone will (an auditor yearly, an investor in DD, a bank for a loan):
- **Monthly management accounts** — even a 1-pager: cash, runway, MRR, burn, budget-vs-actual. Email it to yourself/co-founders. This *is* the board pack you'll later send investors.
- **Clean, current books** — the monthly close (§3) *is* audit-readiness. A company that closes monthly has a painless year-end; one that doesn't has a March nightmare (ops plan's mistake #9).
- **A documented decision trail** — why you priced X, why you hired Y. Future-you and future investors will ask.

## 7. Founder-CFO Mistakes to Avoid

Distilled from the ops plan's realities + this course:
- **Confusing revenue with cash** (Survival Law) — a signed deal isn't money.
- **Watching vanity metrics** (signups) instead of paying customers and runway.
- **No monthly close** — then a year-end horror show.
- **Ignoring the crossover** — feeling default-alive while being default-dead.
- **Raising to avoid the hard work** of pricing and retention.
- **Underpricing** and being unable to raise it later.
- **Building a 30-metric dashboard** for a 3-customer company — rigour theatre.

## 8. Exercises

**8.1 — Build your CFO dashboard (20 min).** In `finance-workbook/phase10-dashboard.xlsx`, make the *one-page* monthly management account: cash, net burn, runway, paying customers, (later) MRR & churn, and a budget-vs-actual block. This is the artifact you'll produce every month forever.

**8.2 — Compute default-alive/dead (20 min).** From your Phase-9 model, project revenue and burn forward and find whether the profit line crosses zero *before* or *after* cash hits zero. Write the verdict — "default alive" or "default dead" — and, if dead, the single biggest lever (price? burn? one more customer cohort?) that flips it.

**8.3 — Run a variance month (15 min).** Take last month's plan vs actuals (or invent a realistic month) and build the §4 variance table. Write the "why" on every gap over ₹5,000. Notice how it changes what you'd do next month.

**8.4 — Write your flip-to-profit criteria (10 min).** Using §5c, write the 3–4 specific conditions under which VSYST should stop optimising for anything but profitability. Date it. Revisit it each quarter — it turns an emotional, in-the-moment call into a pre-committed, rational one.
