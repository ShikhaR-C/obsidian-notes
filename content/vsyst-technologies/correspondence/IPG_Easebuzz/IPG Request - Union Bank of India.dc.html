Integrated Payment Gateway for DZZLO Fuel Payments

A request to Union Bank of India for the net-banking rail and its commercial terms.
Prepared for  Union Bank of India From  Vsyst Technologies Date  16 July 2026

DZZLO connects transport firms with petrol-pump dealers: customers order fuel on credit, and a shared ledger records every invoice for both sides to see. Payment is the one step still outside the platform — today the customer leaves the app, opens their own net banking, and makes the transfer by hand. This note sets out why that gap matters, what the gateway must carry, and where the Bank's current offer falls short.

1Why we need an integrated payment gateway

Every problem below follows from payment sitting outside the platform.

Today, without a gateway With the gateway inside DZZLO
The dealer must be set up and maintained as a beneficiary in the customer's own bank — outside the platform, unlinked to any invoice. No beneficiary management. The dealer is already on the payment screen.
Everything is typed by hand. On transfers averaging ₹1 lakh and reaching ₹5 lakh, one mistyped digit is a serious incident. Invoice, dealer and amount are pre-filled from the bill. No entry, so no room for human error.
A payment sends nothing back. Money moves in the bank while the invoice stays open; acknowledgment is manual and disputable. Payment posts against the invoice and closes the shared ledger the instant it is made — one entry, seen identically by both sides.
Paying is bound to the working day. A customer at their limit must pay to place the next order, but it is recognised only when staff read the statement next morning. The customer pays 24×7, unattended; the invoice confirms on the spot, the limit frees, and the next order goes through at any hour. Easebuzz settles to the dealer on T+1.
UPI does not fit the business. Firms paying firms move money on NEFT and RTGS — averaging ₹1 lakh, against ₹5,000 on UPI. The gateway carries the rails firms actually use — NEFT and RTGS alongside UPI.

Credit opens on the gateway's confirmation of payment, not on settlement into the dealer's account. The moment the gateway confirms, the customer's money has already left their account into the aggregator's escrow, held under RBI payment-aggregator authorisation; T+1 settlement is a regulated cycle, not a counterparty risk. Today the dealer's only alternative is to release credit against a chat-app screenshot he cannot verify. The gateway replaces an unverifiable promise with a confirmed receipt — and lets the dealer sell fuel at 2 a.m. that he does not sell at all today.

The integration is the point, not the gateway alone. A gateway on its own only moves money; the payment must still be found and matched to a bill afterwards. No other platform in this market carries a common ledger. Integrated into DZZLO, payment is made against a selected invoice and lands in a ledger the dealer and customer already share, closing on both sides at once — reconciliation is not reduced, it is removed.

2What the gateway must provide

  • —Core requirement — net banking (NEFT, RTGS, IMPS). Firm-to-firm fuel payments move by bank transfer. IMPS and NEFT carry the everyday ₹5,000–₹2 lakh range; RTGS carries ₹2 lakh and above, where our transfers reach ₹5 lakh.
  • —In support — UPI, debit and credit card, and CBDC as and when the Bank's rails are available, so no customer is turned away for want of a method they hold.
  • —Why not UPI alone. It does not fit the ticket (average ₹1 lakh, reaching ₹5 lakh; not built for firm-to-firm), and the UPI market is already captured. Net banking for firm-to-firm fuel payments is still an open rail — the one where Union Bank of India owns the relationship rather than rents it.
  • —QR and POS do not close the loop. They are not interconnected with DZZLO, so the invoice stays open and the credit limit never frees. An integrated gateway is the only form that closes the loop.
  • —Every rail on every platform. DZZLO runs on Android, iOS and the web; each option must work on all three.

The commercials — the charge must be waived. Pump prices are set by the oil marketing companies and the dealer's commission is a fixed amount per litre, so the dealer cannot raise his price to absorb a transaction charge; and the customer will not pay a fee to settle a bill he already owes. The arithmetic settles it — dealer commission on diesel is ₹2.2 per litre, about 2.4% of a sale at a pump price near ₹93 per litre:

On a ₹1,00,000 diesel order
Diesel dispensed ≈ 1,075 litres
Dealer's gross commission (₹2.2 / litre) ₹2,366
Gateway charge at 1.8% MDR (typical net banking) ₹1,800 — 76% of commission
Gateway charge at 1% MDR ₹1,000 — 42% of commission

And ₹2.2 is gross commission, before the cost of running the outlet. No percentage-based charge survives this business, and the problem worsens as tickets rise toward ₹5 lakh. We therefore request the Bank to waive the charge on this program, and to record that waiver in the terms and conditions rather than leave it as an informal concession. (UPI and RuPay debit already carry zero MDR by regulation, so this ask concerns net banking and cards.)

3The problem with the offer as it stands

The offer has two parts, and each on its own leaves the problem unsolved: net banking priced at 1.8% per transaction, and the program to start with UPI and RuPay debit only, net banking to follow.

The charge is on the wrong axis — and so is the Bank's own cost. At ₹2.2 per litre the dealer earns roughly 2.4% of the sale, so 1.8% takes about 76% of his gross commission, and because commission and MDR both scale with litres the ratio holds at every ticket size. But the Bank's own cost is not a percentage either: Easebuzz charges per transaction, and a net-banking payment is a fixed piece of work whatever it carries — no interchange to fund, no credit extended. The Bank's cost scales with the number of transactions; the quoted price scales with their value. We therefore ask to be charged on the axis the Bank is charged on — a flat fee per net-banking transaction, at whatever level covers the Easebuzz cost and the Bank's margin over it.

Net banking is the lowest-risk rail in the offer. It carries no chargeback: the payer authenticates inside their own bank and pushes the money, with no reversal right against the merchant. Debit cards — the instrument the Bank proposes to start with — do carry chargeback exposure. And there is no dispute to speak of: an identified firm pays against a specific invoice for fuel dispensed into a vehicle that was present.

A UPI-and-RuPay start proves nothing. Those are precisely the rails our customers will not use. The result is not a smaller launch but one where nothing moves — customers keep transferring by hand outside the app, and the program reads as having failed for want of demand when it was never given the rail its demand runs on.

What we ask, in order of preference:

  1. A waiver of the net-banking charge, recorded in the terms and conditions.
  2. Failing that, a flat fee per transaction — any reasonable amount, so long as it is not a percentage of the transfer. The Bank sets the level; we ask only that it sit on the same axis as the Bank's own cost.

If the waiver is governed by a threshold — a minimum number of onboarded dealers, a committed monthly transaction count or value, or a balance to be maintained — we ask the Bank to state it; we would far rather meet a stated condition than argue against a rate. Every dealer we bring onto this program is a current account with Union Bank of India, with the outlet's settlement and float running through it. To support the Bank's own calculation, we will provide projected transaction volumes across 10–15 dealers, built from observed throughput at the outlets.

We would welcome the opportunity to discuss terms at the Bank's convenience.
