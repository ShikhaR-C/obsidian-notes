## Explain-it-like-I'm-5

A tanker leaves a depot outside Raipur at four in the morning, loaded for a mine site past Kondagaon. The driver has two numbers on his mind and only one of them is on the dashboard. The dashboard number is **how much diesel is in the tank**. The number that decides whether he sleeps tonight is **how many kilometres to the next pump that will serve him** — which is not the same thing at all, because it depends on the road, the load, the gradient and whether the pump on the bypass is open at that hour.

Two drivers can carry identical fuel and be in completely different situations. One is on a good road with three pumps ahead of him. The other is on a broken stretch with one pump that shuts at nine. The gauge tells them the same story. The reality does not.

Now watch what the careful driver does that the careless one does not. He decides **at half tank**, not at quarter, what he will do — because at half tank he has choices: take the longer road with the reliable pump, or the shorter road and top up now. At quarter tank he has one choice. At the red light he has none, and whatever the pump on the bypass charges him, he pays.

That is the whole of this lesson. **Cash in the bank is the gauge. Runway is the distance to the next pump. And every decision worth making about money gets made while you still have half a tank.** The rest — schemes, debt, investors, term sheets — is just the list of pumps, what each one charges, and which of them will not serve a truck like yours.

## 1. The one financial duty a CEO cannot delegate

Fred Wilson's reduction of the CEO job to three things ends with the blunt one: _make sure there is always enough cash in the bank_ ([AVC — What A CEO Does](https://avc.com/2010/08/what-a-ceo-does/), 2010). Notice the grammar. Not "manage finances". Not "monitor cash". **Make sure there is always enough.** It is written as an outcome you are personally responsible for, and it is the only one of the three that has a date attached to it.

This is not the same job as the finance job. [[finance/00_README|Finance for Founders]] owns the accounting — the books, the statements, the filings, the model. [[06-money-rails-and-finance-operations|COO lesson 06]] owns the rails and the routines: the current account and maker-checker, who may spend what, the vendor payments calendar, the collections ladder, the weekly cash check, the 13-week forecast's mechanics. Both of those are real jobs and neither is this one.

**This lesson owns the CEO's capital job**, which is four decisions and one number:

### 1.1 Why this is the duty that kills companies

CB Insights' analysis of 431 shut-down companies puts **"ran out of capital" in 70% of failure post-mortems** — the single most common ending. But read their own qualification, because it is the part founders skip: running out of money is described as _the final cause of death, not the root problem_, sitting downstream of poor product–market fit (43%) and bad timing (29%) ([CB Insights, 2026](https://www.cbinsights.com/research/report/startup-failure-reasons-top/)).

That distinction changes what you do about it. If cash were the root cause, the answer would be "get more cash". Because it is the mechanism of death rather than the disease, **the answer is: buy enough time to fix the actual disease, and know exactly how much time you have bought.** Runway is not a comfort number. It is the length of your remaining experiment.

Paul Graham's test is the sharpest one-line version and it costs nothing to run: **are you default alive or default dead** — assuming current expenses and current growth, do you make it to profitability on the money you already have ([Paul Graham — Default Alive or Default Dead?](https://www.paulgraham.com/aord.html), 2015)? Graham's observation is the useful part: most founders do not know, and the ones who do not know are usually default dead. His secondary point matters more for VSYST — the trajectory question can only be answered if the company's growth rate is measured at all, and a pre-revenue company has no growth rate. **For a pre-revenue company the honest answer is "default dead, on a fixed clock", and the whole game is shortening the distance to first revenue while lengthening the clock.**

### 1.2 The three numbers, said from memory

The test that this section passes or fails on is embarrassingly simple, and both other founder-directors should be able to pass it too:

1. **How much cash is in the company's accounts today?** To the nearest ten thousand rupees.
2. **What is the net burn?** Trailing three months, not last month — one month contains an annual bill and lies to you.
3. **Which calendar month does the money run out?** Not "about eight months". _"March."_

If any of the three requires opening a spreadsheet, the duty is not being discharged. [[03-the-ceo-core-value-and-the-founder-contracts|Lesson 03]] already put this on the CEO's own scorecard as row 2 with a standing rule: **never below the agreed red line without a written plan dated before you crossed it.** This lesson is where that red line gets designed.

One more discipline, and it is a governance point rather than a finance point. **The number is a board number.** In an Indian Private Limited company the directors are collectively responsible, and "I assumed the technical director was watching the cash" is not a defence available to anyone ([[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties|lesson 09]]). Put cash, burn and runway as a standing first item in every directors' meeting pack ([[C17-board-pack-agenda-and-minutes|C17]]) so that the number is minuted monthly and nobody can later claim they did not know.

## 2. Burn and runway, properly

### 2.1 The two burns, and why the difference matters more later than now

**Gross burn** is total cash out per month. **Net burn** is cash out minus cash in. Pre-revenue they are the same number, which is exactly why founders form the habit of quoting one and meaning the other and then get confused eighteen months later when the two diverge. Learn the distinction now, when it costs nothing.

Three refinements that the textbook version leaves out and that will bite an Indian SaaS company specifically:

- **Burn is a cash concept, not a P&L concept.** The accrual books and the bank statement run on different clocks ([[finance/03-phase-3-three-statements|Finance Phase 3]]). Compute burn from the bank, always.
- **An annual prepayment is cash in, but it is not revenue yet.** A dealer paying twelve months up front hands you cash today that you earn over twelve months. Your net burn improves immediately; your P&L does not. Both are true, and for survival the cash clock wins — but do not let it make you feel more profitable than you are. §6 treats this properly.
- **GST is not yours.** The 18% you collect on a SaaS invoice under SAC 998434 sits in your bank and belongs to the government. So does TDS you deduct. The COO course's tax-reserve sweep exists precisely so that the burn number is not quietly funded by money you owe ([[06-money-rails-and-finance-operations|COO lesson 06]] §7).

Three CEO-level observations about that table that a bookkeeper will not make for you:

1. **Only one line is genuinely variable with product usage** (SMS/OTP, plus WhatsApp). Everything else is either a decision you made once or a decision you are renewing by not cancelling. That means **your burn is almost entirely a set of past decisions, and cutting it is almost entirely a matter of reversing them.** That is good news and it is also the reason burn creeps: nobody ever notices a subscription.
2. **The field-travel line is the only cost that behaves like an investment.** Cut it last. Cutting travel to save ₹12,000 while the company's problem is that no dealer has paid yet is not thrift; it is the fastest available way to run out of runway more slowly _and_ more certainly.
3. **Founder drawings are the burn.** At the illustrative numbers above they are roughly two-thirds of gross burn. Every conversation about "cutting costs" that does not start there is theatre.

### 2.3 Runway, and the 24-month view

Runway = cash ÷ net burn, and the only interesting thing about the formula is what you feed it. Three refinements:

- **Use trailing-three-month net burn**, not last month's. Annual bills, travel bursts and lumpy legal fees make any single month a liar.
- **Add committed but unpaid cash-outs** — the CA's annual fee, the audit, the trademark filing, the ISO gap-assessment quote you already accepted. Cash flow, not accruals, but _committed_ cash flow.
- **State the month, not the count.** "Seven months" invites rounding. "March" invites action.

Then extend it. A single runway number is a point estimate on a curve; what a CEO needs is **the 24-month cash curve** with the trough marked. The COO course owns the 13-week weekly view, which catches the "fine on average, broke in week six when the salary run and the annual AWS commitment collide" problem. The CEO owns the two-year monthly view, which catches a different problem: **the month in which a decision you have already made becomes unaffordable.** The first hire, the certification, the second district — each has a month attached, and the 24-month curve is where you find out whether that month exists.

| View                              | Granularity | Horizon       | Owner                           | Question it answers                  |
| --------------------------------- | ----------- | ------------- | ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| **Weekly cash check**             | Balance     | Now           | COO / whoever runs the bank     | Did anything surprising happen?      |
| **13-week cash forecast**         | Weekly      | One quarter   | COO, built with the founder-CFO | Which week do we go tight?           |
| **Runway number**                 | Monthly     | Single figure | **CEO**                         | What month does the money end?       |
| **24-month cash curve**           | Monthly     | Two years     | **CEO**                         | Which planned decision does not fit? |

### 2.4 The founder-salary question, decided as a capital question

The [[finance/07-phase-7-bootstrapping-runway-burn|Finance course's]] house call is correct and this course does not contradict it: **pay a modest, real salary from day one.** It keeps the entity boundary clean, makes burn honest, and protects the founders from the bad decisions that unpaid people make. What this lesson adds is the CEO framing.

At pre-revenue, **founder pay is a runway decision, not a market decision** — a point [[04-how-to-become-a-ceo-in-2026|lesson 04]] already makes. Three founder-directors drawing ₹50,000 each is a ₹6 lakh annual difference in runway against three drawing ₹30,000. That is not a small optimisation; at the illustrative burn above it is roughly four extra months of company.

Two mechanics worth knowing before the conversation happens, both **VERIFY LIVE with the CA and CS**:

- **Directors' remuneration in a private company needs to be properly authorised** — check the Articles and pass the resolution rather than discovering the gap in diligence two years later ([[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties|lesson 09]]).
- **Deferring a founder salary is not the same as not paying it.** Accruing it as a director's-loan claim keeps the books honest and preserves your ability to recover it later, but it also creates a liability and brings the deposit rules into view. Get the treatment right at the time, not retrospectively ([[finance/06-phase-6-loans-commission-investments-funds|Finance Phase 6]]).

And the family point, because this is a family-founded company: **the three founders should not automatically draw the same amount, and they should not automatically draw different amounts either.** Decide it explicitly, against contribution and against need, write it down, and revisit it at a fixed date rather than when somebody gets resentful. [[03-the-ceo-core-value-and-the-founder-contracts|Lesson 03]]'s founder contracts are the right home for the decision; [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] is the right home for the reasoning.

## 3. Scenarios, and the trigger ladder

### 3.1 Three cases from a small number of drivers

A scenario model is not a forecast. It is a device for finding out **which uncertainty actually matters**, and it fails the moment it grows more than about five inputs. Pierre Wack's original insight at Shell was that the value of scenarios is not prediction but changing the mental map of the decision-makers ([HBR — Scenarios: Uncharted Waters Ahead](https://hbr.org/1985/09/scenarios-uncharted-waters-ahead), 1985). At three people, the useful version is four drivers:

| Driver                                  | Base    | Bear                 | Bull                       | Where the number comes from                                                                          |
| --------------------------------------- | ------- | -------------------- | -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **New paying GSTINs per month**         | 2       | 0–1                  | 4                          | Your own pipeline, not a market estimate ([[07-customers-markets-and-founder-led-sales\|lesson 07]]) |
| **Average revenue per GSTIN per month** | ₹1,799  | ₹1,200 (discounting) | ₹2,400 (mix shifts to Pro) | The tier sheet in [[06-strategy-ii-moats-positioning-and-the-business-model\|lesson 06]] §7.3        |
| **Share paying annually up front**      | 30%     | 10%                  | 60%                        | §6                                                                                                   |
| **Monthly gross burn**                  | as §2.2 | +10% (creep)         | flat                       | Your own bank statements                                                                             |

Everything else — churn at small N, cost-to-serve per tenant, gateway fees — is noise at this size and belongs in [[C12-unit-economics-and-business-model-calculator|C12]], not in the runway model. **Resist the urge to make the model good.** A four-driver model you rebuild monthly beats a twenty-driver model you built once.

Three rules for using it:

1. **Never plan on the bull case.** Plan on base, survive on bear, and treat bull as a description of what you would do with a surprise, not as a budget.
2. **The bear case must be genuinely bad.** If your bear case still has you closing one dealer a month, it is not a bear case; it is a slightly damp base case. The real bear is: _nobody pays for nine months._ Model that, and find out what it costs you to survive it.
3. **Every scenario ends in a month, not a mood.** The output of the model is three dates. Write them on the same line.

### 3.2 The 12/9/6/3 trigger ladder

This is the mechanism that makes the whole section work, and it is the CEO course's version of a rule the COO course would recognise instantly: **decide the escalation while you are far away from it, because the version of you that is three months from zero is not a good decision-maker.** Andy Grove's point about the value of pre-set rules over in-the-moment judgment applies exactly here, and so does the reason the careful tanker driver decides at half tank.

Pre-commit this table, get all three directors to agree it, minute it, and put it at the top of [[C13-runway-burn-and-scenario-planner|C13]]:

| Runway          | What is true                                                                       | What the CEO's week looks like                                                                                                                              | What must not happen                                                                                | Who is told                                                |
| --------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| **> 12 months** | Normal operations. The clock is not the constraint                                 | Capital work is ~10% of the week: the monthly rebuild, the scheme applications on schedule (§5), the annual prepay push (§6)                                | Complacency — the model going unrebuilt for two months                                              | Monthly board pack                                         |
| **9 months**    | **The early-warning line.** Nothing is wrong yet, and that is exactly why it works | Freeze _new_ recurring commitments. | **6 months**    | **The red line.** Options are still open but narrowing                             | Execute the written plan. Two notes that keep this honest.

**The bands are not universal.** A company with a signed contract landing in month four has a different 6 than a company with an empty pipeline. Adjust the bands to VSYST's actual cash-conversion lag — the time from "dealer says yes" to "money in the account" — which for a sales-assisted district motion may be six to ten weeks. **Your red line must be at least one full cash-conversion cycle above zero, or the lever you pull at the red line cannot land in time.** That single sentence is the reason to set the line at 6 rather than 4.

**The ladder is a communication device as much as a finance one.** The most common way a bootstrapped family company dies is not the arithmetic; it is that one founder knew and the others found out late. Putting the bands in writing means the escalation happens automatically, without anyone having to be the person who raises the alarm.

## 4. The bootstrapped path, taken seriously

Bootstrapping is not the absence of a capital strategy. Done well it _is_ the capital strategy, and it has better evidence behind it in Indian software than almost anywhere else. Done badly it is martyrdom with a spreadsheet. This section is about telling the two apart.

### 4.1 The evidence, and what it does and does not prove

| Company           | What it did                                                                                                           | What it actually demonstrates                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 | Where it does not transfer to VSYST                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Zoho**          | Built to global scale with no outside capital, no IPO, funded from operating profit, R&D in small-town Tamil Nadu     | That a software company can compound for decades on customer money, and that a low-cost location is a durable structural advantage, not a compromise ([Forbes India — The Bootstrapped Buddhist](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/boardroom/the-bootstrapped-buddhist-how-sridhar-vembu-built-zoho/43383/1); [Sridhar Vembu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sridhar_Vembu)) | Zoho started with a services business (AdventNet) funding product development. VSYST has no services engine — the closest analogue is deliberately taking consulting work, which is a real option and a real distraction |
| **Zerodha**       | India's largest broker, founder-owned, never raised venture capital, distributes profit rather than chasing valuation | That in a market with a clear, chargeable transaction, capital is not the constraint — distribution and trust are ([Entrepreneur India — profitable bootstrapped startups](https://www.entrepreneur.com/en-in/news-and-trends/profitable-bootstrapped-startups-recipe-for-success/442592); revenue and profit figures **VERIFY LIVE**)                                        | Zerodha had a regulatory moat and a price cut that competitors could not match. VSYST's district-by-district motion has no such lever                                                                                    |
| **Wingify (VWO)** | Bootstrapped from Delhi, no venture round, reached substantial ARR and exited to a private-equity buyer               | That a bootstrapped Indian SaaS company can reach a real exit without ever raising, and that a profitable company is _bought_, not sold ([Inc42 — the Wingify story](https://inc42.com/startups/wingify-paras-chopra-saas-startup/); [YourStory](https://yourstory.com/2020/08/how-wingify-bootstrapped-and-built-a-profitable-co); exit valuation **VERIFY LIVE**)           | Wingify sold a horizontal product globally, self-serve, in dollars. VSYST sells a vertical product in one country, sales-assisted, in rupees                                                                             |

The pattern across all three is one thing and it is not "they were frugal". It is that **each had a business where a customer would pay early, in cash, for something the company could deliver without much capital.** That is the actual precondition for bootstrapping, and the honest question for VSYST is not "do we admire Zoho" but "**is DZZLO that kind of business?**"

The answer is closer to yes than most software: a dealer pays a subscription, ideally annually, in rupees, for software that costs very little to serve one more tenant. It is not an unqualified yes: the sales motion is field-based and slow, which means the cost of acquiring a district is paid in founder-months before any of it comes back.

### 4.3 The arithmetic of default-alive for VSYST

Run Graham's test in the only form that is meaningful pre-revenue. Take your bear-case burn. Take the number of paying GSTINs at ₹1,799 that would cover it. That number is **the smallest number that matters in this company**, and every founder should know it.

At an illustrative gross burn of ₹2,00,000 a month, the arithmetic is roughly:

```
  Monthly gross burn                      ₹2,00,000
  Growth-tier price, ex-GST               ₹1,799 / GSTIN / month
  ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Paying GSTINs to break even       ≈ 2,00,000 / 1,799  ≈  112

  With founder drawings cut to ₹90,000 total:
  Monthly gross burn                      ₹1,40,000
  Paying GSTINs to break even       ≈ 78
```

Two things fall out of that, and both are strategy findings rather than finance findings.

**First: the break-even number is a district-count question, not a sales-target question.** If a district contains a few hundred addressable pumps and lubricant distributors, break-even is one to three districts fully worked — not a national ambition. That is a genuinely encouraging answer and it should change how the strategy kernel is written ([[05-strategy-i-diagnosis-and-the-strategy-kernel|lesson 05]]).

**Second: cost discipline moves break-even more than heroics do.** Cutting ₹60,000 of monthly burn removes 34 dealers from the target. Selling 34 more dealers takes quarters; cutting ₹60,000 takes an afternoon and a difficult family conversation. That is not an argument for austerity — it is an argument for **knowing the exchange rate between rupees of burn and months of selling**, and for making sure every recurring commitment is priced in dealers rather than rupees.

Do this arithmetic in the `Runway & Scenarios` tab and put the resulting number on the wall. _"78."_

## 5. Non-dilutive money in India — the full stack

This is the section that pays for the lesson. Non-dilutive money is capital, credit, tax relief or cost reduction that **does not cost you ownership**. India has an unusually dense stack of it for a DPIIT-recognised startup, most of it is unclaimed, and most of the reason it is unclaimed is that each item is individually annoying and nobody sequences them.

Two rules before the table. **First: this is a scheduled programme, not an opportunistic one.** Assign each item an owner, a target month and a next action, and review it monthly like a pipeline. **Second: measure each one by realistic payoff per founder-hour**, not by headline value. A ₹20 lakh scheme with a 4% hit rate and forty hours of application work is worse than a ₹6 lakh certification subsidy you will certainly get.

### 5.2 DPIIT recognition — the gate everything else sits behind

Recognition by the Department for Promotion of Industry and Internal Trade is free, online, and the prerequisite for most of what follows. The criteria, as stated on the official page ([Startup India — DPIIT Startup Recognition](https://www.startupindia.gov.in/content/sih/en/startupgov/startup_recognition_page.html)):

- **Entity type:** Private Limited Company, registered Partnership Firm, LLP or Cooperative Society. VSYST qualifies.
- **Age:** up to **10 years** from incorporation — **20 years for DeepTech** startups.
- **Turnover:** less than **₹200 crore** in any previous financial year — **₹300 crore for DeepTech**.
- **Innovation:** working towards innovation or improvement of existing products, services or processes, with potential for employment generation or wealth creation.
- **Not** formed by splitting up or reconstruction of an existing business.

**VERIFY LIVE — these thresholds moved.** The turnover ceiling and the DeepTech category are recent changes to the recognition framework; older guides still say ₹100 crore and 10 years flat ([Treelife — India's revised startup recognition framework](https://treelife.in/startups/indias-revised-startup-recognition-framework-2026/); [Taxmann — DPIIT recognition FAQs](https://www.taxmann.com/post/blog/faqs-dpiit-recognition-for-start-ups/)). Confirm the current text on the portal before writing anything into an application.

Two practical notes. **The Ministry charges no fee** and has not authorised third-party agents — the portal says so explicitly, and every "DPIIT registration service" charging ₹5,000 is selling you form-filling. And the innovation write-up is the part that gets applications rejected: describe what DZZLO does that the dealer's current paper-and-WhatsApp process cannot, in the language of process improvement, not in the language of a pitch deck.

What recognition itself unlocks, before any application: self-certification under specified labour and environmental laws, IPR fast-tracking and fee rebates, relaxations in public procurement (§5.7), a faster winding-up route, and eligibility to apply for the tax benefits below. **VERIFY LIVE** each of these on the portal — the benefit list is revised more often than founders expect.

### 5.3 §80-IAC — the tax holiday, and why you should apply before you need it

**What it is:** a 100% deduction of profits and gains from an eligible business, for **any three consecutive assessment years out of the first ten** since incorporation ([Startup India — 80-IAC](https://www.startupindia.gov.in/content/sih/en/form80iac.html)).

**Who qualifies** — the conditions are narrower than DPIIT recognition and this catches people out:

| Condition          | Requirement                                                                      | Note                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Entity             | **Private Limited Company or LLP only**                                          | Partnership firms and cooperatives get DPIIT recognition but not 80-IAC                                                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| Incorporation date | On or after 1 April 2016 and **before 1 April 2030**                             | Extended by five years in Finance Act 2025 ([CAclubindia](https://www.caclubindia.com/news/tax-benefits-extended-for-start-ups-new-timeline-for-section-80-iac-provisions-until-2030-24480.asp); [TaxGuru FAQs](https://taxguru.in/income-tax/extension-time-limit-u-s-80-iac-startups.html)) |
| Turnover           | Below **₹100 crore** in the relevant financial year                              | Note this is _not_ the ₹200 crore recognition threshold — a genuine trap                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| Approval           | A separate **Certificate of Eligibility from the Inter-Ministerial Board (IMB)** | DPIIT recognition alone is not enough                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| Not formed by      | Splitting up / reconstruction; restrictions on transferred plant and machinery   | Standard anti-abuse conditions                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                |

**All of the above: VERIFY LIVE with the CA**, and remember the renumbering warning at the top of this lesson.

**Why it matters to a pre-revenue company that will pay no tax for years.** Three arguments, in order of weight:

1. **The clock is running from incorporation, not from profitability.** The three years must be taken within the first ten. A company that becomes profitable in year seven still has room; one that ignores it until year nine does not.
2. **The IMB approval process is slow and rejection is common**, so the option is worth acquiring early. DPIIT has been revising the evaluation framework and publicising approvals in batches ([PIB — DPIIT clears 187 startups under the revised 80-IAC framework](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2128860)).
3. **It is a signalling asset.** IMB approval is a third-party validation that is genuinely hard to get, and it reads well to an OMC procurement officer and to a future investor.

**The house call for VSYST: apply for DPIIT recognition immediately; apply for 80-IAC in the same year you first show a profit forecast that is more than a hope, and no later than year five.** Log the decision and the date in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] so it does not quietly lapse.

### 5.5 Udyam / MSME — free, permanent, and mostly misunderstood

Udyam registration is the current MSME registration, done free on the government portal ([Udyam Registration](https://udyamregistration.gov.in/); [Ministry of MSME](https://msme.gov.in/)). VSYST is comfortably a **Micro** enterprise under the revised classification effective 1 April 2025 — micro is up to ₹2.5 crore investment in plant and machinery or equipment **and** up to ₹10 crore turnover; small is ₹25 crore / ₹100 crore; medium ₹125 crore / ₹500 crore ([Taxmann — revised MSME classification](https://www.taxmann.com/post/blog/revised-msme-classification)) — **VERIFY LIVE**, these were raised 2.5× and 2× respectively and older sources still quote the previous limits.

Three things it actually gets you, in order of value to VSYST:

**1. The delayed-payment protection, which is the real prize.** Under the MSMED Act 2006, a buyer must pay a registered micro or small supplier within the agreed period, capped at **45 days**, or **15 days where there is no written agreement** — and the clock runs from the day of acceptance or deemed acceptance of the goods or services, not from the invoice date. Beyond that, compound interest at **three times the RBI bank rate** is payable, and disputes go to the Micro and Small Enterprise Facilitation Council via the [MSME Samadhaan portal](https://samadhaan.msme.gov.in/) ([National Government Services Portal](https://services.india.gov.in/service/detail/msme-samadhaan-delayed-payment-monitoring-system)).

**2. The §43B(h) linkage, which is the enforcement mechanism.** Introduced by Finance Act 2023 and effective from AY 2024-25, this provision disallows the buyer's tax deduction for any sum payable to a **micro or small** enterprise that is not paid within the MSMED time limit. Critically — and unlike the rest of §43B — **there is no proviso saving the deduction if the buyer pays before filing the return**: breach the MSMED clock and the deduction moves to the year of actual payment ([IndiaFilings](https://www.indiafilings.com/learn/section-43bh-new-msme-45-days-payment-rule); [Busy — the 45-day rule explained](https://busy.in/tds/section-43bh-msme-payment-rule-and-45-day-limit-explained/)). **VERIFY LIVE** — this provision has been contentious, is subject to representations, and carries over into the 2025 Act under a different number.

Why a CEO cares, in two directions:

- **As a supplier:** VSYST's registered Udyam status gives every GST-registered dealer customer a _tax_ reason to pay on time, not merely a contractual one. That is a collections lever no dunning email can match, and it should appear on the invoice ([[06-money-rails-and-finance-operations|COO lesson 06]] §6). Note the limitation honestly: the protection covers micro and small _manufacturers and service providers_, and a supplier registered for trading activity is generally not a "supplier" for §15 purposes — **VERIFY LIVE with the CA** whether VSYST's Udyam classification qualifies for this benefit as a software service provider.
- **As a buyer:** VSYST's own contractors and vendors may be registered micro or small enterprises, and paying them late now costs the company a tax deduction as well as goodwill. Add "Udyam number and classification" to the vendor register ([[14-vendors-procurement-and-cost-control|COO lesson 14]]).

**3. Scheme and lending eligibility.** Udyam registration is a precondition for CGTMSE cover, most state incentives, priority-sector lending treatment and public-procurement policy benefits. It costs an hour.

**The honest note for VSYST: neither of these helps today.** A guarantee scheme removes the lender's risk, not the lender's underwriting. A pre-revenue company with no cash flows will still be declined, because the bank is assessing repayment capacity and there is none. **The trigger that makes CGSS real is roughly twelve months of demonstrable, recurring collections** — at which point a working-capital line guaranteed under CGSS becomes a genuinely cheap way to fund growth without dilution. Note the trigger, put it in [[C13-runway-burn-and-scenario-planner|C13]], and stop thinking about it until then. SIDBI is worth a similar note: it operates direct lending schemes for small enterprises and administers the ₹10,000-crore **Fund of Funds for Startups**, but FFS invests in SEBI-registered AIFs rather than in startups directly — you meet it as a limited partner behind a VC, not as an applicant ([SIDBI](https://www.sidbi.in/)) — **VERIFY LIVE**.

### 5.7 Government procurement — a revenue path, not a grant

DPIIT-recognised startups get real relaxations in public procurement under the General Financial Rules 2017: **Rule 170(i)** relaxes the Earnest Money Deposit requirement, and **Rule 173(i)** relaxes prior experience and prior turnover criteria for goods, with parallel relaxations in the consultancy and works procurement manuals ([Startup India — public procurement](https://www.startupindia.gov.in/content/sih/en/public_procurement.html); [Invest India — easing public procurement for startups](https://www.investindia.gov.in/team-india-blogs/easing-public-procurement-startups)). **GeM Startup Runway** puts a Startup India badge on the listing and opens access to government buyers on the [Government e-Marketplace](https://gem.gov.in/). **VERIFY LIVE** the current rule numbers and the exact scope with a CS — GFR provisions are amended by office memorandum.

Why this matters for a fuel-distribution OMS specifically: the buyers in this ecosystem include state transport undertakings, PSU depots and government fleet operators, and the "no prior turnover, no prior experience" relaxation is precisely the barrier that would otherwise disqualify a three-person company from every tender it looked at.

Why it is dangerous: **government revenue arrives late.** Tender cycles are long, payment terms are longer, and a company with no working capital can win a tender and be killed by it. The rule for VSYST is written in [[14-partnerships-ecosystem-and-founder-relationships|lesson 14]] and repeated here as a capital rule: **do not chase a public-procurement deal whose payment terms you could not survive at your bear-case runway.**

## 6. Revenue is the cheapest capital

Nothing in §5 is as valuable as a dealer paying you. This section is short because the point is simple and because [[07-customers-markets-and-founder-led-sales|lesson 07]] owns the selling. What belongs here is the **capital** framing: two revenue decisions that are really financing decisions, and the CEO owns both.

### 6.1 Annual prepay is a financing decision wearing a pricing costume

A dealer who pays ₹21,588 for a year up front has done something no bank would do: given a pre-revenue company twelve months of cash at **zero interest and zero dilution**, secured on nothing but your promise to keep the software running. The [[finance/07-phase-7-bootstrapping-runway-burn|Finance course]] is right to call this DZZLO's cash superpower.

The CEO decisions inside it:

| Decision                                      | The trade                                                                                                                                          | House call for VSYST                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| --------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **How much discount to give for annual**      | Every point of discount is interest you are paying for the cash                                                                                    | Two months free on twelve (≈17%) is the common Indian SaaS shape. **VERIFY LIVE** against what the first cohort will actually accept. Never exceed the value of the runway it buys                                                                     |
| **Whether to push annual at all in year one** | Annual prepay maximises cash but reduces your ability to raise the price at renewal, and locks in a price you set before you understood the market | **Push annual hard for the founding cohort anyway.** The runway is worth more than the pricing option, and [[06-strategy-ii-moats-positioning-and-the-business-model\|lesson 06]] §7.5 already says you will price the first cohort too low regardless |
| **What to do with the cash**                  | It looks like profit and is not — it is a twelve-month obligation                                                                                  | Treat it as unearned revenue in the books, and as **restricted** in your own head. The tax-reserve sweep and a separate runway account are the mechanism ([[06-money-rails-and-finance-operations\|COO lesson 06]] §2)                                 |
| **Whether to offer multi-year**               | Two or three years of cash now, at the cost of a price locked for that long                                                                        | **No, not yet.** You do not know your price. Revisit after the first renewal cohort                                                                                                                                                                    |

The arithmetic to have in your head, because it reframes the sales target completely: **at the illustrative bear-case burn, 20 dealers on annual Growth plans is roughly ₹4.3 lakh of cash in the door and about three additional months of company.** Three months bought by twenty conversations. There is no scheme in §5 with a return like that.

### 6.2 Advances, pilots and services income

Three more revenue-as-capital moves, with the honest caveat on each:

- **Paid pilots.** A pilot that costs the dealer something is worth an order of magnitude more than a free one as a signal, and it is also cash. [[07-customers-markets-and-founder-led-sales|Lesson 07]] §7.4 owns the conversion mechanics. The capital point: **price the pilot to be paid, even if the amount is small**, because an unpaid pilot is a cost centre wearing a customer's clothes.
- **Implementation or data-migration fees.** A one-time onboarding fee is legitimate, is cash up front, and filters out dealers who will never adopt. Keep it small enough not to become the reason for a "no".
- **Services income.** Taking a consulting or custom-build project to fund the product is the classic Indian bootstrapping path — it is what funded Zoho's first decade. It is also the single most common way a product company quietly becomes a services company. **The house rule: any services work must have a written end date, a named founder-hour budget, and a stated reason it is being taken. Log it in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]].** If a second project is taken before the first is finished, that is a strategy change and it should be treated as one.

## 7. Debt and revenue-based finance in India

Debt is capital you repay in rupees rather than in ownership. That makes it cheaper than equity in every scenario where the company survives — and lethal in the scenarios where it does not. The Indian market has grown a real venture-debt ecosystem, and almost none of it is available to a pre-revenue three-person company. Knowing why is what makes this section useful.

### 7.1 The debt ladder, and where VSYST sits on it

| Instrument                            | Who provides it                       | What it needs                                               | Typical shape                                                           | Available to VSYST today?                                                                                                                         |

| **CGSS-backed term loan**             | Banks, NBFCs, venture debt funds      | DPIIT recognition + a lender willing to underwrite          | Guaranteed to 85% up to ₹10 crore                                       | **No, not yet.** §5.6                                                                                                                             |
| **Venture debt**                      | Alteria, Trifecta, Stride and others  | **Almost always a recent institutional equity round**       | 24–36 months, interest plus a small equity kicker                       | **No.** Venture debt lends against an investor's conviction, not yours                                                                            |
| **Revenue-based financing (RBF)**     | GetVantage, Velocity, Klub and others | Predictable recurring revenue, usually 6–12 months of it    | Advance repaid as a % of monthly revenue, flat fee rather than interest | **No.** There is no revenue to advance against                                                                                                    |
| **Invoice discounting / TReDS**       | Banks, TReDS platforms                | Receivables from creditworthy buyers                        | Discount on invoice value                                               | Later, if enterprise or PSU receivables appear                                                                                                    |

Sources: [EquityList — venture debt funds in India](https://www.equitylist.co/blog-post/venture-debt-funds-india); [Recur Club — venture debt funds guide](https://www.recurclub.com/blog/venture-debt-funds-in-india). India's venture-debt market has been estimated at roughly ₹10,000 crore a year and projected to grow substantially — **VERIFY LIVE**, market-size numbers in this space are vendor-published and vary widely. **Also VERIFY LIVE which RBF providers are still operating and on what terms**: the segment consolidated sharply after 2023 and several names that appear in every listicle have pivoted, merged or stopped originating.

### 7.2 The three rules that keep debt from killing you

1. **Debt is repaid on a schedule that does not care about your quarter.** Equity is patient by construction; debt is not. The test before signing anything: _can we service this in the bear case, not the base case?_ If the answer requires the base case, do not sign.
2. **Venture debt is a second cheque, not a first one.** Its actual underwriting is "this company just raised from an investor I respect, and that investor will likely fund it again". A company without that signal is not being conservatively lent to; it is not being lent to at all.
3. **RBF is expensive money priced to look cheap.** A "flat fee of 12%" repaid over six months is not a 12% annual cost — it is roughly double that annualised. RBF makes sense for a business with predictable revenue and a short, provable payback on the spend it funds (performance marketing for a D2C brand is the archetype). **It does not fit a sales-assisted vertical SaaS company with a multi-month sales cycle**, and it is not a substitute for runway.

The honest summary for VSYST: **debt is not currently an option, and that is a symptom rather than a problem.** Every one of these lenders is asking the same question in a different accent — _does money come in reliably?_ — and the answer is not yet. The work that makes debt available is the same work that makes debt unnecessary. Note it, set the trigger at twelve months of recurring collections, and move on.

## 8. When to raise equity — and when not to

Everything up to here has been capital that does not cost you the company. This section is where the lesson turns, and it turns on a question most founders never ask properly.

The question is not _"should we raise?"_ — that is a mood, and it will be answered by whichever mood you are in on the day. The question is: **what does this money buy that no other money buys, and is that thing worth what it costs?** Answer that in writing and the decision makes itself. Skip it and you will raise because someone flattered you, or refuse because refusing feels principled, and neither of those is a decision.

### 8.1 What you are actually selling

Be precise about the transaction, because the language around it is designed to be vague.

You are not borrowing. You are **selling a permanent share of every rupee this company ever earns or is ever sold for**, and you are simultaneously selling a bundle of rights over how it is run — who sits on the board, what you may do without permission, what you must report, and when someone else's clock starts running.

That gives you three prices, not one:

| The price             | What it is                                                                                                                    | When you feel it                                                                |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **The equity price**  | The percentage of the company that stops being yours, forever, compounded through every future round                          | At the exit, or at every dividend that never gets declared                      |
| **The control price** | Board seats, reserved matters, veto rights, information rights — §12                                                          | The first time you want to do something obvious and cannot                      |
| **The clock price**   | A fund has a life, usually ten years, and it must return capital. Your company acquires an expiry date it did not have before | In year four, when "we're profitable and growing 40%" stops being a good answer |

A founder who has only priced the first one has not read the term sheet. A family-founded company that has only priced the first one has not had the conversation it needs to have at home.

### 8.2 The honest triggers

There are only five reasons to sell equity that survive contact with a cold morning. Each has a test you can actually run.

| Honest trigger                                                  | The test that proves it                                                                                                                                                | What the money buys                                                     | True at VSYST today?                                                                                                                                                               |
| --------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **A repeatable acquisition motion that is capital-constrained** | You can state CAC and payback period **from your own closed deals**, not from a benchmark, and every rupee in produces more than a rupee back inside a knowable window | Speed on a machine that already works                                   | **No.** Zero paying tenants means zero CAC data. There is no machine yet                                                                                                           |
| **A window that is genuinely closing**                          | You can name the date it shuts and name who else is running at it                                                                                                      | The right to arrive first at something that will not be available later | **Not yet, and be suspicious of this one** — founders imagine windows. | **A capability you cannot build from cash flow in time**        | The capability has a hard external gate — a certification with a capital requirement, a licence with a net-worth test — and the gate has a date                        | Access to a market that is legally closed to you today                  | **Not yet.** [[certification-and-standards-roadmap\|ISO 27001]] and any GSP-style licensing sit downstream of revenue. | **Working capital the business model structurally requires**    | The model consumes cash before it produces it as a matter of arithmetic, not of impatience — inventory, lending, float                                                 | The ability to operate at all                                           | **No, and the opposite is true.** A subscription with annual prepay is cash-positive by construction (§6)                                                                          |

Notice what all five have in common: **each one describes something that money removes as a constraint.** If money is not the binding constraint, money will not help — it will just be more expensive to be wrong.

### 8.4 The case against raising for a vertical Indian SaaS with a modest ticket

This is the argument that matters most for VSYST, and it is arithmetic rather than philosophy.

Christoph Janz's framework is the cleanest way to see it. To build a ₹100-crore-scale software business you need one of five customer shapes: about 1,000 customers paying "elephant" prices, 10,000 paying "deer" prices, 100,000 "rabbits", a million "mice", or ten million "flies" — and **each animal implies a completely different company**: a different sales motion, a different team, a different capital requirement and a different set of ways to die ([Christoph Janz — Five ways to build a \$100 million business](https://medium.com/point-nine-news/five-ways-to-build-a-100-million-business-82ac6ea8ffd9), 2014; [the five-years-later update](https://medium.com/point-nine-news/five-years-later-five-ways-to-build-a-100-million-saas-business-49ebca14825c), 2019).

Put DZZLO's numbers into it. At an illustrative ₹1,799 per GSTIN per month, one tenant is roughly ₹21,600 a year — a **rabbit**, and a small one. Now the denominator. India has on the order of **90,000–100,000 petrol retail outlets** ([PPAC — retail outlets](https://ppac.gov.in/infrastructure/retail-outlets); **VERIFY LIVE**, this is the number the whole argument turns on and PPAC updates it). Add lubricant distributors and bulk-diesel operators and be generous: call the addressable universe 150,000 paying GSTINs, which is almost certainly too kind because a large share of outlets are company-operated, single-site, or will never buy software from anyone.

```
  Addressable GSTINs (generous)                 150,000
  Revenue per GSTIN per year, ex-GST            ₹21,588
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  100% of India, at today's price       ≈ ₹324 crore ARR
  A realistic strong outcome, 10%       ≈ ₹32 crore ARR
  A very good outcome, 5%               ≈ ₹16 crore ARR
```

Hold that against what an institutional investor needs. A micro-VC fund in India typically runs a corpus under about ₹350 crore (\$40M), leads with cheques of roughly ₹4–7 crore and takes 8–12% ([Blume — India's micro-VC landscape: who's writing the first cheque in 2026](https://blume.vc/commentaries/indias-micro-vc-landscape-whos-writing-the-first-cheque-in-2026), 2026; **VERIFY LIVE**, fund sizes and cheque bands move every vintage). For one position to return a fund of that size, a ~10% stake has to be worth the whole corpus at exit — which at the mid-single-digit revenue multiples Indian vertical SaaS actually transacts at implies an ARR **larger than the entire Indian fuel-retail TAM at today's price.**

That is the whole argument, and it deserves a sentence of its own:

> **If your honest TAM is smaller than the outcome your investor needs, you and your investor are not playing the same game — and the divergence does not show up at signing. It shows up in year three, when you want to be a profitable ₹15-crore-ARR company and they need you to be something else.**

Three more reasons the fit is poor at this specific stage, none of them about ambition:

1. **You would be selling the cheapest equity you will ever sell.** Pre-revenue, pre-price, pre-motion, your valuation is a story. Every month of paying tenants makes the same rupee cost you less ownership. Dilution taken before evidence is the most expensive dilution there is.
2. **A sales-assisted district motion does not accelerate with money the way a self-serve product does.** You cannot buy trust in a Raipur pump office. You can buy more people who have to earn it, which is slower per rupee than it looks and, before the motion is documented, actively destructive.
3. **A family-founder company should settle its own contracts before adding anyone else's.** Vesting, roles, exit terms, what happens if a director wants out — [[C02-founders-agreement-and-vesting-checklist|C02]] and [[03-the-ceo-core-value-and-the-founder-contracts|lesson 03]]. Bringing an investor's SHA into an unresolved founder situation does not resolve it; it freezes it and gives a stranger a vote in it.

### 8.5 The case for raising anyway — argued properly

The above is only half the argument, and a CEO who can only argue one half is not thinking. Here is the strongest honest case for the other side.

**The TAM number is probably wrong on the low side, because subscription is not the ceiling.** The a16z thesis on vertical software is that adding financial products — payments, lending, cards, insurance — to a vertical SaaS platform raises revenue per customer by roughly **2–5×**, and that at the mature end more than half of a vertical platform's revenue comes from financial products rather than software ([a16z — Fintech Scales Vertical SaaS](https://a16z.com/fintech-scales-vertical-saas/); [Inventure — Vertical fintech is the new vertical SaaS](https://blog.inventure.vc/vertical-fintech-is-the-new-vertical-saas-6b0c90992bf0)). DZZLO already sits on the transaction: rate setting, dispatch, three-tier invoicing, vouchers, reconciliation — and there is a payment gateway relationship in progress. **If the honest ARPA is ₹1,799 of software plus a take-rate on fuel payments and a share of dealer working-capital financing, the denominator argument in §8.4 changes shape entirely** — and that is a business a fund can size.

That is a real argument. It is also a **strategy decision, not a fundraising decision**, and the order matters enormously:

> You either change the business to fit the capital, or you fit the capital to the business. Both are legitimate. What is not legitimate is letting a term sheet make the choice for you six weeks before a close, because that is how founders end up running a company they did not choose.

The other three arguments for, stated fairly:

- **The land-grab risk is real in a trust market.** If a funded competitor signs an OMC-level or association-level distribution arrangement covering your districts, the patient strategy loses and you find out late. This is the one window in §8.2 that could actually close.
- **A good investor's introductions are not nothing.** In an ecosystem where an IOCL conversation moves on who will make the call, a well-connected seed investor is a genuine asset — but only a specific one, named in advance. "The network" as a generic benefit is marketing copy.
- **Three people cannot run five experiments.** Capital buys parallelism. That matters _after_ you know which experiments are worth running, and not before.

And one argument you should discount hard in India at this stage: **founder secondary.** Taking money off the table for the family is rare and usually unavailable at seed. Do not plan around it. **VERIFY LIVE** with the lawyer if it is ever offered, and read §13 before you take it.

### 8.6 The house call — and the trigger that would change it

**The house call: VSYST does not raise equity in the next twelve months.** Not as a principle, not as pride, and not permanently — as a dated decision with stated reasons, which is the only kind worth making. The reasons, in order: there is no CAC to fund; the non-dilutive stack in §5 is unclaimed and returns more per founder-hour than any investor meeting will; annual prepay in §6 is free money that is available now; and the founder contracts are not finished.

Write the trigger down, verbatim, in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]], dated, with all three directors' names on it. Something in this shape — set your own numbers, but set them **now**, while nobody is being flattered:

```
CAPITAL DECISION — reviewed monthly, changed only in writing

We will open a priced equity round when ALL FOUR are true
for three consecutive months:

  (a) >= 25 paying GSTINs live, collecting on time
  (b) >= 8 of the last 12 signings came from OUTSIDE the family
      and the domain director's own network      <- the honesty test
  (c) logo churn below the agreed line
  (d) we can state CAC and payback in months from our own data

Until then, the answer to every inbound investor is:
  "Not yet — may I add you to our monthly update?"

SEPARATE DEFENSIVE TRIGGER — overrides the above:
  If a funded competitor signs an OMC-level or association-level
  distribution arrangement covering our districts, we reopen this
  decision within 30 days regardless of (a)-(d).
```

Condition (b) is the one that will be uncomfortable, and it is the most important line in the box. A family-founded company in its home district can manufacture the appearance of product–market fit out of goodwill, and goodwill does not renew at scale. [[07-customers-markets-and-founder-led-sales|Lesson 07]] §9.1 already makes you count cold closes separately. This is that number, doing a second job.

**Meanwhile: take the meetings and refuse the money.** The best-run raises are the ones where the investor has been reading your monthly update for eighteen months before you ask. Start the [[C23-stakeholder-and-investor-update|C23]] list now, with five names on it, and send it on the same date every month whether or not anything good happened. It costs you an hour, it builds the only thing that actually compresses a raise — a relationship with someone who has watched you hit what you said you would hit — and it means that when a trigger does fire, you are running a process (§11) rather than starting one.

## 9. The Indian early-stage funding landscape in 2026

You have decided not to raise for twelve months. That is exactly the right moment to learn this map, for the same reason you learn a route before you drive it at night: **the cost of ignorance is highest when you are in a hurry.** A founder who first opens this map at nine months of runway will take the first cheque that appears. A founder who has known the map for a year will know within one email which three people to write to, what each of them actually buys, and which ones are structurally unable to fund a company like VSYST no matter how good the meeting goes.

Read this section as reconnaissance, not as a to-do list.

### 9.1 The layers, and what each one is actually for

The Indian first-cheque market has five distinct layers, and they are not a ladder you climb — they are different animals that eat different food. Confusing them is the most common early-stage mistake, because a pitch tuned for a micro-VC is the wrong pitch for an angel and an insult to a family office.

| Layer                                     | Who they are                                                               | What they are actually buying                               | What they need from you                                                      | Decision speed                                    |
| ----------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| **Individual angels**                     | Operators, founders who exited, senior professionals, local businesspeople | A person they believe in, and optionality on a big outcome  | Conviction in you and a story they can retell                                | Days to weeks — one person decides                |
| **Angel networks / syndicates**           | A platform that aggregates many individual angels into one round           | The same thing, plus the comfort of a lead they trust       | A lead investor, a deck, a data room, and patience for a screening committee | Weeks to months — committees and rolling closes   |
| **Accelerators**                          | A programme: standard cheque, standard terms, cohort, demo day             | Portfolio breadth and the option on 1-in-30                 | Coachability, a team, and willingness to run their process                   | Fixed intake cycles — you apply on their calendar |
| **Micro-VCs / seed funds**                | A small institutional fund, professional GPs, LP money, a fund life        | A position that could return their fund (§8.4's arithmetic) | Evidence: a market, a motion, and numbers that extrapolate                   | 4–10 weeks, partner meeting, IC                   |
| **Family offices / strategic corporates** | Business families and corporate venture arms deploying own capital         | Sometimes a return; often adjacency to their own business   | A relationship, and a reason your business touches theirs                    | Wildly variable — weeks to never                  |

The single most useful thing on that table is the last column, and the reason is [[C13-runway-burn-and-scenario-planner|C13]]: **decision speed is a runway input.** A raise built on institutional money needs four to six months of runway to survive its own process. A raise built on three angels who already know you can close in six weeks. When you are at nine months, that difference is the whole decision.

2. **Never pay a success fee to raise a seed round.** If a platform, consultant or "fundraising partner" asks for a retainer plus a percentage of the money raised, you are talking to a broker. Real investors do not need a broker to find you, and a broker on your cap table is a signal that reads badly to everyone who comes later. **The exception is a lawyer or CA billing hourly for documentation — that is a professional fee, and it is money well spent.**

### 9.3 Micro-VCs and seed funds in SMB / vertical SaaS

This is the layer that people mean when they say "raising a seed round". A micro-VC in India typically runs a corpus in the low hundreds of crores, writes a first cheque somewhere between ₹50 lakh and ₹5 crore, leads the round and sets the terms ([Blume — India's micro-VC landscape](https://blume.vc/commentaries/indias-micro-vc-landscape-whos-writing-the-first-cheque-in-2026), 2026; [IncorpX — pre-seed and seed funding 2026](https://www.incorpx.io/blog/pre-seed-seed-funding-2026-raise-first-round)). **VERIFY LIVE**: fund sizes, cheque bands and even which funds are still deploying change every vintage, and a fund that has finished deploying will still take your meeting.

Rather than list names that will be stale before you need them, learn the **four questions that tell you whether a fund can fund you** — ask these in the first ten minutes of any meeting, politely, and you will save yourself two months:

| Question to ask                                                                           | Why it matters                                                                                                                                                       | What a bad answer sounds like                                                                             |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **"Which fund are you deploying, what vintage, and how many cheques are left in it?"**    | A fund at the end of its deployment period cannot write you a cheque no matter how much the partner likes you                                                        | Vagueness. A GP who is deploying knows this number exactly                                                |
| **"What is your typical first cheque and target ownership?"**                             | This is §8.4's arithmetic. If they need 15% and you need ₹1 crore, the implied valuation may be one neither of you wants                                             | "It depends entirely on the company." Sometimes true; often a way of not telling you                      |
| **"What's the smallest outcome that works for this fund?"**                               | The single most revealing question in venture, and almost nobody asks it. It surfaces the game mismatch in §8.4 before you spend four months on it                   | Discomfort. Note it and move on                                                                           |
| **"Name two portfolio companies selling to Indian SMBs at a sub-₹50,000 annual ticket."** | Selling ₹1,799/month software to petrol pumps in Chhattisgarh is a specific sport. A fund that has never watched it up close will misread every number you show them | Zero examples, followed by enthusiasm. Enthusiasm without pattern-matching becomes impatience in year two |

What the SMB / vertical-SaaS-literate part of the Indian market is looking for, specifically, and it is not what a consumer investor looks for:

- **Payback period, not growth rate.** For a sales-assisted SMB motion, CAC payback under 12 months is the number that opens doors; over 24 and you are asking them to fund a treadmill ([[C12-unit-economics-and-business-model-calculator|C12]] owns the calculation).
- **Net revenue retention.** Do dealers who stay expand? A vertical platform that only ever collects its first subscription is a much worse business than one whose tenants add GSTINs, users and modules — the §8.5 fintech argument lives here.
- **Evidence of a motion, not evidence of demand.** Twenty dealers who signed because the domain director knows them proves demand and proves nothing about the motion. Eight cold closes prove the motion. This is exactly what §8.6's condition (b) is counting.
- **Density.** Saturating one district is a far stronger signal than scattered logos across five states, because it is the only evidence that the strategy in [[05-strategy-i-diagnosis-and-the-strategy-kernel|lesson 05]] is repeatable.

| **Axilor, T-Hub, NASSCOM 10,000 Startups, IIT/IIM incubators**     | Institutional incubators, sometimes with a grant rather than equity, often gateways to state schemes                                 | Frequently the _cheapest_ option because some are grant-based rather than dilutive — and often the route into the state schemes already covered in §5. **Check whether an incubator relationship is a precondition for any Chhattisgarh scheme before you conclude they are irrelevant**                                                                                                   |

The one test that decides an accelerator application, and it is not the cheque:

> **Would you take this programme if the cheque were zero?** If the honest answer is yes — because of the cohort, a specific mentor, a specific customer introduction, or the forcing function of a demo day — apply. If the honest answer is no and you are applying for the money, you are selling equity at the worst price available for the smallest amount of money available, and §5's non-dilutive stack pays better per founder-hour.

### 9.5 Family offices and strategic capital

Indian business families deploying their own capital are a large and quiet part of the early-stage market, and they behave nothing like funds.

**What is different:** no fund life, therefore no exit clock (§8.1's third price mostly disappears). Decisions are made by one or two people, sometimes fast, sometimes never. They frequently prefer businesses they understand — and a business family with fuel, logistics, transport or infrastructure interests understands DZZLO's customer instantly, with no education required. Many are more comfortable with profitability than with growth-at-any-cost, which aligns with §8.4's honest outcome.

**What is worse:** governance is often informal and personal; terms can be idiosyncratic (a family office asking for a board seat, a personal guarantee or a revenue share is not unheard of); "no" is often communicated by silence; and there is a real risk of an investor whose actual interest is a commercial relationship with your company rather than a return on your equity.

**The hard rule, and it applies with particular force in fuel:** if a prospective investor is also a potential customer, supplier, distributor or channel partner, **the investment and the commercial relationship must be negotiated separately, priced separately and documented separately.** A dealer group that both owns 8% of you and buys your software has a permanent conflict of interest, and it will surface in your pricing conversation with every other dealer in the district. [[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties|Lesson 09]] §7.5 makes the same call about advisors; it applies double to money.

The strategic corporate case — an oil marketing company, a large distributor group, a fintech — is the same argument at higher stakes. Strategic money buys distribution and buys a ceiling at the same time: once one OMC owns a piece of you, the others read you as theirs. That is a strategy decision for [[14-partnerships-ecosystem-and-founder-relationships|lesson 14]] and [[C26-partnership-evaluation-and-mou-checklist|C26]], not a capital decision, and it must be made in that order.

## 10. Instruments — what you actually sign, and the Indian catch in each

An instrument is the legal shape money takes on the way into your company. Founders treat it as paperwork and negotiate the valuation instead. That is backwards. **The instrument decides who bears which risk, what happens in the bad outcome, what you must file and when, and how much tax and compliance the transaction drags behind it** — and in India it also decides whether the transaction is legal at all, because some instruments are simply not available to some companies.

This section is the most statute-heavy in the lesson. Read it once for the shape, and then never sign anything on the basis of it. **Every rule below is VERIFY LIVE with a Company Secretary and a Chartered Accountant, and where a non-resident is involved, with a lawyer who does FEMA work.** The renumbering warning at the top of this lesson applies with full force here.

### 10.1 The four instruments that matter at seed in India

| #   | Instrument                          | One-line shape                                                                                                                                                                           |
| --- | ----------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | **Priced equity — ordinary shares** | You agree a valuation now and sell ordinary equity shares at that price                                                                                                                  |
| 2   | **Priced equity — CCPS**            | You agree a valuation now and sell compulsorily convertible preference shares that carry protections and convert to equity later. **This is what an Indian seed round almost always is** |
| 3   | **Convertible note**                | Money now, shares later at a price set by a future round — a borrowing that converts. Legally restricted in India                                                                        |
| 4   | **iSAFE**                           | India's adaptation of the US SAFE, implemented as CCPS with deferred pricing                                                                                                             |

### 10.2 Priced equity, and why almost nobody buys ordinary shares

An investor who buys **ordinary equity shares** owns exactly what you own — the same rights, the same downside, the same everything. It is the simplest possible cap table and the one no institutional investor accepts, for a reason that is easy to state: in a modest exit, ordinary shares mean the investor's ₹3 crore and your ₹0 of cash contribution rank identically, and they are not being paid to take that risk.

So Indian seed rounds are done in **CCPS — compulsorily convertible preference shares**. The structure carries three things the investor wants, all of which sit inside the preference share rather than in a side letter:

- **A liquidation preference** — a right to get their money back first in a sale or winding up (§12.1).
- **An anti-dilution adjustment** — a conversion ratio that moves if you later sell shares cheaper (§12.2).
- **Conversion to equity that is compulsory**, which is what keeps the instrument on the right side of Indian foreign-exchange law.

That last point is the one Indian founders under-appreciate, and it is worth stating plainly: **under India's foreign-exchange framework, a compulsorily convertible instrument is treated as equity (a capital instrument), while an optionally convertible or redeemable one is treated as debt** and falls into the external-commercial-borrowing regime with an entirely different and far heavier rulebook. "Compulsorily" is not a stylistic flourish. It is the word that makes the instrument work. **VERIFY LIVE** with a FEMA lawyer before anyone gets creative about optionality.

There is also a benefit to _you_ in CCPS that founders routinely forget: because the preference shares carry the economic protections, **the ordinary equity share stays cheap**. That keeps your ESOP strike price low — which is the difference between an option grant that motivates a hire and one that is a joke ([[C20-compensation-bands-and-esop-design|C20]], [[10-building-the-team-hiring-equity-and-firing|lesson 10]]).

Two Indian details on CCPS that a first-time founder will be surprised by:

| Detail                                                                            | What it means                                                                                                                                                                                                              | What to do                                                                                                                                                                                           |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Preference shares carry a dividend right**                                      | If the coupon is cumulative and unpaid, it accrues as an obligation against a company that has no cash                                                                                                                     | Indian seed CCPS are conventionally issued with a **nominal, non-cumulative** dividend — a fraction of a percent — precisely to neutralise this. Check the number in the term sheet. **VERIFY LIVE** |
| **Preference shareholders can acquire full voting rights if dividends go unpaid** | Under the Companies Act, preference shareholders vote only on matters affecting their own rights — **but if the preference dividend is in arrears for a specified period, they acquire voting rights on every resolution** | Ask your CS to confirm the current arrears period and drafting consequence before signing. This clause has surprised founders who assumed CCPS were permanently non-voting. **VERIFY LIVE**          |

**The company-law machinery a priced round drags with it** (a private limited company issuing shares to identified persons, i.e. a preferential allotment by private placement):

1. **Board resolution** approving the offer, and a **special resolution** of members — a 75% majority ([Section 62(1)(c), Companies Act 2013, read with Rule 13 of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014](https://ca2013.com/rule-13-companies-share-capital-and-debentures-rules-2014/)).
2. **A valuation report from a registered valuer** (IBBI-registered). **The issue price cannot be lower than the price the valuer determines** ([EquityList — preferential allotment: procedure, pricing and rules](https://www.equitylist.co/blog-post/preferential-allotment); [TaxGuru — CCPS issuance through private placement, step by step](https://taxguru.in/company-law/ccps-issuance-private-placement-step-by-step-process.html)).
3. **A private placement offer letter in Form PAS-4** to named, identified persons — with a cap of **200 persons in a financial year per kind of security**, excluding qualified institutional buyers and employees under an ESOP.
4. **Money through banking channels into a separate bank account only.** No cash, ever, and the account cannot be used for anything else until allotment.
5. **Allotment within 60 days** of receiving the money, or refund it (with interest, past a further grace period).
6. **Filings: PAS-3** for the return of allotment, and **MGT-14** for the special resolution, both to the ROC on time.
7. **Share certificates within the statutory window** after allotment, stamped at the applicable state rate, and the **register of members** updated.

Every one of those steps has a deadline and a penalty, and a botched allotment is the single most common thing that turns up in diligence four years later and delays a Series A. **The whole sequence belongs to your CS, on a checklist, before the money moves — not after.** [[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties|Lesson 09]] and [[C18-director-duties-and-governance-checklist|C18]] own the governance side; [[05-legal-and-governance-foundation|COO 05]] and [[T16-compliance-calendar|T16]] own the calendar.

### 10.3 Convertible notes — and the Indian constraint nobody mentions until late

A convertible note is money now, equity later. You do not agree a valuation today; you agree that the money converts at the next priced round, usually with a **discount** (say 20% off the round price) and often a **valuation cap** (a ceiling on the price at which it converts, so early money is rewarded if the next round is large). If no round happens, it repays, or converts at a floor. In the US it is the default first-cheque instrument and it is genuinely simple.

**In India it is not simple, and for most companies it is not available.** Two separate bodies of law have to permit it, and both impose conditions:

| Requirement                                                              | Detail                                                                                               | Consequence of missing it                                                                                                                                                 |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **DPIIT recognition**                                                    | The carve-out that makes convertible notes legal exists **only for a recognised start-up**           | A company without DPIIT recognition cannot issue convertible notes at all. **This is §5.2's recognition doing a second job you had not counted**                          |
| **Minimum ₹25 lakh, in a single tranche, per investor**                  | Below that amount the carve-out does not apply                                                       | The money is treated as a **deposit** under the Companies (Acceptance of Deposits) Rules — a regime a private company is not equipped to comply with, with real penalties |
| **Convertible into equity, or repayable, within 10 years of issue**      | Extended from the original five years                                                                | Outside the window, the same deposit problem                                                                                                                              |
| **Sector on the 100% automatic FDI route** (for a non-resident investor) | Software and IT services are; check yours                                                            | Government approval route, i.e. months                                                                                                                                    |
| **Reporting**                                                            | Issue to a non-resident must be reported to the RBI within the prescribed window through the AD bank | An unreported issue is a FEMA contravention requiring compounding                                                                                                         |

Sources: [Invest India — convertible notes](https://www.investindia.gov.in/team-india-blogs/convertible-note-flexible-funding-options-startups); [IncorpX — convertible notes for Indian startups: FEMA rules](https://www.incorpx.io/blog/convertible-notes-startups-india-fema-rules); [Bhavya Sharma & Associates — convertible notes: legal, FEMA and compliance guide](https://www.bhavyasharmaandassociates.com/convertible-notes-indian-startups-fema-compliance-guide/); [Legal Suvidha — guide on convertible notes](https://legalsuvidha.com/blog/guide-on-convertible-notes). **VERIFY LIVE — every threshold above, including the ₹25 lakh floor and the 10-year window, with your CS and a FEMA lawyer.**

Read the first two rows together, because they combine into a sentence with a real consequence for VSYST:

> **A convertible note is only legal for a DPIIT-recognised start-up, and only for cheques of ₹25 lakh or more in a single tranche.** Which means: the small friendly angel cheque of ₹10 lakh — the most likely first outside money a Raipur company will ever be offered — **cannot** be taken as a convertible note. It has to be priced equity or CCPS, with a valuation report and the full §10.2 machinery, or it must not be taken at all.

That is exactly the kind of thing that is discovered at the wrong moment. **Get DPIIT recognition now** (§5.2, §14) — not because you plan to issue a note, but because it is the precondition for an instrument you might need in eighteen months, and recognition takes time you will not have then.

### 10.4 iSAFE — India's answer, and what it does and does not solve

The **iSAFE (India Simple Agreement for Future Equity)** was created by 100X.VC in 2019 as an Indian adaptation of Y Combinator's SAFE. The problem it solves is real: at pre-seed you cannot honestly price the company, and neither can the investor, so both sides want to defer the valuation without pretending the money is a loan ([100X.VC — investing through iSAFE notes](https://www.100x.vc/blog/investing-through-i-safe-notes-yagnesh-sanghrajka-100x.vc-startups-2019); [TaxGuru — iSAFE explained](https://taxguru.in/company-law/isafe-india-simple-agreement-future-equity.html)).

**How it differs from a US SAFE, and this is the whole point:** a US SAFE is a contract — nothing is issued when the money arrives. Indian law does not accommodate that cleanly, so **the iSAFE is implemented as CCPS that are allotted immediately on receipt of the money**, with the conversion ratio determined later at the next priced round. It is not debt, it does not accrue interest, and it converts compulsorily on a trigger event — a priced round, a liquidity event, or the outside date (commonly three years) — whichever comes first ([Mondaq — Decoding iSAFE](https://www.mondaq.com/india/securities/1330992/decoding-isafe--part-i); [Vinod Kothari Consultants — the iSAFE option: legality and taxation](https://vinodkothari.com/2024/03/the-isafe-option-to-start-up-funding-legality-and-taxation/); [TBA Law — decoding the iSAFE document](https://www.tbalaw.in/insights-decoding-isafe)).

**What it genuinely solves:** the valuation conversation, the length of the negotiation, and the legal bill. A standard-form document that both sides recognise is worth real money and real weeks.

**What it does not solve, and this is the part that surprises people:** because shares are actually allotted, **the iSAFE drags the entire §10.2 private-placement machinery with it** — board resolution, special resolution, valuer's report, PAS-4, separate bank account, PAS-3, MGT-14. "Simple" describes the _commercial_ negotiation, not the compliance load. Anyone who tells you an iSAFE is paperwork-free has not filed one.

Two more honest notes. First, **the outside conversion date is a real term** — a three-year clock that converts at a floor price if no round happens is a materially different deal from one that converts at the cap, and a company that never raises again will convert its iSAFE holders in at a price nobody modelled. Second, **iSAFE terms are not one document**; different investors use different versions with different caps, discounts, MFN clauses and pro-rata rights. Read the actual paper, not the concept.

### 10.6 Angel tax — §56(2)(viib) — where it actually stands

For nearly a decade, the single most feared line in Indian startup finance was **section 56(2)(viib)** of the Income-tax Act, 1961 — "angel tax". It taxed, as income **in the hands of the company**, any share premium received by an unlisted company above the fair market value of those shares. Its practical effect was that a startup could raise ₹1 crore at a valuation an investor was happy to pay, and then receive a demand from the tax department asserting that the valuation was too optimistic and the excess was taxable income. It caused enormous damage, most of it to companies that had done nothing wrong.

**It has been abolished.** The Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 omitted clause (viib), and the **Income-tax Act, 2025 — which takes effect from 1 April 2026 — contains no equivalent provision** ([Legal500 / Sarthak Advocates — abolition of angel tax in India](https://www.legal500.com/developments/thought-leadership/abolition-of-angel-tax-in-india-a-boost-for-the-startup-ecosystem/); [ClearTax — angel tax](https://cleartax.in/s/angel-tax); [IncorpX — angel tax abolished: startup funding, FY 2026-27](https://www.incorpx.io/blog/angel-tax-abolished-startup-funding-fy-2026-27)).

**For a share issue by VSYST in FY 2026-27, the position is therefore that there is no angel-tax charge on the premium.** That is genuinely good news and it removes the most common reason Indian founders used to under-price rounds.

Now the four qualifications, because "abolished" is being reported more simply than it deserves:

| Qualification                                                      | Why it matters                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **The abolition is prospective**                                   | Issues made in earlier years, and open assessments and demands for those years, continue under the old law. If VSYST has any historical share issue at a premium, **ask the CA whether it is exposed** — the answer is probably no for a newly incorporated company, but ask                                                                                                                                        |
| **Commentary differs on the exact first unaffected year**          | Sources variously describe the omission as effective from AY 2025-26 or from 1 April 2025. For a FY 2026-27 issue the answer is the same either way, but **VERIFY LIVE** the precise cut-off with your CA before relying on it for anything historical                                                                                                                                                              |
| **The section number is gone; the _valuation_ requirement is not** | §10.7. Angel tax died. Rule 11UA did not, and neither did any of the other reasons you need a valuer                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| **A different provision still taxes the _recipient_**              | The Act separately taxes a person who _receives_ property, including shares, for less than fair market value. That is the mirror image of angel tax, it applies to **the investor or the employee, not the company**, and it is very much alive. It is why founder share issues, ESOP grants and secondary sales all still need a defensible valuation. **VERIFY LIVE** the corresponding clause under the 2025 Act |

> **The one-line summary to carry: angel tax is gone, valuation discipline is not.** A founder who reads "angel tax abolished" and concludes "we don't need a valuation" has drawn precisely the wrong lesson, and will discover it at the ROC counter rather than at the tax office.

### 10.7 Rule 11UA — the valuation rulebook that outlived the tax

**Rule 11UA of the Income-tax Rules** is the machinery that defines fair market value for unquoted shares. CBDT substantially rewrote it by notification in September 2023: it widened the permitted valuation methods, introduced **a specific mechanism for valuing CCPS** (including an option to derive CCPS value from the fair market value of unquoted equity shares), and added a **10% safe-harbour tolerance** so that a small variance between the issue price and the computed fair value does not itself create a problem ([Dewan P N Chopra & Co — Rule 11UA: new methods for valuing CCPS and unquoted shares](https://www.dpncindia.com/rule-11ua-new-methods-for-valuing-ccps-and-unquoted-shares); [RSM India — CBDT amends Rule 11UA](https://www.rsm.global/india/insights/tax-insights/newsflash-cbdt-amends-rule-11ua-income-tax-rules-1962-respect-angel-tax); [TaxGuru — amendment in Rule 11UA](https://taxguru.in/income-tax/amendment-rule-11ua-valuation-shares-angel-tax.html)).

The practical points, all **VERIFY LIVE**:

- **Two workhorse methods:** Net Asset Value and Discounted Cash Flow. For a pre-revenue company, NAV produces a number close to nothing and DCF produces whatever the projections say — which is why the projections attached to a DCF report are a document you should read line by line before signing, not a formality your CA prepares alone.
- **A merchant banker's report has a shelf life.** The report is acceptable only if dated within a limited window before the issue — commonly stated as **90 days**. Get the timing right or you will pay for the valuation twice.
- **Who may value depends on the purpose, and the purposes do not share a rulebook.** A registered valuer for the Companies Act. A merchant banker or chartered accountant for tax and FEMA purposes, depending on the provision. **These are different appointments under different statutes and they can produce different numbers.** Ask your CA and CS, together, in one call, which reports the specific transaction needs — this is the single most common source of duplicated professional fees in a first round.
- **Rule 11UA still governs things you will definitely do**, angel tax or not: ESOP perquisite valuation at exercise, transfers of unquoted shares between shareholders, secondary sales, and any issue below fair value where the recipient is taxed.

### 10.8 If any investor is not resident in India — FEMA, pricing and FC-GPR

Everything above assumes rupees from residents. The moment an investor is a person resident outside India — including a non-resident Indian, a foreign fund, an overseas family office, or a foreign-incorporated entity — a second and entirely separate rulebook applies, administered by the RBI through your bank. **You cannot do this yourself. Budget for a FEMA-competent CA or lawyer from the first conversation, not from the closing.**

| Requirement                           | What it is                                                                                                                                                                                                                      | Deadline / consequence                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Sector and entry route**            | Software and IT services sit on the 100% automatic route, meaning no prior government approval. | **The land-border rule**              | An entity of, or with a beneficial owner in, a country sharing a land border with India requires **prior government approval** regardless of sector, and the beneficial-ownership test looks through structures                 | Applies to the investor's ultimate ownership, not just its registered address. **Ask every non-resident investor to confirm beneficial ownership in writing.** **VERIFY LIVE** with a FEMA lawyer                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| **Pricing guidelines**                | Shares issued to a non-resident must be at **not less than** fair value, computed under an internationally accepted pricing methodology and certified by a SEBI-registered Category-I merchant banker or a chartered accountant | Below fair value = a contravention needing compounding. Note the asymmetry: the floor protects the _outflow_ of value from India, so you may price high but not low                                                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| **Form FC-GPR**                       | The report of a fresh issue of capital instruments to a non-resident, filed on the RBI's **FIRMS** portal through your AD Category-I bank                                                                                       | **Within 30 days of allotment** — measured from allotment, **not** from when the money arrived ([EquityList — FC-GPR filing guide](https://www.equitylist.co/blog-post/fc-gpr-filing-india); [CAclubindia — FC-GPR on the FIRMS portal](https://www.caclubindia.com/articles/fcgpr-filing-on-rbi-firms-portal-fema-compliance-for-fdi-in-india-process-documents-penalties-53783.asp)) |

| **Form CN**                           | Reports issue or transfer of **convertible notes** involving a non-resident                                                                                                                                                     | Separate again; see §10.3                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| **FLA return**                        | An annual return on foreign liabilities and assets, filed once the company has any foreign investment on its books — **including in years when nothing happened**                                                               | Annual, due mid-July. | **Late Submission Fee / compounding** | A missed deadline is a technical contravention. It is fixable, through a prescribed late fee or a compounding application                                                                                                       | It is fixable and it is not free, and an uncompounded contravention will be found in diligence                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         |

**VERIFY LIVE — the whole table, including every form name and deadline**, with a FEMA practitioner. RBI's forms and portal workflows change, and a filing done on last year's understanding is still a late filing.

The judgment call this creates for VSYST, stated once so you can put it down:

> **A single non-resident angel cheque roughly doubles the compliance surface of your first round and adds a permanent annual filing.** That does not make it wrong — a well-connected NRI investor with fuel-sector reach could be worth far more than the cost. It does mean the decision must be made with the cost on the table, and it must be made _before_ the term sheet, because retro-fitting FEMA compliance onto a closed round is expensive and occasionally impossible.

## 11. How a raise actually runs

A raise is not a series of meetings. **It is a process with a start date, a close date and a forcing function**, and the single biggest difference between founders who raise well and founders who raise badly is whether they understood that before they took the first meeting.

The failure mode is easy to describe and almost universal: a founder takes meetings as they come, over five months, one at a time. Each meeting goes "well". Nobody says no; investors rarely say no, because a soft maybe costs them nothing and preserves an option. The founder reads twelve maybes as momentum, runs out of runway, and then discovers that the only investor still talking has enormous leverage. **Nothing in that sequence was a bad meeting. The sequence itself was the error.**

### 11.1 The timeline — and the part that starts eighteen months early

| Phase                                    | Duration                             | What actually happens                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Phase 0 — relationships**              | **12–18 months before**              | The [[C23-stakeholder-and-investor-update\|C23]] monthly update to a list of five to fifteen names. No ask. Just a record of what you said you would do and what you did. This is the phase that compresses everything after it, and it is the only one you can be doing today |
| **Phase 1 — readiness**                  | 4–6 weeks                            | Narrative, deck, model, data room, cap table cleaned, the diligence red list closed ([[C14-fundraise-readiness-and-data-room-index\|C14]])                                                                                                                                     |
| **Phase 2 — list and sequencing**        | 1–2 weeks                            | Build a tiered target list. Get warm introductions lined up so that first meetings can be _batched_                                                                                                                                                                            |
| **Phase 3 — the meetings**               | **3–4 weeks, compressed on purpose** | First meetings, then partner meetings. Compression is the whole technique: parallel conversations create real, non-manufactured urgency                                                                                                                                        |
| **Phase 4 — term sheet and negotiation** | 1–3 weeks                            | Ideally more than one. §12 is what you are negotiating                                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| **Phase 5 — diligence**                  | 3–6 weeks                            | Legal, financial, technical, customer references                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| **Phase 6 — documentation and closing**  | 3–6 weeks                            | SSA, SHA, conditions precedent, board and shareholder approvals, money in, filings                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| **Total, from first meeting to money**   | **4–6 months**                       | And it can be longer. This is why §8.3 says a raise is the slowest available fix for a cash-flow problem                                                                                                                                                                       |

**Start with at least nine months of runway. Twelve is better.** The reason is not comfort. It is that an investor can smell desperation through a screen, and the terms available to a founder with three months of cash are not the terms available to a founder who can credibly say "we're fine either way, and here is our profitability path" ([Paul Graham — How to Raise Money](https://www.paulgraham.com/fr.html), 2013; [Paul Graham — The Fundraising Survival Guide](https://www.paulgraham.com/fundraising.html), 2008).

### 11.2 The narrative comes before the deck

Write the story in prose first — one page, no slides. If you cannot write it as five paragraphs that a stranger finds compelling, no deck will rescue it, and you will spend three weeks arranging fonts around an argument that does not exist. [[C15-investor-narrative-and-deck-outline|C15]] owns the format; [[12-communication-the-ceo-as-chief-storyteller|lesson 12]] owns the craft.

The five paragraphs, in this order:

1. **The change in the world.** Something is different now — digitisation of fuel retail compliance, GST and e-invoicing pushing paper businesses onto systems, a generational handover in dealer families. Start here, not with your product. Investors fund a wave; they do not fund a feature.
2. **The specific broken thing.** Not "SMBs lack software". _A fuel dealer sets tomorrow's rate at 11 PM tonight, dispatches on a paper slip at 5 AM, and reconciles a month later from a diary._ Concrete beats general every time.
3. **Why now, and why you.** The domain-expert director's three decades, the technical CEO who ships, and a live product in both app stores. **Say the unfair advantage out loud** — investors are pattern-matching for founder-market fit and will not infer it.
4. **The evidence.** Whatever is actually true, stated without inflation: paying tenants, retention, the cold-close count from §8.6's condition (b), CAC and payback if you have them. **A small honest number beats a large vague one** — experienced investors discount vagueness far harder than they discount small.
5. **The wedge and the expansion.** Rate-and-dispatch is the wedge. The expansion is the §8.5 argument: the platform sits on the transaction, and financial products follow. **This is the paragraph that answers §8.4's TAM problem**, and it must be honest — an expansion you do not intend is a lie with a three-year fuse.

### 11.3 The deck

Ten to fifteen slides. One idea per slide. The classic templates still hold: [Sequoia's business-plan outline](https://www.sequoiacap.com/article/writing-a-business-plan/) and Guy Kawasaki's **10/20/30 rule** — ten slides, twenty minutes, thirty-point type. The full slide-by-slide sits in [[C15-investor-narrative-and-deck-outline|C15]].

Four India-specific notes that generic advice will not give you:

- **Show the product on a phone.** DZZLO is mobile-first and a working screen recording of a real dispatch with a driver OTP does more than three slides of architecture. Record it at a real pump.
- **Get the denominator right and cite it.** Use [PPAC's published retail-outlet count](https://ppac.gov.in/infrastructure/retail-outlets), name the source on the slide, and show the _reachable_ subset rather than the whole country. A sourced ₹300-crore TAM is stronger than an unsourced ₹5,000-crore one, because the second tells an investor you have not done the work.
- **Do not hide the ticket size.** ₹1,799 per GSTIN is what it is. Address §8.4 head-on with the expansion path rather than letting a partner find the arithmetic in the meeting after yours.
- **Never put a "use of funds" slide up that is 60% salaries with no plan attached.** Tie every rupee to a milestone that changes the company's evidence base.

### 11.4 The data room

Build it before you need it. Not because investors demand it early — they do not — but because **assembling it is how you find your own problems**, and finding them four months before a diligence request is the difference between a fixable issue and a re-priced round. [[C14-fundraise-readiness-and-data-room-index|C14]] is the full index; the shape is:

| Folder                    | Contents                                                                                                                       | The one that catches people out                                                                                                                 |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **01 Corporate**          | Incorporation certificate, MoA/AoA, board and shareholder minutes, statutory registers, ROC filing history, share certificates | **The minute book.** Governance built retroactively is visible instantly ([[C17-board-pack-agenda-and-minutes\|C17]])                           |
| **02 Cap table**          | Current cap table, all share issue documents, founders' agreement, vesting, ESOP scheme and grants                             | **Vesting.** A three-founder company with no vesting is a diligence finding, every time ([[C02-founders-agreement-and-vesting-checklist\|C02]]) |
| **03 Financials**         | Audited accounts, monthly MIS, bank statements, the runway model, tax filings                                                  | **Reconciliation** between the MIS you show and the audited accounts you file                                                                   |
| **04 Tax and compliance** | GST returns, TDS, PF/ESI if applicable, DPIIT and 80-IAC status, the compliance calendar                                       | Related-party transactions with the family petrol pump. **Paper them now** ([[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties\|lesson 09]] §3.4)   |
| **05 Customers**          | Contracts, the pipeline, cohort retention, churn, reference list                                                               | Whether your contracts are actually signed, or are WhatsApp confirmations                                                                       |
| **06 Product and tech**   | Architecture note, security posture, roadmap, third-party licences, app-store accounts                                         | **IP assignment.** Every founder, employee and contractor who has ever written code must have assigned it to the company in writing             |
| **07 Team**               | Org chart, employment contracts, offer letters, comp bands                                                                     | Contractors doing employee work                                                                                                                 |
| **08 Legal**              | Vendor and partner agreements, IOCL and Easebuzz correspondence, litigation (if any), insurance                                | Any MoU that contains an exclusivity or IP clause nobody read                                                                                   |

**The diligence item that most often costs an Indian founder real money is IP assignment**, and it is free to fix today and expensive to fix later. If any code in DZZLO was written by anyone — a founder before incorporation, a friend, a freelancer, an intern — without a signed assignment to VSYST Technologies Pvt. Ltd., that is an open question about who owns your only asset. **Close it this month.**

### 11.5 Run it as a process, with a close date

The mechanics that convert twelve maybes into a closed round:

1. **Build a tiered list of 30–50 names**, not five. Tier A: funds and angels whose thesis genuinely fits (§9.3's four questions answered well). Tier B: plausible. Tier C: unlikely but useful practice.
2. **Take your first four meetings from Tier C.** You will be bad at this for the first three. Be bad at it in front of people whose "no" costs you nothing, and rewrite the deck after each one.
3. **Warm introductions only, and ask for them properly.** Send the introducer a forwardable paragraph they can paste, so that saying yes takes them ninety seconds. Cold outreach to Indian seed funds converts poorly; the founder of a portfolio company is the highest-yield path.
4. **Compress the calendar.** Get Tier A and B first meetings into a three-week window. This is not a trick — it is the only honest way to create parallel processes, and parallel processes are what produce more than one term sheet.
5. **Announce a close date, internally, and hold it.** "We're closing on the 30th" said to yourself and your co-founders is a scheduling decision. Said to an investor as manufactured pressure when it is not true, it is a lie they have heard before and will test.
6. **Track everything in one sheet** — name, firm, source of intro, stage, last contact, next step, owner. The `Pipeline` tab in [vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx](toolkit/vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx) works exactly the same way it does for dealers in [[07-customers-markets-and-founder-led-sales|lesson 07]]. **A raise is a sales process with a longer cycle and a worse CRM.**
7. **Force the no.** After the second meeting, ask directly: _"What would you need to see to write a cheque, and is that something we could show you in the next sixty days?"_ A real investor answers this. A polite maybe does not — and now you know, in week three instead of month four.
8. **Debrief every meeting in writing, the same day.** What did they push on? What did you not have an answer for? Three meetings in, the pattern in the objections is telling you something about the business, not about the pitch ([[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]]).

### 11.6 Diligence, and how to survive it

Diligence is where the round is actually lost, and it is almost never lost on the thing the founder was worried about. It is lost on **inconsistency** — a number in the deck that does not match the MIS, a customer count that does not match the contracts, a founder who says one thing and a co-founder who says another.

| Stream                  | What they check                                                                            | How to be ready                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Legal**               | Corporate records, cap table, IP assignment, contracts, litigation, related-party dealings | Fix the §11.4 red list **before** the first meeting, not during the process                                                                                                                                  |
| **Financial**           | Revenue recognition, burn, the model's assumptions, tax and statutory dues                 | One version of the numbers. **Never maintain a "deck number" and a "real number"**                                                                                                                           |
| **Technical**           | Architecture, security, tenant isolation, key-person risk, code quality                    | Have an honest one-page technical note. Known weaknesses stated by you read as competence; found by them, they read as concealment ([[13-product-and-technology-leadership-for-a-technical-ceo\|lesson 13]]) |
| **Customer references** | They will call your dealers. Some without asking                                           | Know which dealers you would be happy to have called, and make sure the unhappy ones are unhappy about something you have already disclosed                                                                  |
| **Founder references**  | They will call people who have worked with you                                             | Give them a real list, including someone who has seen you at your worst                                                                                                                                      |

**The rule that governs all five: disclose early, disclose in writing, disclose the thing you are least comfortable with.** A problem you name in week one is a term. The same problem discovered in week six is a re-price or a walk.

### 11.7 Term sheet to closing

| Step                                                                    | What it is                                                                                                                                                                 | Watch for                                                                                                                                                                           |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Term sheet**                                                          | A short, mostly **non-binding** summary of the commercial terms — except that the **exclusivity / no-shop and confidentiality clauses usually _are_ binding**              | Read §12 before signing. This document sets everything; the long-form agreements implement it, they do not renegotiate it                                                           |
| **Exclusivity clock**                                                   | A no-shop period, commonly 30–60 days, during which you may not talk to other investors                                                                                    | **Negotiate it down and make it lapse automatically.** If diligence drags, an open-ended no-shop hands all leverage to one party                                                    |
| **Confirmatory diligence**                                              | §11.6                                                                                                                                                                      | Every week here is a week of runway. Set milestone dates in the term sheet                                                                                                          |
| **SSA — Share Subscription Agreement**                                  | The purchase contract: how many shares, at what price, when the money moves, and the **representations and warranties** you and often the founders personally give         | **Warranty caps and survival periods.** Founders giving uncapped personal warranties is a real risk in Indian seed documents. Get it capped, and get it limited to a defined period |
| **SHA — Shareholders' Agreement**                                       | The governance contract: board, reserved matters, transfer restrictions, information rights, exit provisions. **This is the document you live inside for the next decade** | §12 and §13, all of it                                                                                                                                                              |
| **Conditions precedent**                                                | Things that must be true before the money moves — cleaned cap table, vesting executed, statutory filings brought current, sometimes specific hires                         | CPs are where deferred housekeeping becomes an emergency. Two closing rules:

- **Get your own lawyer.** Not the investor's lawyer, not a friend, not a template. An Indian seed round done properly costs real money in legal fees and it is among the best money you will ever spend. The standard reference for what the terms mean is [Brad Feld and Jason Mendelson's _Venture Deals_](https://www.venturedeals.com/) — read it before, not during. [Y Combinator's published documents](https://www.ycombinator.com/documents) are useful for seeing what standard looks like, even though the instruments differ in India (§10.4).
- **The money is not raised until it is in the bank.** Not at the term sheet, not at signing, not at the "closing dinner". Do not hire, do not sign a lease, do not tell your team, and do not stop selling until the credit shows in the account. Rounds fall apart at every one of those stages, and they fall apart most often after a founder has already spent the money in their head ([Elad Gil — _High Growth Handbook_](https://growth.eladgil.com/)).

## 12. The terms that matter more than the valuation

Ask a founder what they got and they will tell you the valuation. Ask them in year four what actually happened and they will tell you about a term.

**Valuation is the headline. The terms are the deal.** A ₹15 crore valuation with a 2x participating liquidation preference, a full-ratchet anti-dilution, a 15% pre-money option pool and a redemption right is a worse deal — arithmetically, not rhetorically — than ₹12 crore clean. This section works the arithmetic so you can see it rather than take it on faith. All of it lives in [[C16-term-sheet-decoder-and-cap-table|C16]] and the `Cap Table & Dilution` and `ESOP Pool` tabs of [vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx](toolkit/vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx).

**The running example**, used for every calculation below so the numbers are comparable:

```
VSYST, illustrative seed round
  Pre-money valuation           ₹12 crore
  Investment                    ₹3 crore
  Post-money valuation          ₹15 crore
  Investor ownership            20%
  Founders before the round     100%  (three founder-directors)
```

### 12.1 Liquidation preference — the term that decides the likely outcome

A liquidation preference is the investor's right to be paid **first** out of the proceeds of a sale or winding up. Two variables define it: the **multiple** (1x, 1.5x, 2x) and whether it is **participating** or **non-participating**.

- **1x non-participating** — the investor takes the higher of (their money back) or (their ownership percentage of the proceeds). They choose; they do not get both. **This is the founder-friendly market standard and it is what you should insist on.**
- **1x participating ("double dip")** — the investor takes their money back **and then also** their percentage of what remains.
- **2x participating** — twice their money back first, then their percentage of the rest.

Now the arithmetic on the running example. "Everyone else" means founders plus the option pool.

| Exit value    | 1x non-participating                             | 1x participating                                 | 2x participating                                 |
| ------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------ |
| **₹9 crore**  | Investor ₹3.00 cr · everyone else **₹6.00 cr**   | Investor ₹4.20 cr · everyone else **₹4.80 cr**   | Investor ₹6.60 cr · everyone else **₹2.40 cr**   |
| **₹15 crore** | Investor ₹3.00 cr · everyone else **₹12.00 cr**  | Investor ₹5.40 cr · everyone else **₹9.60 cr**   | Investor ₹7.80 cr · everyone else **₹7.20 cr**   |
| **₹30 crore** | Investor ₹6.00 cr · everyone else **₹24.00 cr**  | Investor ₹8.40 cr · everyone else **₹21.60 cr**  | Investor ₹10.80 cr · everyone else **₹19.20 cr** |
| **₹75 crore** | Investor ₹15.00 cr · everyone else **₹60.00 cr** | Investor ₹17.40 cr · everyone else **₹57.60 cr** | Investor ₹19.80 cr · everyone else **₹55.20 cr** |

Read the ₹15 crore row twice, because it is the row that matters for VSYST. **At an exit exactly equal to the post-money valuation — a genuinely respectable outcome — the founders receive ₹12 crore under a clean term and ₹7.2 crore under an aggressive one. Forty per cent of the founders' outcome, decided by a clause, at an identical headline valuation.**

And notice the shape of the whole table: **the preference matters most in the modest outcomes and barely at all in the enormous ones.** At ₹75 crore the difference between the best and worst column is 8% of the founders' proceeds. At ₹9 crore it is 60%. Which means:

> **Because §8.4 says VSYST's honest outcome is a good-but-modest one, the liquidation preference is, for this specific company, the single most consequential term in the document — more consequential than the valuation.** A founder pattern-matching on Silicon Valley advice, where the assumed outcome is enormous, will under-weight exactly the term they should fight hardest on.

Three related traps:

| Trap                             | What it does                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  | What to ask for                                                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| -------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Uncapped participation**       | The double dip runs all the way up                                                                                                                                                                                                            | If you must accept participation, **cap it** — participation ceases once the investor has received 2x or 3x, above which they convert to ordinary. Better: refuse participation                                                    |
| **Stacked (senior) preferences** | A later round's preference ranks _ahead of_ the earlier one. Across three rounds the stack can exceed the entire exit value, and the ordinary shareholders — you and your team — receive nothing at a sale that looks successful from outside | Ask for **pari passu** stacking. Model the stack every round; the `Cap Table & Dilution` tab should show "preference overhang" as a line                                                                                           |
| **Redemption / put rights**      | A right for the investor to require their shares to be bought back after some years — a debt obligation wearing an equity costume                                                                                                             | **Refuse.** A company that could fund a redemption would not have needed the money. And for a non-resident investor, an assured-return or guaranteed-exit right raises serious FEMA questions — **VERIFY LIVE** with a FEMA lawyer |

### 12.2 Anti-dilution — full ratchet versus broad-based weighted average

Anti-dilution protects the investor if you later sell shares at a **lower** price than they paid. It works by adjusting the price at which their preference shares convert into equity — more equity shares for the same money.

Same running example. The investor bought at **₹100 per share**: ₹3 crore for **3,00,000 CCPS**, against **15,00,000** fully diluted shares in total. Eighteen months later the company raises a down round: **₹1.5 crore at ₹50 per share — 3,00,000 new shares.**

**Full ratchet.** The conversion price resets all the way to the new, lower price, as though the investor had paid ₹50 from the start.

```
  New conversion price          = ₹50
  Investor's shares on conversion = ₹3,00,00,000 / ₹50 = 6,00,000 shares
  Extra shares created for free   = 3,00,000
```

**Broad-based weighted average.** The conversion price moves only _partway_ down, weighted by how much cheap stock was actually issued relative to the size of the company.

```
  NCP = OCP x (A + B) / (A + C)

    OCP = old conversion price                       = ₹100
    A   = fully diluted shares before the down round
          (INCLUDING the option pool and all
           convertibles - this is what "broad" means) = 15,00,000
    B   = money raised / OCP = ₹1,50,00,000 / ₹100    =  1,50,000
    C   = shares actually issued in the down round    =  3,00,000

  NCP = 100 x (15,00,000 + 1,50,000) / (15,00,000 + 3,00,000)
      = 100 x 16,50,000 / 18,00,000
      = ₹91.67

  Investor's shares on conversion = ₹3,00,00,000 / ₹91.67 = 3,27,273
  Extra shares created            = 27,273
```

|                                 | Full ratchet | Broad-based weighted average | Narrow-based weighted average |
| ------------------------------- | ------------ | ---------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| New conversion price            | ₹50.00       | ₹91.67                       | ₹90.91                        |
| Investor's shares               | 6,00,000     | 3,27,273                     | 3,30,000                      |
| **Extra shares taken from you** | **3,00,000** | **27,273**                   | **30,000**                    |

**Full ratchet costs the founders eleven times as much as broad-based weighted average in this example**, and the multiple gets worse the smaller the down round is relative to the company. The "narrow-based" variant excludes the option pool and unissued convertibles from **A**, which mechanically produces a lower conversion price and more shares for the investor — a smaller effect than the ratchet, but always in the investor's direction, and it grows with the size of your pool.

**The house call: broad-based weighted average, and nothing else.** It is genuine market standard, it is defensible in any negotiation, and a fund that insists on full ratchet at seed is telling you something about how it expects to behave later. Two related asks worth making: a **carve-out** so that shares issued under the ESOP, to strategic partners, or on conversion of existing instruments do not trigger the adjustment; and **no "pay-to-play"** trap — or, better, a pay-to-play clause that requires an investor to participate in the down round to keep their anti-dilution protection.

### 12.3 The option pool — pre-money versus post-money, worked

This is the term most often mispriced by first-time founders, because it does not look like a price at all. It looks like housekeeping.

The investor says: _"We need a 15% option pool for the next eighteen months of hiring, and it should be in the pre-money."_ Those last four words move roughly two crore rupees, and the word "valuation" is never spoken.

**Pool created pre-money** — the pool is carved out of the company _before_ the new money arrives, so **only the existing shareholders (you) are diluted by it.** The investor's percentage is unaffected.

**Pool created post-money** — the pool is created _after_ the round, so **everyone dilutes for it, the investor included.**

Same running example: ₹3 crore at ₹12 crore pre / ₹15 crore post, investor at 20%, founders at 100% beforehand.

| Pool size   | Created    | Founders  | Investor | Pool  | **Founders' value at ₹15 cr** | Cost to founders vs no pool |
| ----------- | ---------- | --------- | -------- | ----- | ----------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| **No pool** | —          | **80.0%** | 20.0%    | —     | **₹12.00 cr**                 | baseline                    |
| **6%**      | Post-money | **75.2%** | 18.8%    | 6.0%  | **₹11.28 cr**                 | −₹0.72 cr                   |
| **6%**      | Pre-money  | **74.0%** | 20.0%    | 6.0%  | **₹11.10 cr**                 | −₹0.90 cr                   |
| **10%**     | Post-money | **72.0%** | 18.0%    | 10.0% | **₹10.80 cr**                 | −₹1.20 cr                   |
| **10%**     | Pre-money  | **70.0%** | 20.0%    | 10.0% | **₹10.50 cr**                 | −₹1.50 cr                   |
| **15%**     | Post-money | **68.0%** | 17.0%    | 15.0% | **₹10.20 cr**                 | −₹1.80 cr                   |
| **15%**     | Pre-money  | **65.0%** | 20.0%    | 15.0% | **₹9.75 cr**                  | −₹2.25 cr                   |

Two conclusions, and the second one is the one nobody tells you.

**First — the pre-money pool is a silent discount on the price you agreed.** With a 15% pre-money pool, the founders end up holding 65% of a ₹15 crore company: **₹9.75 crore**, against a headline pre-money of ₹12 crore. The investor effectively bought in at a **₹9.75 crore** pre-money, an **18.75% discount on the number in the term sheet**, and every document will still say ₹12 crore. This is why the manoeuvre has a name — the **option pool shuffle**.

**Second — and this is the part that matters — the _size_ of the pool is worth far more than the pre-versus-post argument.**

```
  Winning the pre/post fight at a 10% pool:   70.0% -> 72.0%   = +2.0 points
  Cutting the pool from 15% pre to 6% pre:    65.0% -> 74.0%   = +9.0 points
```

**Four and a half times the value, and it is a far easier argument to win**, because it is an argument about facts rather than about market convention. So do this:

1. **Build the pool bottom-up from an actual hiring plan**, not from a percentage someone quoted. List the roles you will genuinely hire in the next eighteen months, the grant each one needs at your comp bands, and total it. [[C20-compensation-bands-and-esop-design|C20]] and [[10-building-the-team-hiring-equity-and-firing|lesson 10]] own that arithmetic; the `ESOP Pool` tab holds it.
2. **Bring the plan to the negotiation as a document.** "Our 18-month plan needs 6.2%; here are the eleven roles and the grants" beats "15% feels like a lot" in every room, every time.
3. **Only then argue pre versus post.** You will usually lose it — the pre-money pool is genuinely market convention in India and worldwide — but losing a 2-point argument after winning a 9-point one is a good afternoon.
4. **Count existing grants separately.** If part of the pool is already promised to people, that portion is not available for future hiring, and an investor sizing "the pool" may be sizing the _unallocated_ portion. Get the definition written down.

### 12.4 Board composition and control

At seed, the board usually goes from three founder-directors to some combination of founders, an investor director, and possibly an independent. Three shapes, in descending order of founder comfort:

| Shape                                                              | What it means                                                | Note for VSYST                                                                                                                                        |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Observer only**                                                  | The investor attends and receives the pack but does not vote | The best seed outcome. Ask for it first                                                                                                               |
| **One investor director on a board that keeps a founder majority** | E.g. three founders + one investor = four seats              | **A four-person board is an even board.** Decide the tie-break — chair's casting vote, or a fifth independent — in the SHA, not in the first deadlock |
| **Investor director plus a jointly-appointed independent**         | Founders no longer control the board on their own            | Acceptable later; premature at seed for a three-person company                                                                                        |

Two points specific to a family-founded Indian company:

- **VSYST already has three founder-directors.** Adding an investor director creates the even-board problem above and, more importantly, changes what board meetings _are_ — from a family conversation with an agenda to a meeting with an outsider in the room. [[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties|Lesson 09]] §8.7 already tells you not to let the board meeting become the family meeting. After a raise that stops being advice and becomes a requirement.
- **Board control and shareholder control are different things and both matter.** Under the Companies Act an ordinary resolution needs a simple majority and a **special resolution needs 75%** — which means a shareholder or bloc holding **more than 25% can block every special resolution**, including further share issues, AoA amendments and a great many things a growing company needs to do. Track both numbers on your cap table, every round. **VERIFY LIVE** the specific thresholds and which matters fall into which category with your CS.

### 12.5 Protective provisions — the reserved matters list

These are the decisions that require the investor's consent regardless of what the board or the shareholders decide. They are where control actually lives, and they are usually skimmed because they are drafted as a long list in an annexure.

Typical list, with the ones that bite a company like VSYST marked:

| Reserved matter                                    | Normal                 | **Bites at VSYST because…**                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Sale, merger, or winding up of the company         | Yes, always reasonable | —                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| Issue of new shares or any change to share capital | Yes                    | Includes topping up the ESOP pool — so hiring plans acquire a consent step                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| Amendment of the MoA / AoA                         | Yes                    | —                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| **Any related-party transaction**                  | Yes                    | **This is the big one.** Every arrangement with the family petrol pump, every payment to a relative, every rent cheque to a director's property becomes a consented, disclosed item. [[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties\|Lesson 09]] §3.4 asks you to paper these anyway — **do it before the raise, or diligence will do it for you** |
| Borrowing above a threshold                        | Yes                    | Negotiate a **threshold**, not a blanket veto                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| Approving the annual budget                        | Common                 | Ties your operating year to an external approval date. Agree the timetable up front                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| **Changing founder compensation**                  | Common                 | An investor will normalise founder salaries — see §2.4. For a family company where directors' pay may be entangled with family arrangements, decide this at home first                                                                                                                                                                             |
| Hiring or firing key employees / C-level           | Common                 | Fine at C-level; push back if drafted to catch every hire above a low salary band                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| Changing the nature of the business                | Yes                    | The §8.5 fintech expansion is a **change in the nature of the business.** If that is your plan, do not let the clause be drafted so tightly that your own strategy needs permission                                                                                                                                                                |
| Transfer of founder shares                         | Yes                    | Interacts with what happens when a family director wants out (§13)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |

**How to negotiate this list — you are not going to delete it.** Do three things instead: (a) attach **materiality thresholds** in rupees to everything that can carry one, so ordinary operations do not need consent; (b) add a **deemed-consent clause** — if the investor does not respond within a stated number of business days, consent is deemed given, which prevents a slow investor from freezing the company; (c) make sure consent sits with an **investor majority** rather than with each investor individually, so that one small holder cannot block the company later.

### 12.6 Founder vesting — including the reset

Almost every institutional round imposes vesting on founder shares — typically four years with a one-year cliff — _even on shares you already own_. This feels outrageous the first time you see it and it is entirely standard, and the logic is honest: the investor is buying a team, and a founder who leaves in month eight holding a third of the company makes the company unfundable forever.

What to negotiate:

- **Credit for time served.** If you incorporated three years ago and have been full-time throughout, argue for a substantial portion vested at closing. Investors expect this ask.
- **Single-trigger vs double-trigger acceleration on a change of control.** Double-trigger (acquisition **and** termination without cause) is standard and reasonable. Full single-trigger acceleration is rarely granted.
- **Good leaver / bad leaver definitions.** Get "cause" defined narrowly and objectively. A vague cause definition combined with a bad-leaver forfeiture is the most dangerous pair of clauses in a founder document.
- **What "leaving" means for a director who stays a relative.** This is the family-company clause that no template has, and it is the one you most need. A director who stops being full-time but remains a shareholder, a relative and a person at Diwali dinner is a situation the SHA must address in writing, before it happens.

**Do the vesting now, between the three of you, before any investor asks.** [[C02-founders-agreement-and-vesting-checklist|C02]] and [[03-the-ceo-core-value-and-the-founder-contracts|lesson 03]] own it. Vesting agreed voluntarily among three founders is a fairness mechanism. Vesting imposed by an investor during diligence is a negotiation between family members with a stranger holding the pen.

### 12.7 Transfer terms — drag, tag, ROFR

| Term                              | What it does                                                                                                                       | The negotiation                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Drag-along**                    | If a defined majority approves a sale, everyone else **must** sell on the same terms. Prevents a small holder blocking a good exit | Fight over the **threshold** — insist that the drag requires a founder-majority approval too, not just an investor majority. Add a **minimum price floor** below which the drag cannot be exercised. And limit the **warranties** you can be dragged into giving |
| **Tag-along**                     | If a large holder sells, smaller holders may join on the same terms                                                                | Broadly good for you. Make sure it is mutual                                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| **ROFR / ROFO**                   | Right of first refusal / first offer on any share transfer                                                                         | Normal. Just check the process has a **time limit**, or a transfer can be frozen indefinitely                                                                                                                                                                    |
| **Founder lock-in / non-compete** | Founders cannot sell, and cannot compete, for a defined period                                                                     | Check the duration, the geographic scope, and — for a family company — **whether it accidentally catches the family's existing fuel business.** This is a real risk at VSYST and must be carved out explicitly                                                   |

### 12.8 Information rights, and the no-shop

**Information rights** are usually: monthly management accounts within a stated number of days, quarterly reviewed financials, audited annuals, an approved annual budget, and an inspection right. All reasonable. The only mistake founders make is **agreeing to a cadence they cannot hit** — a contractual monthly MIS by the 10th, from a three-person company with no finance hire, is a breach of contract waiting to be signed. Agree what you can actually deliver; the machinery for delivering it is [[06-money-rails-and-finance-operations|COO 06]] and [[17-the-numbers-a-ceo-watches|lesson 17]], and [[C23-stakeholder-and-investor-update|C23]] is the format.

**The no-shop / exclusivity** clause in the term sheet is one of the few parts that binds you. Three rules: keep it **short** (30 days, 45 at most), make it **lapse automatically** if the investor has not closed by a stated date, and make sure it does **not** prevent you from continuing conversations that were already live before signing. An open-ended no-shop is how a founder with six months of runway ends up with three.

### 12.9 The two-round dilution table — what actually happens to a founder

The arithmetic nobody does until it is too late. Three founders start at 100%. Two rounds later:

| Shareholder                                  | Today      | After seed (₹3 cr @ ₹15 cr post, 10% pool pre-money) | After Series A (₹15 cr @ ₹60 cr post, pool topped to 12%, pre-money) |
| -------------------------------------------- | ---------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Founders (three, combined)**               | **100.0%** | **70.0%**                                            | **49.0%**                                                            |
| Seed investor                                | —          | 20.0%                                                | 14.0%                                                                |
| Series A investor                            | —          | —                                                    | 25.0%                                                                |
| ESOP pool                                    | —          | 10.0%                                                | 12.0%                                                                |
| **Total**                                    | 100.0%     | 100.0%                                               | 100.0%                                                               |
| _Founders' value at that round's post-money_ | —          | _₹10.50 cr_                                          | _₹29.40 cr_                                                          |

If the seed investor exercises pro-rata rights and takes ₹3 crore of the ₹15 crore Series A, the last column becomes: founders 49.0%, seed 19.0%, new Series A investors 20.0%, pool 12.0%.

Now the comparison that should be printed and pinned somewhere:

| Path                                                                                                         | Founders' combined holding after the Series A                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Seed round, then Series A (above)                                                                            | **49.0%**                                                                                      |
| **No seed round** — bootstrap to the same metrics, then raise the same ₹15 cr at ₹60 cr post with a 12% pool | **63.0%**                                                                                      |
| **Difference**                                                                                               | **14 percentage points — ₹8.4 crore at a ₹60 crore valuation, ₹14 crore at a ₹100 crore exit** |

**The honest caveat, stated as plainly as the number:** that comparison is only real if you actually reach Series A metrics without the seed money. If the seed round is what got you there, the 63% column does not exist — it is 100% of nothing. **That is precisely the trade §8.2 and §8.6 are asking you to price, and the whole point of writing the trigger down in advance is to make that trade with arithmetic in front of you rather than optimism.**

Three more readings of the same table:

- **Per founder.** If the CEO holds 40% of the founder block, the CEO's personal stake after two rounds is **19.6%**. Founders instinctively track the combined number and are surprised by their own.
- **Control.** At 49% combined, the founders can no longer pass an ordinary resolution on their own — but they remain comfortably above the ~25% blocking stake for special resolutions (§12.4). At the round after, both thresholds come into play. **Model control, not just economics, every round.**
- **The pool is the quiet 12%.** It is not lost — it goes to the people who build the company, which is the point (§10.2, [[C20-compensation-bands-and-esop-design|C20]]). But it dilutes exactly like an investor and should appear in every model as its own line, never netted off.

## 13. What changes the day you take money

The money arrives on a Tuesday. Nothing feels different on Wednesday. The changes are real, they are permanent, and most of them arrive slowly enough that founders attribute them to something else — to growth, to stress, to a co-founder becoming difficult. They are not. They are the terms, operating as designed.

### 13.1 Before and after

|                            | Bootstrapped VSYST today                                   | VSYST the day after a seed round                                                                         |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Who you answer to**      | Each other, and the customers who pay you                  | Each other, the customers, **and a shareholder with contractual rights**                                 |
| **The board**              | Three founder-directors, family, informal                  | A board with an outsider in the room, a notice period, a pack, and minutes that will be read years later |
| **Reporting**              | Whatever you choose to look at                             | A contractual cadence with dates. Miss it twice and it becomes the topic                                 |
| **Decisions**              | Yours                                                      | Yours, except for a list in an annexure (§12.5)                                                          |
| **Related-party dealings** | A family arrangement                                       | A consented, disclosed, minuted transaction — every time                                                 |
| **Founder pay**            | A family decision (§2.4)                                   | A reserved matter, usually normalised and capped                                                         |
| **Pace**                   | Your judgment about what is sustainable                    | An implicit contract to reach the next round's metrics inside the money's life                           |
| **Time horizon**           | Indefinite. The company can just keep being a good company | **Finite.** A fund has a life and must return capital                                                    |
| **Failure**                | Painful, private, yours                                    | Painful, semi-public, and with a formal process                                                          |
| **A great outcome**        | ₹15 crore of ARR, profitable, family-owned, forever        | A liquidity event — and "forever" is no longer on the menu                                               |

That last row is the one that ends more founder-investor relationships than any other, and it is fully visible on day one if anyone bothers to look.

### 13.2 Reporting, the board and the reserved matters, in practice

Three concrete workload changes, so you can price them in hours rather than in feelings:

- **Monthly:** a management pack — cash, burn, runway, revenue, pipeline, churn, headcount, and a written CEO commentary. Whether or not the SHA specifies one, you should send it ([[C23-stakeholder-and-investor-update|C23]]). **Budget three to four hours a month once the data pipeline exists, and considerably more before it does.** [[17-the-numbers-a-ceo-watches|Lesson 17]] owns the numbers; [[06-money-rails-and-finance-operations|COO 06]] owns the plumbing.
- **Quarterly:** a real board meeting with a pack circulated in advance, an agenda, decisions and minutes. [[C17-board-pack-agenda-and-minutes|C17]] is the template and [[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties|lesson 09]] is the discipline. **Budget one to two days of CEO time per quarter, including preparation.**
- **Continuously:** the reserved-matters list becomes a lookup you run before acting. Most founders under-estimate this by an order of magnitude — not because consent is refused, but because **asking takes calendar time**, and calendar time is what a fast company is made of. This is exactly why §12.5's deemed-consent clause matters more than it sounds.

**Roughly two to four working days a month of CEO time is the honest cost of institutional capital, forever.** That is not an argument against raising. It is an argument for knowing the number before you agree to it, and for [[15-the-ceo-operating-cadence-and-calendar|lesson 15]]'s calendar to have somewhere to put it.

### 13.3 The pace expectation

Nobody writes the growth expectation into the term sheet, which is precisely why it causes trouble. It is nonetheless real, and it goes like this: **the seed money is sized for 18 to 24 months, and the unspoken contract is that within that window you reach the metrics that make a Series A possible.** If you do not, the money has not merely been spent — the company is now a company that raised and stalled, which is a harder story to tell than a company that never raised.

Three consequences worth naming in advance:

1. **The bar moves.** "Profitable and growing 40%" is an excellent outcome for a bootstrapped company and an awkward conversation with a fund that needs a fund-returner (§8.4).
2. **Optionality narrows.** The patient, district-by-district, saturate-before-you-expand strategy in [[05-strategy-i-diagnosis-and-the-strategy-kernel|lesson 05]] is correct and it is slow. Capital creates pressure to run it faster than the evidence supports, and running it faster is how the strategy stops working.
3. **The next raise starts early.** In practice you begin preparing the Series A around month 12–14 of an 18–24-month runway — which means roughly half the money's life is spent preparing to raise again. **This is the most under-priced cost of institutional capital**, and it consumes the founder-CEO specifically.

### 13.4 The exit clock

A venture fund has a defined life — commonly around ten years with extensions — after which it must have returned capital to its own investors. Once you have taken money from one, **your company has acquired an expiry date it did not have before**, and everyone in the room is aware of the year even when nobody says it.

This does not mean you will be forced to sell in year seven. It means that from the moment you sign, there is a permanent, structural interest in a liquidity event, and it will shape conversations that appear to be about something else — a partnership discussion that is really a positioning-for-acquisition discussion, a pricing conversation that is really a growth-rate conversation.

**Sit with the three questions before you sign, not after:**

- **Would we sell this company?** Not "would we like to be rich" — _would we hand over the company built on the family's name and network to somebody else, and go to work for them or leave?_
- **At what number?** Say an actual rupee figure out loud, per founder. Discover now if one of you says "never" and another says "₹8 crore each and I'm done".
- **What if we cannot sell and cannot raise again?** This is the most common outcome for a funded company that plateaus, and it is a specific, survivable situation — but only for founders who have thought about it in advance. [[18-risk-crisis-and-the-hard-things|Lesson 18]] and [[C30-pre-mortem-crisis-playbook-and-succession|C30]] own the machinery.

### 13.5 What a family-founder team must decide before signing

This is the section of this lesson written specifically for VSYST, and it is a **before**, not an after. Every item below is easy to settle among three relatives in a room today and extremely hard to settle once an investor's lawyer is drafting around it. Work the list, write the answers down, and get all three signatures on the document ([[C02-founders-agreement-and-vesting-checklist|C02]], [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]]).

| #   | The decision                                                                                               | Why it must be settled first                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| --- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | **Are all three of us genuinely willing to be minority owners of a company an outsider has a vote in?**    | Ask each person separately, and accept a "no". A reluctant third director discovered in diligence is worse than a decision not to raise                                                                                                                                                 |
| 2   | **Which of us are "Founders" under the SHA — and do we understand it is usually joint and several?**       | Founder obligations — warranties, non-compete, vesting, lock-in — typically bind all named founders **together**. One director's breach can become all three directors' problem                                                                                                         |
| 3   | **Who is genuinely full-time, on paper and in fact?**                                                      | Investors price a team. | 4   | **What is each founder's salary, and are we willing to have it normalised and capped?**                    | §2.4 decided this as a capital question. | 5   | **Every related-party arrangement with the family pump — papered, priced at arm's length, board-approved** | Rent, shared staff, shared premises, vehicle use, a customer relationship, anything. [[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties\|Lesson 09]] §3.4 asks for this anyway. **Do it now: it is housekeeping today and a diligence finding in six months**                               |
| 6   | **A written carve-out for the family's existing fuel business from any founder non-compete**               | The default non-compete drafting can catch it. | 7   | **What happens if a director wants out — who buys, at what price, on what formula?**                       | The single hardest conversation in a family company, and the one that curdles fastest if left open. | 8   | **What happens if a director dies or becomes incapacitated?**                                              | Shares, board seat, role, and what the family expects. | 9   | **Are we each personally comfortable giving warranties and indemnities — and have we capped them?**        | In Indian seed documents founders frequently give personal warranties. **Get a cap, get a survival period, and get every director to read the schedule.** Uncapped personal liability across three relatives is a family risk, not just a company risk                                  |
| 10  | **Does anyone in the family expect income from this company?**                                             | A family business distributes. **A venture-backed company essentially never declares a dividend** — the money is reinvested by design. | 11  | **Who speaks to the investor?**                                                                            | One voice, named. | 12  | **Where do family decisions get made now?**                                                                | Not in the board meeting ([[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties\|lesson 09]] §8.7). After a raise, the boundary has to be a scheduled thing, not an intention                                                                                                                  |

**The test for whether you have done this properly:** hand the twelve answers to someone outside the family and ask them to find the one that has been fudged. There will be one. It is the one that will come back.

### 13.6 What does not change — and must not

Two things, both easy to lose in the first six months after a round:

- **The judgment is still yours.** An investor has rights, information and opinions. They do not have your seat. [[02-how-a-ceo-thinks|Lesson 02]]'s decision journal exists so that in month nine you can look back and see whether a decision was yours or was a deference. Deferring to a smart investor once is prudence. Deferring by default is abdication, and it is the specific failure mode of a first-time founder who is grateful for the money.
- **The customer is still the point.** The most dangerous month in a startup's life is the one after a round closes, because the company briefly feels successful without having sold anything new. **The dealers in [[07-customers-markets-and-founder-led-sales|lesson 07]]'s pipeline do not care that you raised.** Keep the Friday pump visits in the calendar the week after the money lands. If they come out of the calendar, that is your first real warning that the round changed the wrong things.

```
  2. Net monthly burn                    -> the number, not the range
  3. Runway in months                    -> base case, and the bear case beside it
  4. Cold closes in the last 12 signings -> the honesty test, condition (b)
```

The first three are §1.2. The fourth is the one that will tell you the truth about whether the trigger box is anywhere near firing, and it is the only one a family-founded company can fool itself about.

### 14.3 What VSYST will not do — the capital no-list

Write these down too. A no-list is a decision; a vague intention is not ([[C25-product-bets-and-the-no-list|C25]]).

- **No equity raise until the §8.6 trigger box fires** — or until the defensive trigger fires, in which case the decision is reopened within 30 days, not acted on within 30 days.
- **No fundraising broker, consultant or "partner" taking a retainer plus a percentage.** Lawyers and CAs billing hourly, yes. Success fees on a seed round, never (§9.2).
- **No accelerator application taken for the cheque.** Only if the answer to "would you do it if the cheque were zero?" is yes (§9.4).
- **No cheque from a non-resident investor without a FEMA lawyer engaged before the term sheet** (§10.8) — and never one where beneficial ownership has not been confirmed in writing.
- **No convertible note under ₹25 lakh in a single tranche**, and none at all before DPIIT recognition exists — it would be a deposit, not a note (§10.3).
- **No investment from anyone who is also a customer, supplier or channel partner** without the commercial relationship being negotiated and papered separately first (§9.5).
- **No multi-year prepay**, because you do not yet know your price (§6.1).
- **No second services project** before the first has hit its written end date (§6.2).
- **No venture-debt or RBF conversations.** They are structurally unavailable pre-revenue and the meetings cost founder-hours that §5 pays better for (§7).
- **No valuation quoted to anyone, ever, before there is a reason and a valuer's report.** A number said casually in a coffee conversation in Raipur becomes an anchor you will negotiate against for two years.
- **No US flip conversation** without a lawyer in the room and a written reason that survives §8.4 (§9.4).

## 15. Exercises

Do 15.1 first — it is the only one that produces a number you will use every week for the rest of the company's life. Do 15.2 the same day, because both of its filings are free and both take longer to be granted than you think. Every exercise produces a dated artefact.

**15.1 — Say the three numbers, then build the model that defends them (45 min, alone, with the bank statements open).** Before opening anything, write down from memory: **cash in the bank, net monthly burn, runway in months.** Now open the bank statements and the books and compute all three properly. **Note the gap** — the gap is the exercise. Then build §3.1's three cases in the `Runway & Scenarios` tab of [vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx](toolkit/vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx): base, bull and bear, each driven by no more than four assumptions you can name out loud. Finally, fill §3.2's ladder in [[C13-runway-burn-and-scenario-planner|C13]] with **the actual calendar dates** on which you hit 12, 9, 6 and 3 months in the **bear** case, and write the pre-committed action for each. **Artefact:** a completed C13 with four dated rows, and a three-scenario `Runway & Scenarios` tab. **Success test:** your remembered runway and your computed runway differ by less than a month — and if they do not, the real artefact is the calendar reminder you just set to re-check every Monday.

**15.2 — Claim the stack: two filings today, and a schedule for the rest (45 min, with the CA on WhatsApp).** File **DPIIT recognition** and **Udyam registration** today — both are free, both are online, and DPIIT is the gate under §80-IAC, CGSS, several Chhattisgarh benefits **and the convertible-note carve-out in §10.3** ([Startup India — recognition](https://www.startupindia.gov.in/content/sih/en/startupgov/startup_recognition_page.html); [Udyam registration](https://udyamregistration.gov.in/)). Then build a one-page **claimed-benefits schedule** covering every item in §5: scheme, eligibility, who owns it, the date it was applied for, the date of the outcome, and the rupee or rupee-equivalent value. Include the cloud and tooling credit programmes, and apply for at least two of them before you close the laptop. **Artefact:** two filed applications with acknowledgement numbers, and a claimed-benefits schedule filed in the vault with every row owned and dated. **Success test:** every row has either an application date or an explicit "not eligible, because…" — no row is blank.

**15.3 — Work your own dilution, then score a real term sheet (45 min, alone, then 20 min with both co-founder-directors).** In the `Cap Table & Dilution` tab, build **your** version of §12.9's two-round table using VSYST's actual current shareholding and your own assumed round sizes. Then, in the `ESOP Pool` tab, reproduce §12.3's seven-row comparison and — this is the part that matters — **size the pool bottom-up from a real eighteen-month hiring plan** using [[C20-compensation-bands-and-esop-design|C20]]'s bands, and see how far your honest number is from 15%. Finally, find a **real, publicly available seed term sheet** and score it against §12.10's ten lines in [[C16-term-sheet-decoder-and-cap-table|C16]], marking each green or red, and read [Brad Feld and Jason Mendelson's _Venture Deals_](https://www.venturedeals.com/) on the two you got wrong. **Artefact:** a filled `Cap Table & Dilution` tab, an `ESOP Pool` tab with a bottom-up pool number, and a completed ten-line C16 scorecard on a real document. **Success test:** you can state, without looking, what a 15% pre-money pool costs the three of you in rupees at a ₹15 crore post-money — and each co-founder-director can state their own post-two-round personal percentage.

**15.4 — Run §13.5's twelve questions with all three directors, and write the answers down (60 min, one sitting, no phones).** Ask each of the twelve questions out loud. **Do questions 1, 7, 9 and 10 individually and in writing first** — each director writes their own answer before anyone speaks — because those four are the ones a family will otherwise resolve by nodding. Then read the answers aloud. Where three answers differ, do not resolve it in the room; note it, name an owner and set a date. Feed the settled answers into [[C02-founders-agreement-and-vesting-checklist|C02]], including the founder vesting schedule, the non-compete carve-out for the family fuel business, the exit mechanism for a departing director, and the dividend expectation. **Artefact:** twelve written answers signed by all three directors, an updated C02, and a dated list of the unresolved ones with owners. **Success test:** at least two of the twelve come back with genuinely different answers from the three directors. If all thirty-six answers agree on the first pass, you asked the questions in a way that invited agreement — ask again, in writing, separately.

**15.5 — Sign the capital-decision box and set its review date (30 min, all three directors).** Take §8.6's box, change the numbers to ones you actually believe, and — this is the only rule — **set them today, before anyone has been flattered by an investor.** Argue condition (b), the cold-close test, until all three of you agree on both the count and the definition of "outside the family and the domain director's own network". Write the defensive trigger in your own words, naming the specific arrangement you would consider a genuine window closing. Sign it, date it, and put a recurring **monthly** review on all three calendars plus a **quarterly** agenda item on the board calendar. **Artefact:** a dated, three-signature capital decision in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] with four named conditions, one defensive trigger, and a review cadence in the calendar. **Success test:** the next time an investor emails, nobody has to think — the answer is already written, and it is _"not yet; may I add you to our monthly update?"_

**15.6 — Start the relationship eighteen months early, and find your own red list (45 min).** Name **five real people** who could plausibly be your first investors — angels, operators, a fund partner, a family office — and how you would reach each one warmly. Write update #1 to §11.1's Phase 0 standard using [[C23-stakeholder-and-investor-update|C23]]: what you said you would do last month, what you did, the number that moved, the number that did not, what you need. **Send it today**, with no ask in it. Then build the [[C14-fundraise-readiness-and-data-room-index|C14]] folder tree from §11.4 and walk it once, marking every folder **green / amber / red** — and pay particular attention to IP assignment, vesting, the minute book and related-party papering. **Artefact:** five named recipients, update #1 sent and dated, a C14 folder tree in the vault, and a red list with an owner and a date on every red item. **Success test:** the red list has at least three items on it, and the IP-assignment row is one of the first two you close.
