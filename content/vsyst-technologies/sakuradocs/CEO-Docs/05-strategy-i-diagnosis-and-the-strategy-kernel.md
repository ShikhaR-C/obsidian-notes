# 05 — Strategy I: Diagnosis and the Strategy Kernel

_Phase 2 · The Outward Job · Months 1–3. After this lesson you can tell a strategy from a wish; write VSYST's diagnosis as one sentence a competent competitor could disagree with; assemble a three-part strategy kernel and a five-choice cascade for DZZLO OMS; size the Indian fuel-distribution software market bottom-up from the per-GSTIN pricing metric and re-run the arithmetic yourself; name the three assumptions whose failure would kill the plan; and put all of it on one page that changes on evidence rather than on mood._

## Explain-it-like-I'm-5

There is a hardware shop on Malviya Road in Raipur. Nuts, bolts, pipe fittings, cement, wire. It has been in the family thirty years and this year the takings are down a fifth. The father says: **"We must double sales."** That is not a plan. That is a wish with a number stapled to it.

The son spends four days doing nothing but watching the counter and calling old customers. He finds one fact: the shop's money never came from walk-in householders — it came from eleven building contractors who bought on 30-day credit and sent a boy to collect. A new shop opened near the bypass eighteen months ago offering 45-day credit. Six of the eleven contractors moved. That is a **diagnosis** — a sentence about what is actually happening, that somebody could argue with.

Then he says the thing that decides everything after it: **"We will not win those contractors back on credit days, because we cannot fund 45 days. We will win them back on delivery — our godown is two kilometres from the new construction belt and theirs is nine."** That is a **guiding policy**: it says what you will do _and_ what you refuse to do. And then four actions that all pull the same way — buy a second tempo, put the counter boy on WhatsApp taking site orders by 8 AM, stop stocking the slow retail-size items eating shelf space so the fast contractor SKUs are always in stock, and give one named contractor a same-day guarantee in writing so the other ten hear about it at the tea stall.

Notice what the son did _not_ do: he did not write "double sales", "become the most trusted hardware brand in Chhattisgarh", or a list of eleven initiatives. He found the real obstacle, chose an approach that used an advantage he actually had, and picked a handful of actions that made each other stronger. **That is the whole of this lesson.** The rest is vocabulary, arithmetic and the discipline to be honest about what you do not know.

## 1. What strategy is not

Start here, because the fastest way to get good at strategy is to stop producing the four things that impersonate it. Richard Rumelt — Professor Emeritus of Strategy at UCLA Anderson ([UCLA Anderson faculty page](https://www.anderson.ucla.edu/faculty-and-research/strategy/faculty/rumelt)) — made his career on the observation that **bad strategy is not the absence of strategy; it is an active, recognisable artefact** with its own house style: "Mom-and-apple-pie values, fluffy packages of buzzwords, motivational slogans, and financial goals" ([_Good Strategy Bad Strategy: The Difference and Why It Matters_, Crown Currency, 2011](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/91324/good-strategy-bad-strategy-by-richard-rumelt/9780307886231/)). His four hallmarks, first laid out for a general audience in "The Perils of Bad Strategy" ([McKinsey Quarterly, June 2011](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/the-perils-of-bad-strategy)), are below with an Indian-SaaS example of each. The examples are **illustrative composites** of decks and plan docs that circulate in Indian vertical SaaS — the pattern is real, the specific company is not.

### 1.2 Three tests you can run in ten seconds

Use these on any sentence claiming to be strategy — including yours.

1. **The opposite test.** Could a competent, well-run competitor state the _opposite_ as their approach and still sound sane? "We will serve customers well" fails: nobody's strategy is to serve customers badly. "We will refuse every dealer outside a 150 km radius of Raipur until one district is saturated" passes — a serious competitor could rationally do the opposite (go wide and thin) and many do.
2. **The refusal test.** What does this forbid? A guiding policy that forbids nothing is not a policy, it is a mood. Write the sentence, then write "which means we will **not** \_\_\_". If the second half is empty, throw the first half away.
3. **The mechanism test.** What has to be true in the world for this to produce the result? If the answer is "we work hard", the strategy is a hope. This is the question that turns a target into a theory, and it is the same question [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log\|C29]] makes you write down for every consequential decision.

A fourth test worth borrowing from engineering: **would you bet money on being wrong?** If you cannot name what would prove the strategy false, you have not written a strategy, you have written a preference. §7 turns that into a mechanism.

## 2. The strategy kernel

Rumelt's contribution is not the critique; it is the replacement. A strategy has a hard nugget at its centre — **the kernel** — with exactly three parts, and everything else in a strategy document is elaboration ([_Good Strategy Bad Strategy_, Crown, 2011](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/91324/good-strategy-bad-strategy-by-richard-rumelt/9780307886231/)).

| Part                | The question it answers                                                         | The failure if you skip it                                                              | The test                                                             |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Diagnosis**       | What is going on here? What is the _nature_ of the obstacle?                    | Everything downstream is arbitrary. You get initiatives with no theory                  | Could a competent competitor read it and disagree?                   |
| **Guiding policy**  | Given that diagnosis, what is our overall approach — and what does it rule out? | You get a to-do list. Actions compete for the same scarce person instead of reinforcing | What does it forbid?                                                 |
| **Coherent action** | What will we actually do, and how do the pieces support each other?             | You get a policy nobody can act on — a strategy that lives only in a deck               | Does each action make at least one other action cheaper or stronger? |

The word doing the most work is **coherent**. Rumelt's later book pushes the same idea one level deeper into what he calls the **crux** — the one part of the challenge that is both critical and addressable, the place where effort actually converts into progress ([_The Crux: How Leaders Become Strategists_, Profile Books / PublicAffairs, 2022](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Rumelt)). At three people with no outside capital, VSYST cannot do five things; the crux is what decides _which one_.

### 2.1 A worked non-software kernel — Nirma against Surf

Take the Indian business case everyone half-remembers and read it as a kernel.

In 1969 Karsanbhai Patel, a chemist in the Gujarat government's Department of Mining and Geology, started making detergent powder in a 10×10 ft room at home and selling 15–20 packets a day from a bicycle on his 15 km ride to work. His powder sold at **₹3.50/kg against Hindustan Lever's Surf at ₹13/kg** — roughly 73% cheaper — and by 1985 it was one of the most popular household detergents in large parts of India ([Nirma — history and pricing, citing Business Standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nirma)).

Read as a kernel:

| Kernel part          | Nirma                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| -------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Diagnosis**        | The mass Indian household could not afford branded detergent, and the market leader's cost structure — phosphate-and-enzyme formulation, national advertising, urban distribution — physically could not follow the price down without destroying its own economics. The constraint was not product quality; it was **price and reach**, and the incumbent was structurally unable to attack there |
| **Guiding policy**   | Reach the price point the incumbent cannot match, using a formulation and a route to market the incumbent does not use — and accept that this rules out competing on cleaning performance, packaging, or urban department stores                                                                                                                                                                   |
| **Coherent actions** | A phosphate-free synthetic formulation that removed the expensive inputs; home manufacturing with no factory overhead; direct door-to-door selling before any retail push; cash sales with no credit; later, mass-market advertising in the vernacular rather than English press                                                                                                                   |

Notice the coherence: the formulation makes the price possible, the price makes door-to-door viable (a household will buy on the spot at ₹3.50), the cash-only sale funds the next batch without working capital, and the absence of overhead is what lets the price hold when volume rises. **No single action explains Nirma. The interlock does.** And notice the refusal: Nirma did not try to be a cheaper Surf. It declined the whole axis on which Surf was strong.

That refusal is the part founders skip. It is also the part that makes strategy uncomfortable, because it means saying out loud that you will be worse at something.

### 2.2 Coherent versus merely parallel

Here is the distinction, with two lists of five actions for the same company. Both lists are plausible. Only one is a strategy.

**Merely parallel** — five sensible things, done at once, by three people:

1. Launch a self-serve website with pricing and card checkout.
2. Hire a field sales rep in Raipur.
3. Build a WhatsApp Business API integration.
4. Start a content-marketing blog in English.
5. Apply to a startup accelerator.

Each is defensible. Together they are a workload. They share no theory, they compete for the same two pairs of hands, and if four succeed and one fails you cannot say whether the strategy worked.

**Coherent** — five actions that make each other cheaper:

1. Pick one district cluster and refuse the rest for two quarters.
2. Get one named, respected dealer in that cluster live and visibly happy, and film him at his own pump in Chhattisgarhi/Hindi.
3. Use that video as the opening move with the district association office-bearer, whose endorsement is the trust channel that actually works for this buyer.
4. Do white-glove onboarding personally — migrate the outstanding receivables ledger yourself — so the first invoice happens in days, not weeks.
5. Ship only what unblocks onboarding in that cluster; put every other feature request on the no-list.

Action 2 makes action 3 cheap. Action 3 makes action 1's remaining prospects warm instead of cold. Action 4 is what makes action 2 _true_ rather than a claim. Action 5 protects the founder-hours that actions 2 and 4 consume. And the whole set only works because of action 1 — the refusal.

Use this as a working definition: **actions are coherent when removing one makes the others measurably harder.** Test it on your own list before you commit a quarter to it.

Rumelt has a name for the structure that makes this bite: a **chain-link system**, where the whole is limited by its weakest link, so improving any other link changes nothing until the weak link is fixed. Fuel-dealer adoption is a chain-link system — brilliant reconciliation reporting produces zero rupees if the owner never trusts you enough to let you migrate his ledger. It is also why §3's constraint discipline matters more than feature velocity.

### 2.3 The VSYST kernel, worked

Here is a real kernel for VSYST, written to be argued with rather than admired. §9 gives it to you as an editable draft; this section explains where each line comes from.

> **Diagnosis.** Indian fuel dealers do not lack software. They lack a _reason to change_ a paper-and-Tally process that has never visibly failed them — and the person who must decide to change it is a 45–65-year-old owner with low software trust, a real switching cost measured in ledger migration and staff retraining, and thin operating cash. So VSYST's binding constraint is **not features and not price. It is adoption friction and trust, and both are earned at district level, one dealer at a time, through people the owner already believes.**

That diagnosis is doing four things at once. It **names the obstacle** (no reason to change, not no product). It **names the decider** (the owner, not the manager, not the accountant). It **names the currency** (trust, not features). And it **locates the unit of work** (the district, not the country). Each of those is contestable, which is what makes it a diagnosis rather than a slogan — a competent competitor could rationally claim the constraint is price, or the customer side of the network, or OMC distribution, and behave completely differently as a result.

It is also grounded rather than invented. The evidence sits in the vault already: the owner buys on trust that flows through three paths — the dealer next door, the OMC territory manager, or the pump's chartered accountant — and "no amount of Google Ads closes this buyer without a human touch somewhere in the chain" ([[12_OWNER_ACQUISITION|owner acquisition]]). The substitute is not a competing product; it is paper plus Tally plus WhatsApp plus Excel, and roughly three-quarters of Indian SMEs already run business on WhatsApp ([[01_IDEA_VALIDATION|idea validation]]). And the category's own history says the fight is not on code: fifteen-plus regional vendors already sell petrol-pump software at ₹15,000–25,000 one-time, and none of them lost to a better feature list.

> **Guiding policy.** Win **district clusters completely** rather than India thinly — starting in Chhattisgarh, where a founder can drive to the customer and back in a day — by making the _first fourteen days_ of a dealer's life the product we optimise above all else, and by borrowing trust from the three channels the owner already believes (the dealer next door, the association office-bearer, the CA) instead of trying to manufacture it with marketing.
>
> **Which means we will not:** sell outside the chosen cluster even when an inbound lead arrives from Pune; build features requested by prospects who have not yet completed onboarding; compete on price against ₹15,000 one-time desktop licences; or spend money on brand marketing before referral density exists.

> **Coherent actions (the next two quarters).**
>
> 1. **Choose the cluster and publish the refusal.** Raipur + Durg divisions — 12 of Chhattisgarh's 33 districts, all inside a day's drive of the office. Everything outside goes on a waitlist with a written, dated reason.
> 2. **Make time-to-first-invoice the company's single obsessed-over number.** Founder-run white-glove onboarding including ledger migration, with a hard target measured in days. This is the chain-link's weakest link.
> 3. **Manufacture the first three reference dealers deliberately.** Pick them for _credibility with peers_, not for revenue; over-serve them; film each at his own pump in the local language.
> 4. **Buy trust wholesale through gatekeepers.** One district association office-bearer, one OMC territory manager, and two CA firms that serve pumps — each given something valuable before anything is asked ([[14-partnerships-ecosystem-and-founder-relationships|lesson 14]] runs this properly).
> 5. **Ship only onboarding-blocking work.** Every other request goes to the no-list with the dealer's name against it, so the no-list becomes the roadmap input rather than a graveyard ([[C25-product-bets-and-the-no-list|C25]]).

Now run the coherence test. Kill action 1 and actions 2–4 lose their density — the founder is driving to Bilaspur and Pune in the same week. Kill action 2 and action 3 produces reference dealers who are not actually live, which destroys action 4's credibility the first time the office-bearer asks a hard question. Kill action 5 and action 2 starves. **Removing any one makes the others harder — so the set is coherent.**

And note the **proximate objective**: not "become the category leader in Indian fuel-distribution software", which is a blue-sky objective nobody can act on this week, but "twelve districts, a measurable time-to-first-invoice, three filmable reference dealers, four gatekeepers." Close enough to be achievable; specific enough that failure is visible.

## 3. Doing the diagnosis — the actual method

A diagnosis is not produced by thinking harder. It is produced by a specific, unglamorous procedure. This section is the procedure.

### 3.2 Interviewing for evidence rather than for compliments

The failure mode is universal: you describe your product, the dealer is polite, you leave encouraged and learn nothing. Rob Fitzpatrick's _The Mom Test_ exists entirely because of this — "how to talk to customers and learn if your business is a good idea when everyone is lying to you" ([The Mom Test](https://www.momtestbook.com/)). The operating rules are three: **talk about their life, not your idea; ask about specifics in the past, not opinions about the future; and talk less, listen more.**

Steve Blank's version is older and blunter: the facts you need are not inside the building, so get out of it, and treat the plan as a set of hypotheses to be tested against customers rather than a document to be executed ([steveblank.com](https://steveblank.com/2010/03/04/perfection-by-subtraction-the-minimum-feature-set/)). The Indian-fuel-dealer twist is that "getting out of the building" is not optional here even in principle — this buyer, as VSYST's own research already concluded, "cannot be sold digitally alone."

| Question that produces a compliment                   | Question that produces a fact                                                           | Why the second one works                                                                           |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "Would you use an app that shows your credit ageing?" | "Show me how you knew, last month, which customer was overdue. Can I see the register?" | Past behaviour is observable; future intent is free to give away                                   |
| "Is ₹1,500 a month reasonable?"                       | "What did you last pay for software, when, how much, and who decided?"                  | Reveals real budget authority and the mental price anchor set by ₹15,000 one-time desktop licences |
| "Do you have problems with stock variance?"           | "What was the last shortage that cost you real money? What did you do that week?"       | Forces a specific incident, with a cost and a date                                                 |
| "Would your customers use the app?"                   | "How did your last five orders come in — call, WhatsApp, in person?"                    | Directly tests the two-sided assumption without asking anyone to predict                           |
| "Would you recommend us?"                             | "Who else should I show this to — and would you introduce me on WhatsApp right now?"    | An introduction is a costly signal; a compliment is free                                           |
| "What features do you need?"                          | "Walk me through yesterday, from opening to day-end summary"                            | Feature requests are solutions in disguise; the walkthrough gives you the underlying job           |

That last row is Clayton Christensen's point: customers "hire" a product to do a job, and the job — not the demographic and not the feature list — is what predicts the purchase ([Christensen, Hall, Dillon & Duncan, "Know Your Customers' Jobs to Be Done", HBR, September 2016](https://hbr.org/2016/09/know-your-customers-jobs-to-be-done)). The job a Raipur pump owner hires software for is almost certainly **"let me sleep without wondering whether today's cash matches today's litres, and let me not be the one who gets a notice"** — a job about anxiety and exposure, not about efficiency. Price the product against that job, not against a desktop licence.

Fifteen to twenty of these conversations, written up the same day, is the raw material for a diagnosis. Structure them with [[C09-icp-and-customer-discovery-guide|C09]] and keep the notes; §7's assumption register is built from them.

### 3.3 Naming the binding constraint

Eliyahu Goldratt's Theory of Constraints says a system's throughput is set by exactly one constraint at a time, and improving anything else is local efficiency that changes nothing. The five focusing steps are **Identify → Exploit → Subordinate → Elevate → Repeat** ([Umbrex — TOC five focusing steps](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/organization-frameworks/theory-of-constraints-five-focusing-steps/); [TOC Institute](https://www.tocinstitute.org/five-focusing-steps.html)).

The COO course applies this to the _operating system_ — where founder-hours are usually the constraint ([[02-how-a-coo-thinks|COO lesson 02]] §5). **The CEO applies the same instrument to the market**, which is a different line with a different bottleneck. Model the go-to-market as a production line and find the station where everything queues:

```
reach  →  meeting  →  demo  →  agreement to try  →  onboarding  →  FIRST INVOICE  →  habit  →  payment  →  renewal  →  referral
```

| Step            | The question                                                                      | VSYST's likely answer today (hypothesis, not fact)                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| --------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Identify**    | Which stage has the lowest throughput, with everything downstream idle behind it? | Not reach — the domain director can get meetings. Not demo — the product demos well. The queue almost certainly forms at **onboarding → first invoice**, where ledger migration, staff training and owner trust all have to clear at once |
| **Exploit**     | How do we get the most out of that stage as it is, before spending anything?      | Do it personally and completely: migrate the outstanding receivables yourself, sit at the pump for the first day-end, put the owner's own top five customers in before you leave                                                          |
| **Subordinate** | What does everything else give up to serve it?                                    | Feature work, marketing, partnerships, and any prospect outside the cluster all yield to onboarding capacity. This is what the guiding policy's refusals are _for_                                                                        |
| **Elevate**     | What investment raises its capacity?                                              | A written onboarding SOP the domain director can hand to a hired field rep; a data-import tool; a WhatsApp-first customer touchpoint so the dealer's customers need no install ([[11_EDUCATION_GAP\|the education gap]])                  |
| **Repeat**      | Where does the constraint move next?                                              | Probably to **trust at the top of the funnel** — you can onboard faster than you can find dealers who believe you. That is when gatekeepers and reference density become the whole job                                                    |

The discipline here is negative as much as positive. **Do not polish a non-constraint.** A beautiful analytics dashboard while dealers stall before their first invoice is efficient and useless.

### 3.4 The "what is the hardest part of this?" test

Rumelt's crux question, and the fastest diagnostic instrument in this lesson. Ask each director, separately and in writing: **"What is the hardest part of making this business work? Not the most urgent — the hardest."** Then compare the three answers.

- **If all three match**, you have a candidate crux and probably a diagnosis. Write it down before the agreement decays.
- **If they differ**, that is the most valuable finding of the quarter: your company does not agree on what business it is in, and every resource-allocation argument for the next year is really this argument in disguise. Resolve it before writing a plan.
- **If an answer is a task** ("the hardest part is finishing the billing module"), it is not the crux. Push: _and if that shipped tomorrow, what would still be hard?_ Repeat until the answer stops being work and starts being a property of the world.

Run this every quarter. A crux that has not moved in four quarters means you have not been attacking it.

### 3.5 Symptom versus constraint

The most common diagnostic error is treating the loudest symptom as the constraint. Symptoms are downstream and plural; the constraint is upstream and singular. Here is the discrimination table for VSYST, with the test that separates the two — because the point of naming a symptom is to find the test, not to argue about it.

| Symptom you will observe                | Tempting (wrong) constraint | Candidate real constraint                                                                                                                       | The test that separates them                                                                                                                                              |
| --------------------------------------- | --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "It's too expensive"                    | Price is too high           | Value is unproven, or cash genuinely is not there ([[10_AFFORDABILITY_PROBLEM\|WTP vs ATP]])                                                    | Offer three months free with the same onboarding effort. If they still don't reach a first invoice, it was never price                                                    |
| Dealers sign up and go quiet            | Product is missing features | Onboarding never completed — no ledger migrated, no staff trained                                                                               | Segment by whether a ledger was migrated. Compare 30-day activity of the two groups                                                                                       |
| "My accountant handles all that"        | Wrong buyer                 | The owner has delegated the _pain_, so the job you are selling against is the accountant's fee, not the owner's anxiety                         | Ask what the accountant is paid per month. If the software costs more than the fee, you are losing on arithmetic, not persuasion                                          |
| Customers never install the app         | Customer UX is bad          | The customer side should require zero install — WhatsApp/OTP only, dealer does the heavy lifting ([[11_EDUCATION_GAP\|the education gap]] §5.4) | Ship the zero-install path to five relations and compare order flow                                                                                                       |
| Long silences between meetings          | Sales follow-up is weak     | The owner has no forcing deadline; nothing in his year makes today the day                                                                      | Test against a real deadline — a GST/e-invoicing threshold crossing, an audit, a season                                                                                   |
| Feature requests in every meeting       | Product gap                 | A polite way of saying "not yet" — Rumelt's failure-to-face-the-challenge, in the customer's voice                                              | Ask: "If we built exactly that, would you sign this week?" Ask three times. Count the yeses that convert                                                                  |
| Regional competitors win on price       | Category is commoditised    | Buyer cannot distinguish, so defaults to the cheapest — a **positioning** failure, not a price failure                                          | Run a blind five-minute comparison with three dealers. If they cannot name a difference, fix the message before the price ([[C08-positioning-and-messaging-canvas\|C08]]) |
| Founder is the bottleneck in every deal | Need to hire salespeople    | At this stage founder-led selling is correct and hiring too early buries the signal ([[07-customers-markets-and-founder-led-sales\|lesson 07]]) | Not a strategy problem yet. Revisit after a repeatable script exists                                                                                                      |

Two warnings. **Five-whys can walk you off a cliff** — each "why" is a choice of branch, and with no data you will reliably choose the branch that flatters the product. Anchor every step to an observation. And **the constraint moves**: a diagnosis with no review date silently becomes doctrine. §8 fixes the date.

### 3.6 What a good diagnosis looks like

Six properties. Check yours against all six before it leaves the room.

1. **It names a mechanism, not a mood.** "Dealers are not tech-savvy" is a mood. "The owner cannot verify the software's claims without letting it hold his ledger, and letting it hold his ledger is the switching cost" is a mechanism.
2. **It is falsifiable.** You can say what evidence would prove it wrong.
3. **A competent competitor could disagree** — and would then behave differently.
4. **It simplifies.** One sentence, maybe two. A page-long diagnosis is an admission you have not found it.
5. **It implies action.** If nothing follows from it, it is an observation.
6. **It is uncomfortable.** A diagnosis that flatters everything the company already does is almost certainly wrong, because if the company were already aimed at the real obstacle you would not be stuck.

### 3.7 The one-week diagnosis sprint

A diagnosis that never gets a calendar slot never gets written. Here is the week, sized for a three-person company where two of the three also have to ship code. Run it once, then re-run the short version every quarter (§8 sets the date).

| Day          | Work                                                                                           | Who                          | Output that goes in the vault                                                                                                              |
| ------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Mon**      | Database day. Run the aggregations in §3.1 and write each query next to its number             | Technical CEO                | A one-page evidence sheet — no interpretation, only numbers and the queries that produced them                                             |
| **Tue**      | Field day. One full shift at a friendly pump, 6 AM to 2 PM, no laptop                          | Domain director              | A written narrative of the day, plus a list of every piece of paper touched and who touched it                                             |
| **Wed**      | The dead. Five calls to dealers who said no, or signed up and went silent                      | Whoever has the relationship | Five verbatim "reason for no" notes — their words, not your summary                                                                        |
| **Thu**      | Gatekeepers. One association office-bearer, one OMC territory manager, one CA who serves pumps | Domain director + CEO        | Three district maps in prose: who is respected, who is second-generation, which pumps matter                                               |
| **Fri (AM)** | The crux question, in writing, separately, before anyone speaks (§3.4)                         | All three directors          | Three sealed answers, opened together                                                                                                      |
| **Fri (PM)** | Write the diagnosis. One sentence. Then the guiding policy, then five actions                  | CEO holds the pen            | A filled [[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager\|C04]], dated, in `Strategy Kernel` in [vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx](toolkit/vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx) |

Two rules for the week. **Nobody demos the product** — the moment you demo, the conversation becomes about you and the evidence stops. And **write everything up the same day**; a field note written on Thursday about Tuesday is a memory of a memory, and the specific detail that turns out to matter is exactly the one that decays first.

The output of §3 is one sentence. Everything from here on is the machinery for turning that sentence into choices you can be held to.

## 4. Playing to Win's choice cascade

Rumelt gives you the _shape_ of a strategy. Lafley and Martin give you the _interrogation_. A.G. Lafley ran Procter & Gamble as CEO; Roger Martin was Dean of the Rotman School of Management at the University of Toronto ([Rotman faculty bio](https://www.rotman.utoronto.ca/FacultyAndResearch/Faculty/FacultyBios/Martin)) and wrote up the method the two of them used on P&G's brands as _Playing to Win: How Strategy Really Works_ (Harvard Business Review Press, 2013) ([rogerlmartin.com](https://rogerlmartin.com/lets-read/playing-to-win)). Their claim is narrow and useful: **strategy is an integrated set of choices that positions you to win, and there are exactly five choices.**

| #   | The question                              | What it really asks                                                                                                      | The failure if you dodge it                                                                   |
| --- | ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | **What is our winning aspiration?**       | What does winning look like, for whom, in a form we would recognise if it happened?                                      | "Be the best" — unfalsifiable, so no choice follows                                           |
| 2   | **Where will we play?**                   | Which segments, geographies, channels, price points, stages of the value chain — and, by omission, which not             | You play everywhere, which at three people means nowhere                                      |
| 3   | **How will we win?**                      | The specific way you create more value in that arena than anyone else — cost, differentiation, or a structural asymmetry | "Better product" — which is a hope that the buyer evaluates products, and this buyer does not |
| 4   | **What capabilities must be in place?**   | The few reinforcing activities you must be distinctively good at                                                         | You list every function and are distinctively good at none                                    |
| 5   | **What management systems are required?** | The measures, cadences, systems and structures that build and sustain those capabilities                                 | The strategy stays a document; nothing in the week changes                                    |

The single most misread thing about the cascade is its arrows. It is drawn top-to-bottom, but it is not a sequence — it is a set of simultaneous equations. A where-to-play choice you cannot win in is not a choice, it is a wish; a how-to-win that needs a capability you cannot build is a fantasy; and a capability with no management system behind it decays inside two quarters. Martin's own warning is that planning processes gravitate to the comfortable boxes — budgets and capabilities — precisely because the uncomfortable boxes, two and three, force you to say where you will _not_ play and admit you might lose ([Martin, "The Big Lie of Strategic Planning", _HBR_, January–February 2014](https://hbr.org/2014/01/the-big-lie-of-strategic-planning); [Martin, "A Plan Is Not a Strategy", HBR video](https://hbr.org/video/6280466648001/a-plan-is-not-a-strategy)).

The rest of this section runs the cascade for DZZLO OMS. Treat the answers as a **draft to be argued with**, not as settled — §9 hands the whole thing to you as an editable block.

### 4.1 Winning aspiration — and how it differs from a target

A target is a number you want. An aspiration is a description of a _state of the world_ in which you have won, specific enough that a stranger could look at the world in three years and tell you whether it happened.

| Statement                                                                                                                                            | Which is it? | Why                                                                                                             |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "₹1 crore ARR by FY28"                                                                                                                               | Target       | Says nothing about who, where, or why they chose you. §6 shows it is also a much bigger number than it sounds   |
| "Be India's leading fuel-distribution platform"                                                                                                      | Slogan       | No arena, no test, no refusal                                                                                   |
| "In Raipur and Durg divisions, a new fuel dealer who wants to stop running his credit book on paper asks two people what to use, and both say DZZLO" | Aspiration   | Names the arena, names the mechanism (word of mouth between dealers), and is checkable by asking twenty dealers |

VSYST's draft aspiration, in the third form:

> **By the end of FY28, in the twelve districts of Raipur and Durg divisions, DZZLO is the default answer when one fuel dealer asks another what he uses to run his order book, credit ledger and invoicing — and enough of those dealers pay for it that the company covers its own costs without outside capital.**

Two things that aspiration deliberately contains. It names **referral density**, not revenue, as the definition of winning — because in a trust market referral density is the leading indicator and revenue is the lagging one. And it names **self-funding**, not scale, because VSYST is bootstrapped and the honest winning condition for a bootstrapped company is not growth, it is the right to keep making its own decisions ([[08-capital-runway-and-fundraising|lesson 08]] is where that choice gets argued properly).

### 4.2 Where to play — three axes, three refusals

Where-to-play is not one question. For VSYST it is three: **which segment**, **which geography**, **which buyer inside the firm**. Each has to be answered with a refusal attached.

#### Axis 3 — Buyer

Inside a dealer firm there are four people who could plausibly be "the customer", and choosing wrong is the most expensive mistake on this axis.

The refusal: **the product is not sold to the manager, and it is not priced for the customer side.** Both of those are attractive, both are wrong for the beachhead, and both would quietly change the product if allowed to.

### 4.3 How to win — and "what would have to be true"

Segments and geography are the arena. How-to-win is the claim that you will beat everyone else _in that arena_, and it must be a claim about a structural asymmetry, not about effort.

There are only three honest candidates for a three-person, pre-revenue company in this market.

| Candidate how-to-win                                           | The claim                                                                                                                                                                               | Why it fails, or why it holds                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| -------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Win on features**                                            | We build a better product than the fifteen regional pump-software vendors                                                                                                               | **Fails.** Fifteen products are already listed in this category at ₹3,990–₹19,840 ([SoftwareSuggest — petrol pump software](https://www.softwaresuggest.com/petrol-pump-software)) and none of them lost to a feature list. The buyer cannot evaluate features before he trusts you, and after he trusts you he does not need to                                          |
| **Win on price**                                               | Undercut the incumbents                                                                                                                                                                 | **Fails, twice.** The incumbent anchor is a one-time licence, so any subscription looks infinitely expensive on a spreadsheet; and the true substitute — paper plus the existing munim — is priced at zero marginal rupees. You cannot underprice zero                                                                                                                    |
| **Win on trust density and time-to-value inside one district** | In twelve districts we will be the vendor whose customer you already know personally, and the one who gets a dealer to his first correct invoice faster than anyone else will bother to | **Holds — conditionally.** It uses two things VSYST actually has (a domain director with standing in this trade, and founders who will do unscalable onboarding) and it attacks the constraint §3.3 identified. It is also _structurally_ hard to copy: a national competitor cannot afford district-level hand-holding, and a local competitor cannot afford the product |

That third row is the how-to-win. Now apply Martin's discipline: instead of arguing about whether it is right, ask **what would have to be true for it to work** — and then test the shakiest ones. This reframe is the practical heart of _Playing to Win_, and it is what turns a strategy argument from a contest of confidence into a research agenda.

| What would have to be true                                                                                    | How shaky is it?                                                                                                          | The cheapest test                                                                 | Status today                               |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |

| Twelve districts contain enough dealer GSTINs, at a price they will pay, to cover VSYST's costs               | High — §6 says the honest answer is "probably not on subscription alone"                                                  | Run §6's arithmetic with your own numbers, then run it against the payments layer | **Partially falsified already** — see §6.6 |
| The onboarding motion can be taught to somebody who is not a founder                                          | Unknown, and unknowable until it has been done a few times                                                                | Write the SOP after the third onboarding, hand it to the third director, watch    | Not yet possible                           |
| No national player finds twelve districts of Chhattisgarh worth the ground game                               | Medium — true today; a funded competitor changes it                                                                       | Watch OMC territory managers for who else is calling on their dealers             | Monitored, not tested                      |

Notice that two of the five are not really about the customer at all — they are about whether VSYST can do the thing it says it will do. That is normal for a company this size and it is why §7 exists.

### 4.4 Capabilities — the few, not the many

Capabilities in the cascade are not "things we are good at". They are **the small set of reinforcing activities without which the how-to-win is impossible.** If a capability could be removed and the strategy still work, it is a competence, not a capability.

| Capability                                                                                                      | Why the how-to-win needs it                                                                                                          | Have it?                                                                                            | How it gets built                                                                                                                                                                      |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **District trust access** — being introduced rather than arriving                                               | The entire trust thesis; without it the motion is cold calling, which this buyer does not answer                                     | **Yes, uniquely** — the domain director. Currently a person-shaped dependency, not a company asset  | Extraction (§3.1) plus deliberate gatekeeper relationships ([[14-partnerships-ecosystem-and-founder-relationships\|lesson 14]], [[C26-partnership-evaluation-and-mou-checklist\|C26]]) |
| **Ruthless onboarding execution** — ledger migration, staff training, first day-end, done by hand               | This is the constraint. It is also the only part of the experience a competitor cannot fake in a demo                                | Partly — done, never measured                                                                       | Measure it, then SOP it ([[T08-sop-template\|T08]] in the COO course)                                                                                                                  |
| **Fuel-native compliance correctness** — three-tier invoicing, TCS past the turnover threshold, the rate window | It is the reason a generic accounting package is not a substitute; get it wrong once and the trust thesis dies with the first notice | Yes — it is what the product already is                                                             | Keep it; treat correctness bugs as the highest-severity class                                                                                                                          |
| **Vernacular, low-literacy product surfaces**                                                                   | The manager and the driver are the daily users, and neither reads English UI                                                         | Partly                                                                                              | Product work with a named owner ([[13-product-and-technology-leadership-for-a-technical-ceo\|lesson 13]])                                                                              |
| **Web-only billing and server-side gating**                                                                     | Protects the margin that funds white-glove onboarding — the store commission would otherwise eat the difference                      | Designed, not built ([[app-store-economics/08-dzzlo-subscription-strategy\|subscription strategy]]) | Build order is in that doc; do not re-litigate                                                                                                                                         |

What is deliberately **not** on that list: brand, enterprise sales, partnerships with OMCs at head-office level, and a self-serve funnel. All are good things. None of them is load-bearing for this how-to-win, and a three-person company that builds a non-load-bearing capability has spent the only resource it cannot buy.

| **Where to play**      | Petrol-pump dealers (not lubricant distributors, not bulk-diesel operators) · 12 districts of Raipur + Durg divisions (not the rest of Chhattisgarh, not India) · sold to the **owner** with the second-generation son as champion (not to the manager, not to the customer side) | No second segment, no thirteenth district, no manager-led deal, for two quarters                                       |
| **How to win**         | Trust density and time-to-first-invoice inside one district cluster: the vendor whose customer you already know, who gets you to a correct invoice faster than anyone will bother to                                                                                              | We will not win on features, and we will not win on price                                                              |
| **Capabilities**       | District trust access · ruthless hand-run onboarding · fuel-native compliance correctness · vernacular surfaces · web-only billing                                                                                                                                                | No brand marketing, no enterprise motion, no self-serve funnel                                                         |
| **Management systems** | The COO's operating system, unchanged — scorecard, weekly cadence, onboarding SOP, DoA, quarterly rhythm — with **time-to-first-invoice** installed as this year's constraint metric                                                                                              | The CEO does not build a parallel reporting system; there is one scorecard                                             |

Read the five rows together and check the interlock, the same test §2.2 applied to actions. Row 2's twelve-district refusal is what makes row 3's hand-run onboarding affordable; row 3 is what makes row 4's trust access convert into paying dealers instead of goodwill; row 5's single constraint metric is what stops row 3 from silently degrading the moment the founders get busy. **Pull any row and the others get harder.** That is the test.

## 5. Market structure and where power sits

You can execute a good strategy inside a bad industry and still make no money. Michael Porter's contribution — first published in 1979 and restated with three decades of evidence in 2008 — is that industry profitability is not luck and not the average competence of the firms in it; it is the outcome of **five forces that determine who captures the value the industry creates** ([Porter, "How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy", _HBR_, March 1979](https://hbr.org/1979/03/how-competitive-forces-shape-strategy); [Porter, "The Five Competitive Forces That Shape Strategy", _HBR_, January 2008](https://hbr.org/2008/01/the-five-competitive-forces-that-shape-strategy)).

Two things founders get wrong about five forces, both worth naming before the table.

**It is an analysis of an industry, not of your competitors.** The output is not a list of rivals; it is a judgment about where the profit pool sits and who is standing on it. If you finish a five-forces exercise with a competitor matrix, you did the wrong exercise — competitor-by-competitor comparison belongs in [[C06-market-map-sizing-and-segments|C06]] and moat analysis belongs in [[06-strategy-ii-moats-positioning-and-the-business-model|lesson 06]].

**Structure is not destiny, but it sets the odds.** Porter's own point in the 2008 restatement is that a firm can _shape_ structure as well as position within it. A vertical SaaS company that gets its customers' data into a shared rail is trying to change force three; a company that builds an integration nobody else has is trying to change force four.

### 5.3 What the structure actually says

Read the six forces together and three conclusions fall out, none of them comfortable.

1. **The industry's profit pool for dealer-side subscription software is thin, and it is thin structurally** — low entry barriers, a free substitute, and a price-anchored buyer. That is not a fixable condition; it is the shape of the arena. §6 puts a number on it.
2. **Therefore the strategy cannot be "get a lot of subscription customers" alone.** It has to be either (a) win a district so completely that the trust barrier becomes real and pricing power follows, or (b) move to a layer where power sits differently — money movement — or, most likely, (a) in service of (b).
3. **The most dangerous single event in this structure is an OMC shipping a free dealer app.** It would not have to be good. Write that on the risk register now ([[T15-risk-register|T15]] in the COO course; [[18-risk-crisis-and-the-hard-things|lesson 18]] for the CEO's handling), and put a named early-warning signal against it in §7's assumption table.

None of these says "do not do it". They say: **do not expect the arena to be generous, and build the strategy so that being right about the district matters more than being right about the product.**

## 6. Sizing the prize

Market sizing goes wrong in exactly two ways, and founders alternate between them. The first is the **top-down fantasy**: "the Indian lubricants market is USD 4.9 billion ([IMARC](https://www.imarcgroup.com/india-lubricants-market)); if we take 1% of that…" — a sentence that contains no customer, no price and no unit, and that Bill Gurley demolished in the canonical piece on the subject, where he showed that a market size is not a fact you look up but **an argument you construct**, and that the argument usually smuggles in its conclusion ([Gurley, "How to Miss By a Mile: An Alternative Look at Uber's Potential Market Size", _Above the Crowd_, July 2014](https://abovethecrowd.com/2014/07/11/how-to-miss-by-a-mile-an-alternative-look-at-ubers-potential-market-size/)). The second is the **refusal to size at all** — "we'll just find customers and see" — which is how a company discovers in year three that the entire arena it chose is worth less than one competent person's salary.

The purpose of this section is neither optimism nor pessimism. It is to answer a single operational question: **at the price we have decided to charge, how many customers must say yes before this company covers its own costs — and does the chosen arena contain that many?** If the answer is no, the strategy is falsified by arithmetic before a single dealer is called, and it is far cheaper to find that out here.

### 6.1 The three numbers, and what each one is actually for

| Number                                   | Definition used in this course                                                                                                                                 | What it is for                                                                                                    | What it is _not_ for                                                 |
| ---------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **TAM** — total addressable market       | Every entity in India that could, in principle, become a paying tenant of a fuel-distribution OMS, times the annual price                                      | Deciding whether the _category_ is big enough to be worth a decade. A sanity ceiling                              | Forecasting. Nobody ever gets TAM                                    |
| **SAM** — serviceable addressable market | The subset TAM restricted to what this product, in its current form, sold through the channel this company actually has, could serve — for VSYST, Chhattisgarh | Deciding whether the **where-to-play** choice (§4.2) is survivable                                                | Pitching. A SAM quoted without its restrictions is a TAM in disguise |
| **SOM** — serviceable obtainable market  | What a company of _this_ size, with _this_ headcount, in _this_ number of quarters, can realistically win                                                      | Setting the plan, the hiring, and the break-even test. **This is the only one that touches a decision this year** | Impressing anyone. SOM is always embarrassingly small, and should be |

Two rules make this honest. **Build bottom-up, never top-down** — start from a countable unit (an outlet, a GSTIN, a distributor), multiply by a price you have actually quoted to a human, and stop. And **write the source next to every input**, because a sizing model is only as good as its worst-sourced cell, and six months later nobody remembers which cells were counted and which were felt.

### 6.3 From outlets to GSTINs — the conversion nobody does

VSYST does not sell outlets. It sells **subscriptions to a GSTIN**, because that is the billing unit the company has already decided on: every company — dealer or customer, including each sister company — is its own subscription per GSTIN, users unlimited and free ([[app-store-economics/08-dzzlo-subscription-strategy|subscription strategy]]). So the countable unit above has to be converted, and the conversion is where most sizing models silently inflate by 30–60%.

| Adjustment                                                                                                                      | Direction   | Working assumption                 | Why, and how to replace the assumption with a fact                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------- | ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **COCO outlets** — company-owned, company-operated. There is no dealer, so there is no tenant                                   | Reduces     | **−6%**                            | The OMCs run some outlets directly and appoint service providers rather than dealers ([IndianOil, COCO divestment and service provider](https://iocl.com/coco-divestment-and-service-provider); [BPCL COCO](https://www.bharatpetroleum.in/bharat-petroleum-for/business-associates/coco)). **VERIFY LIVE** — no official all-India COCO percentage was findable for this lesson; ask an OMC divisional office for the split in your districts and replace this cell |
| **Multi-outlet dealers** — one firm, one GSTIN, several pumps                                                                   | Reduces     | **÷ 1.3 outlets per dealer GSTIN** | A dealer who runs three pumps under one firm pays once. **VERIFY LIVE** — this is the single most leverage-heavy assumption in the whole model, and it is _free_ to check: count distinct firm names against outlets in your own twelve districts using the OMC dealer list or the association register                                                                                                                                                              |
| **Sister companies** — the same family running a pump firm, a transport firm and a lubricant agency, each separately registered | _Increases_ | Not counted                        | Deliberately excluded, because counting it would flatter the model. In practice it is the most likely source of upside and the easiest expansion sale you will ever make                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| **The customer side** — fleets, factories, hospitals                                                                            | Zero        | Not counted, ever                  | They ride free on the dealer's tenant by design. They are a network asset, not a revenue line                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |

Two of those four are honest reductions, one is deliberate conservatism, and one is unmeasured. **That distribution — mostly conservative, with the biggest lever explicitly flagged — is what a defensible model looks like.** A model where every adjustment happens to point upward is not a model.

Applying the two reductions: **103,023 × 0.94 ÷ 1.3 ≈ 74,500 dealer GSTINs** in India, and **2,587 × 0.94 ÷ 1.3 ≈ 1,870** in Chhattisgarh.

For scale, India had **1,67,14,646 active GST taxpayers** as at 30 June 2026 ([GSTN nine-year statistical report, via A2Z Taxcorp](https://a2ztaxcorp.net/nine-years-of-gst-taxpayer-base-crosses-1-67-crore-as-indias-digital-indirect-tax-ecosystem-scales-new-milestones-report-highlights-expansion-of-taxpayer-base-192-27-crore-returns-filed-an/); state-wise active-taxpayer files are published on the [GST portal's statistics page](https://www.gst.gov.in/download/gststatistics) — **VERIFY LIVE**, and download the Chhattisgarh row yourself). Fuel-distribution dealer GSTINs are therefore about **0.45%** of all registered businesses in India. That is the correct emotional calibration for this exercise: **you have chosen a very narrow vertical, on purpose, and narrowness is the strategy** — but it means the ceiling is set early and you should know where it is.

### 6.4 The price side — what one GSTIN can pay

The pricing metric is decided; the price points are not. The current tier sheet is a **placeholder** pending a real floor calculation — Starter ₹599, Growth ₹1,799 (hero), Pro ₹4,999 (anchor), Enterprise on quote, per GSTIN per month, ex-GST ([[10-pricing-metric-decision|the pricing-metric decision]]). **VERIFY LIVE** before any of these reach a customer's ear.

What constrains them is not willingness to pay but **ability to pay**. A mid-volume pump selling 200 KL/month grosses ₹1.5–2 crore of topline and takes home only **₹1.5–4 lakh of operating profit**, on a dealer commission of roughly ₹1–3 per litre ([[10_AFFORDABILITY_PROBLEM|the affordability problem]]). The competitive anchors sit low and are all verifiable: TallyPrime Silver at **₹750/month** rental or ₹22,500 lifetime plus GST ([Tally Solutions](https://tallysolutions.com/buy-tally/)), Zoho Books India from **₹749**/organisation/month billed annually ([Zoho Books India](https://www.zoho.com/in/books/pricing/)), Vyapar from about **₹4,399/year** ([Vyapar pricing](https://vyaparapp.in/pricing)), and fifteen listed petrol-pump packages starting at **₹3,990–₹19,840** ([SoftwareSuggest](https://www.softwaresuggest.com/petrol-pump-software)). **VERIFY LIVE** — all four vendors reprice without notice.

So model three price points, not one, and label them:

| Scenario | Blended realised ₹/GSTIN/month (ex-GST) | The story that produces it                                                                   |
| -------- | --------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Low**  | **₹600**                                | Almost everyone lands on Starter; discounting to close; annual prepay at a deep discount     |
| **Base** | **₹1,200**                              | A realistic mix — roughly half Starter, half Growth, a handful of Pro — after discounts      |
| **High** | **₹1,800**                              | The hero tier actually holds, because onboarding proved value before the price was discussed |

Use **Base** everywhere below. Quote **Low** to yourself when planning runway, and **High** only when you have signed invoices to prove it.

### 6.5 The arithmetic — TAM, SAM, SOM, and the formula

The formula, so it can be re-run rather than re-argued:

```
billable_GSTINs = (outlets × (1 − coco_share) ÷ outlets_per_GSTIN)
                  + lpg_distributors + sko_ldo_dealerships + other_segments

annual_revenue  = billable_GSTINs × penetration × blended_price_per_month × 12
```

Five inputs, every one of them a cell you can change: `outlets`, `coco_share`, `outlets_per_GSTIN`, `blended_price_per_month`, `penetration`. That is the whole model. It lives in the **`Market Map`** tab of [vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx](toolkit/vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx), with the source URL and as-on date in the cell next to each input, and it is filled by [[C06-market-map-sizing-and-segments|C06]].

| **20%** — a genuinely dominant position in a district cluster | **187**       | ₹13.47 lakh           | **₹26.94 lakh** | ₹40.40 lakh |

Read that table slowly, because it contains the most important sentence in this lesson.

**Ten percent of the chosen arena — a real, hard-won, two-year outcome that most vertical-SaaS companies never reach — is ₹13.5 lakh a year.** That is roughly ₹1.1 lakh a month, from ninety-four dealers who each had to be visited, trusted, onboarded by hand and supported. It does not pay three founders. Depending on how you cost the company, it may not pay one.

Turn the arithmetic around and it becomes a strategy test rather than a disappointment. If VSYST's all-in cash cost is **₹1.5–2 lakh a month** — the realistic bootstrapped number for three founder-directors drawing minimal salary, with AWS, WhatsApp conversation costs, SMS/OTP, gateway fees, travel, statutory filings and a CA on retainer ([[C13-runway-burn-and-scenario-planner|C13]] does this properly; the illustrative figures here are not VSYST's audited numbers) — then break-even on subscription alone requires:

| Annual cash cost            | @ ₹600/mo                          | @ ₹1,200/mo     | @ ₹1,800/mo     |
| --------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | --------------- | --------------- |
| **₹18 lakh** (₹1.5 L/month) | 250 GSTINs — **26.7%** of the pool | 125 — **13.4%** | 83 — **8.9%**   |
| **₹24 lakh** (₹2.0 L/month) | 333 — **35.6%**                    | 167 — **17.8%** | 111 — **11.9%** |
| **₹36 lakh** (₹3.0 L/month) | 500 — **53.5%**                    | 250 — **26.7%** | 167 — **17.8%** |

This is the promised answer to the third row of §4.3's "what would have to be true" table, and it is a **partial falsification**, stated arithmetically:

> **Twelve districts of Chhattisgarh, on subscription revenue alone, at the Base price, require somewhere between 13% and 27% penetration of every dealer GSTIN in the cluster for VSYST to cover its own costs.** That is not impossible — it is exactly what "win a district cluster completely" means — but it is a much harder claim than "sign up some dealers", it takes years rather than quarters, and it leaves no room at all for hiring.

Three responses are available, and a CEO has to pick, not hope:

1. **Hold price, widen later.** Keep the twelve-district refusal, get to 15–20% penetration, and only then extend the same motion to Bilaspur division — where another ~700 outlets sit — using an SOP rather than a founder. This is the guiding policy as written, and it says the company stays small and slow for two to three years.
2. **Raise realised price.** Move the blend from ₹1,200 towards ₹1,800 by proving value inside fourteen days so the price conversation happens _after_ the first correct invoice, not before. Cheapest lever available; entirely a function of onboarding (§3.3's constraint again).
3. **Add a second revenue layer that is not the dealer's subscription.** Which is §6.6.

None of these changes the diagnosis. All of them change the plan. **That is what a market-sizing exercise is for.**

## 7. Strategy under uncertainty

Everything from §2 to §6 was written as though you knew things. You do not. VSYST is pre-revenue, has never renewed a customer, has never measured its own onboarding, and has never watched a dealer decide _not_ to pay in month thirteen. The kernel in §2.3 and the cascade in §4.6 are not conclusions. They are **a set of bets, stated clearly enough to be settled** — and the difference between a good strategy and a confident one is entirely whether you know which is which.

So name the trap first. **The trap is the plan that reads like a forecast.** It has months across the top, dealer counts and rupees down the side, and a total in the bottom-right corner that somebody will eventually be held to. It contains no marker distinguishing the cells that came from a bank statement from the cells that came from a hope, and after a fortnight nobody can tell them apart. Then the plan gets defended, because defending it is cheaper than admitting which half was invented — and a company that is defending its plan has stopped learning from the market and started arguing with it.

Everything in this section exists to prevent that specific outcome.

### 7.1 Two kinds of not-knowing, and only one of them yields to a spreadsheet

Frank Knight drew the line that still matters, in 1921: **risk** is not-knowing where the odds are knowable, and **uncertainty** is not-knowing where they are not ([Knightian uncertainty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knightian_uncertainty)). Insurance exists for the first. Entrepreneurial profit, in Knight's argument, exists precisely because of the second — you get paid for bearing what cannot be priced.

|                                | **Risk**                                                                                                   | **Uncertainty**                                                                                                                                                         |
| ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| What you have                  | A distribution, or enough history to build one                                                             | A hypothesis and no history                                                                                                                                             |
| The right instrument           | Expected value, scenarios, a discount rate                                                                 | An experiment, a small commitment, an option                                                                                                                            |
| What a bigger spreadsheet does | Genuinely improves the answer                                                                              | Adds decimal places to a guess and increases confidence without increasing knowledge                                                                                    |
| VSYST examples                 | AWS cost per tenant next quarter; GST filing dates; the cash in the current account; the PPAC outlet count | Whether a dealer renews in month 13; whether referral travels inside a district; whether an OMC ships a free app; whether an NBFC will ever underwrite from your ledger |
| Failure mode                   | Under-modelling — not doing the arithmetic §6 does                                                         | **Over-modelling** — doing §6's arithmetic to four significant figures on inputs that are pure assertion                                                                |

Look at the last row honestly. §6 built a five-input model and ran it across twelve price-and-penetration combinations. That was worth doing — it produced a _partial falsification_ nobody could have argued their way to. But three of its five inputs (`coco_share`, `outlets_per_GSTIN`, `penetration`) are assumptions dressed as parameters, and the model's precision is entirely borrowed from the two inputs that are real. **The number of decimal places in an output is set by the worst input, not the best one.** Every founder knows this about engineering and forgets it about planning.

The practical consequence: a pre-revenue company is operating almost entirely in the right-hand column, and therefore **the primary output of planning is not a forecast. It is a list of things to go and find out, ranked by how much they would cost you if wrong.**

### 7.2 Discovery-driven planning — the plan as a learning instrument

Rita Gunther McGrath and Ian MacMillan named the alternative in 1995: when an initiative's ratio of assumptions to knowledge is very high, **the purpose of planning shifts from predicting the outcome to converting assumptions into knowledge as cheaply and as fast as possible** ([McGrath & MacMillan, "Discovery-Driven Planning", _HBR_, July–August 1995](https://hbr.org/1995/07/discovery-driven-planning); [HBR — A Refresher on Discovery-Driven Planning, 2017](https://hbr.org/2017/02/a-refresher-on-discovery-driven-planning); [overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery-driven_planning)). It is the direct ancestor of what later became customer development and lean startup ([Steve Blank — What's a startup? First principles](https://steveblank.com/2010/01/25/whats-a-startup-first-principles/), 2010; [The Lean Startup — principles](https://theleanstartup.com/principles)) — and it is more useful to a bootstrapped Indian company than either, because it never assumes you have money to burn on iteration.

Its four documents, and what each one becomes at VSYST:

| Discovery-driven document      | The question it answers                                                                                          | What conventional planning does instead                        | The VSYST version                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Reverse income statement**   | Start from the profit required, work backwards to the revenue required, then to the costs the model is _allowed_ | Starts from revenue assumptions and lets costs follow          | §6.5's break-even table, run backwards: cash cost ₹1.5–2 L/month → **125–167 paying GSTINs at ₹1,200 blended** → the whole plan must fit inside the cost of acquiring and serving 125 dealers       |
| **Pro forma operations specs** | What physically has to happen, per unit, for that model to be real?                                              | Skipped entirely, or replaced by a headcount plan              | Dealers onboarded per founder-week · support tickets per tenant per month · founder-hours per closed deal · kilometres driven per onboarding. All four are countable; **none is currently counted** |
| **Key assumptions checklist**  | Which beliefs is this standing on, ranked by shakiness rather than by comfort?                                   | Buries assumptions in cell formulas where nobody re-reads them | §7.5's register, and section (c) of [[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager\|C04]]                                                                                                                          |
| **Milestone planning chart**   | What is the next checkpoint, which assumption does it settle, and what are we allowed to spend before it?        | Sets deadlines by calendar and funds the whole plan up front   | §7.6's tranches                                                                                                                                                                                     |

The discipline that makes it work is the third document, and it is the one everybody skips because it is the only one that is embarrassing to write.

**The reverse income statement, worked, because it takes four lines.** Required annual cash cost, ₹18–24 lakh (**VERIFY LIVE** — this is illustrative; [[C13-runway-burn-and-scenario-planner\|C13]] owns the real number). At the Base blended price of ₹1,200 ex-GST per GSTIN per month, that is **125–167 paying dealers**. If a founder can onboard, at absolute best, two dealers a week while also building the product and running the company, 125 dealers is **roughly sixty founder-weeks of onboarding** — more than a year of one person's entire capacity, with nothing left for selling, support or code. That single line is more informative than the whole of §6, and it was free. **It says the plan is not constrained by the market. It is constrained by founder-weeks** — which is exactly what the COO course says about operating systems ([[02-how-a-coo-thinks\|COO lesson 02]] §5), arriving from the opposite direction.

### 7.3 The assumption-to-knowledge ratio

McGrath's ratio is usually quoted as a metaphor. Treat it as an instrument instead, because it takes twenty minutes and it changes what you do.

**The method.** Take the kernel and the cascade — the diagnosis sentence, the guiding policy, its refusals, the five actions, the five cascade rows. Break them into the individual load-bearing beliefs each one rests on. Then mark every belief with exactly one letter:

```
K  KNOWN         We have direct evidence we generated ourselves.
                 A bank statement, a database query, a signed document,
                 a bill, a government file we downloaded.

A  ASSUMED       We believe it, and the belief traces to something —
                 someone else's research, a domain director's experience,
                 an analogy from another market. Not our evidence.

U  UNTESTED      We have asserted it. There is no evidence at all,
                 ours or anyone's, and we would not know how to get any
                 without changing what we do.
```

Then count. The rule of thumb the number is for: **while U + A dominates, every rupee should buy information rather than capacity.** Hiring, marketing spend, a second state, a salesperson — all of them convert cash into capacity, and capacity built on an untested belief is the most expensive thing a small company can own, because you cannot un-hire cheaply and you cannot un-open a territory at all.

Here is VSYST's kernel, scored honestly. This is the table that should make the room uncomfortable.

What matters is what follows from it: **at this ratio, the plan's job is not to allocate capacity, it is to buy letters.** Every one of the five "U" rows can be moved to "A" or "K" for the price of conversations, a stopwatch and a database query. None of them requires money. Several of them have been available for months.

Run the score again at day 90 and put both versions side by side. **The trend in the ratio is a better health indicator for a pre-revenue company than any revenue number**, because revenue at n = 3 tells you almost nothing and the ratio tells you whether the company is learning or merely working. Put it on the `Strategy Kernel` tab of [vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx](toolkit/vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx) as a single cell: `K / (K+A+U)`.

### 7.4 The smallest experiment that could falsify the strategy

Karl Popper's demarcation rule is the one idea from philosophy of science a founder actually needs: a claim is scientific only if it forbids something — only if some observable outcome would count as refuting it ([falsifiability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falsifiability)). **A strategy nobody can imagine being wrong is not bold; it is empty.** §1.2's fourth test asked whether you would bet money on being wrong. This is the mechanism that turns that question into a calendar entry.

Four rules for an experiment worth the week it costs:

1. **Write the failing result first.** Before you run anything, complete this sentence: _"If we see ***, we will stop doing *** and do \_\_\_ instead."_ If you cannot complete it, do not run the experiment — you have designed a demo, not a test. This is the single highest-yield discipline in the section and it costs one sentence.
2. **Make it cheap enough to survive being wrong.** The right size is the smallest thing that could still produce the failing result. Not the smallest thing that could produce a positive one — that is a different and much easier experiment, and it is the one founders build.
3. **Do not let the person who wants the answer run the test alone.** At VSYST that is structurally hard, because there are three of you and you are related. The workable substitute: the director who most wants the answer writes the prediction down _and_ the number, before running it, and one of the other two holds the paper ([[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log\|C29]] exists for exactly this; [[02-how-a-ceo-thinks\|lesson 02]] §5 runs the mechanism).
4. **Pre-commit the sample and the stopping rule.** "Fifteen dealers, and we stop at fifteen whatever we have heard" beats "we'll keep asking until it's clear", which always resolves in favour of the answer that was wanted.

**The confirmation trap, in its VSYST-specific form.** Three founders drive to a district, spend a warm evening with a friendly dealer who is a family acquaintance, and come home certain. That is not evidence about the market; it is evidence about the acquaintance. The optimism is structural rather than personal — teams systematically underweight the base rate of comparable ventures and overweight their own case ([Lovallo & Kahneman, HBR 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12858711/); [planning fallacy](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/planning-fallacy)) — and the correction is not more enthusiasm-resistance, it is **sampling discipline**: pick the dealers _before_ you leave, include at least three who have no connection to any of you, and include one who already bought a competitor's software.

The ladder of experiment costs, cheapest first. Always run the highest row that could still falsify the claim:

| Rung                       | What it is                                                      | Typical cost at VSYST    | What it can and cannot settle                                                                                                            |
| -------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **1. Query your own data** | A Mongo aggregation over what tenants already did               | An hour                  | Settles behavioural facts you already own and have never looked at (§3.1). Cannot settle anything about people who are not yet customers |
| **2. Ask, structured**     | 15 dealers, same questions, recorded, non-leading (§3.2)        | Three days, fuel money   | Settles _what happened_ — who told them about the last tool they bought. Cannot settle what they will do                                 |
| **3. Observe**             | Sit at a pump through a day-end and a month-end and watch       | Two days                 | Settles where time actually goes and what breaks. The highest-yield rung, and the one founders skip because it feels passive             |
| **4. Concierge**           | Do the thing manually for one dealer without building anything  | A week                   | Settles whether the value is real before any code exists                                                                                 |
| **5. Paid pilot**          | A signed letter, a real invoice, a grandfathered founding price | Weeks, plus the discount | The only rung that settles willingness to pay. Everything below it settles interest, which is free and worth nothing                     |
| **6. Build and ship**      | Engineering                                                     | Months                   | Settles almost nothing that rungs 1–5 could not have settled sooner and cheaper, which is why it is at the bottom                        |

Read that ladder against how the last six months were actually spent. For most technical founder-CEOs the honest answer is that rung 6 got the time and rungs 2–5 got the intention — and the tell is a beautiful product with one K in fifteen.

### 7.5 VSYST's three biggest unfalsified assumptions

Three, not ten. A register with ten rows gets read once; a register with three gets argued about. These are the three where **being wrong costs a year**, and all three are currently marked **U** — no evidence at all, VSYST's or anyone else's.

|                                                   | **A1 — Referral travels**                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        | **A2 — Onboarding is fast and repeatable**                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        | **A3 — Recurring payment survives month 13**                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Stated so it could be false**                   | A fuel dealer's choice of business software is materially influenced by another dealer in his district whom he knows personally, and that influence is strong enough that saturating a district lowers the cost of the next sale                                 | A founder can take a signed dealer from agreement to first correct invoice in a small, predictable number of days — and the process is compressible into an SOP a hired field rep can run, rather than being founder charisma                                                                                                                                     | A dealer will pay a recurring monthly fee against a one-time-licence anchor and a zero-rupee paper substitute, and will still be paying in the thirteenth month without a founder in the room                                                                                                                                                                          |
| **Which kernel line dies if it is false**         | The entire guiding policy. District saturation is _only_ rational if density pays; if referral does not travel, twelve districts is an arbitrary and expensive constraint and going wide is correct                                                              | The how-to-win (§4.3, row 3) and coherent action 2. If onboarding cannot be compressed, VSYST is a consultancy that bills once, not a software company                                                                                                                                                                                                            | Every number in §6, the winning aspiration, and the existence of the company. | **The smallest experiment that could falsify it** | Ask **15 dealers, none of them friends of any director**, one question about the past, not the future: _"The last business tool you started using — who told you about it?"_ Count how many name another dealer. | **What it costs to run**                          | Three days and fuel. **No money**                                                                                                                                                                                                                                | Three weeks of founder time you were going to spend anyway — the only new cost is the stopwatch and the honesty                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   | The discount, and the discomfort of asking                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             |

Three things about that table are worth saying out loud.

**First: none of the three experiments requires money, a hire, or a line of code.** All three could have been run in the time it took to read this lesson twice. That is the usual finding. The experiments that would settle a strategy are almost always cheaper than the work being done instead of them — which is why the reason they go unrun is never cost. It is that a "U" you have not tested still feels like it might be a "K", and a "U" you have tested might turn out to be a "no".

**Second: A3 is the one that will get postponed**, because asking for money is the only one of the three that can end a friendly relationship, and because "we'll charge once the product is ready" is an infinitely renewable excuse. It is also the only assumption whose failure invalidates the other two — a dealer who will not pay makes referral and onboarding speed irrelevant. **Run it first.** [[06-strategy-ii-moats-positioning-and-the-business-model\|Lesson 06]] §6.1's rule is the operational form: never free, always priced, sometimes paid later.

**Third: A2 has a hidden second half that most founders test badly.** Onboarding three dealers quickly proves that _you_ can do it. It does not prove the process transfers, and the strategy needs the transferable version — that is what "coherent action 5" and the hired-rep future depend on. The tag that tells you the truth is the hours bucket, not the total: if the hours are in migration and training, an SOP and an import tool fix them; if they are in owner hand-holding, you have discovered that the product is a person, and no SOP fixes that ([[10-building-the-team-hiring-equity-and-firing\|lesson 10]] on what is and is not hireable).

**The fourth assumption, deliberately parked.** §6.6's claim — that the ledger becomes an underwriting asset an NBFC will pay to originate against — is at least as shaky as A1–A3 and considerably more consequential. It is off the register on purpose, because **it is not testable this year at any price** and a register containing untestable rows trains you to ignore registers. What it gets instead is a **trigger**: when 30 dealers have twelve unbroken months of reconciled ledger, take that data to one NBFC and one bank and ask what they would need to see. Write the trigger, with the number, in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log\|C29]] today, so that the conversation happens because the condition was met rather than because someone got excited in a meeting.

### 7.6 Milestones, not deadlines — releasing money against redeemed assumptions

The last discovery-driven document is the one that turns all of this into behaviour: **you do not fund the plan, you fund the next checkpoint.** Each checkpoint redeems one or more assumptions, and reaching it unlocks the next tranche of spending. Amazon's framing of the same discipline is the one-way / two-way door test — reversible decisions should be made fast on roughly 70% of the information you would like, irreversible ones should not ([Amazon, 2015 Letter to Shareholders](https://s2.q4cdn.com/299287126/files/doc_financials/annual/2015-Letter-to-Shareholders.PDF); [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2018/11/19/jeff-bezos-simple-strategy-for-answering-amazons-hardest-questions--.html)) — and the entrepreneurship literature's version is **affordable loss**: commit only what you can lose without ending the venture, rather than what a projected return would justify ([Sarasvathy — effectuation and the affordable-loss principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saras_Sarasvathy)). For a bootstrapped company with no outside capital, affordable loss is not a philosophy. It is the only funding rule available.

| Checkpoint                                                   | Assumption it redeems                     | What it unlocks                                                                                        | The kill-or-continue rule                                                 |
| ------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **15 structured dealer conversations, none of them friends** | A1, plus four of §7.3's "A" rows for free | Permission to keep the twelve-district refusal                                                         | <4 of 15 name another dealer → re-open where-to-play before anything else |
| **3 dealers onboarded, stopwatch running, hours tagged**     | A2                                        | Permission to write the onboarding SOP and build the import tool                                       | Majority of hours in "owner hand-holding" → stop selling, fix delivery    |
| **3 signed letters and 3 collected payments**                | A3                                        | Permission to quote a price out loud and to publish tiers                                              | 0 of 8 close at any price → business model review, not a discount         |
| **10 paying dealers, 2 of them referred by another dealer**  | A1 again, but with money attached         | The first non-founder hire conversation ([[10-building-the-team-hiring-equity-and-firing\|lesson 10]]) | 0 referrals in 10 → density is not compounding; the arena is wrong        |
| **30 dealers × 12 unbroken reconciled months**               | The parked fourth assumption              | The first NBFC conversation (§6.6)                                                                     | Reconciliation breaks → the money layer is years away, not quarters       |

Two rules make this real rather than decorative. **The tranche is written before the checkpoint is attempted**, so that "we're nearly there" cannot quietly release the money. And **a missed checkpoint is a scheduled conversation, not a failure** — it goes on the directors' agenda with the register open, because the whole point of writing the failing result in advance was to make the conversation survivable. [[19-planning-okrs-and-capital-allocation\|Lesson 19]] turns this into the annual planning and capital-allocation mechanism; here it is only the strategy's own release valve.

### 7.7 The residue no experiment will settle

Some things stay in Knight's right-hand column no matter how disciplined you are, and pretending otherwise produces the worst artefact of all: a register full of rows that cannot move. Those get a different instrument — **an early-warning signal with a named watcher and a pre-agreed response** — which is the promise §5.3 made and this is where it is kept.

| Event you cannot test for                                                                    | Why no experiment settles it                                                               | The early-warning signal, and who watches it                                                                                                                                                                                                 | The pre-agreed response                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **An OMC ships a free dealer app**                                                           | It depends on a decision inside someone else's organisation, on a timetable you cannot see | An OMC digital-initiative tender or RFP; a territory manager mentioning a pilot; a dealer-portal feature that starts to look like an order book. **Domain-expert director watches**, monthly, through the same relationships action 4 builds | Do not compete on the free tier. Move immediately to the layers a free OMC app will not touch: the dealer's _customer_ side, credit and reconciliation, and multi-OMC dealers. Write this response down now, while it is cheap to think clearly ([[18-risk-crisis-and-the-hard-things\|lesson 18]]; [[T15-risk-register\|T15]] in the COO course) |
| **A statutory change reshapes the product** — e-invoice threshold, TCS, GST return structure | Announced in a Budget or a notification, on nobody's schedule but the Council's            | The CA on retainer, plus GST Council press releases. **CEO watches**, because it is a direction question, not a compliance-calendar question ([[COO-Docs/index\|COO Docs]] owns the calendar)                                                | Compliance correctness is a stated capability (§4.4). **The rule that ties §7 together.** A strategy under uncertainty is not a prediction that you defend; it is **a set of named bets, each with a stated failing result, a cheapest test, an owner, a date, and a written consequence.** When you have that, being wrong stops being a humiliation and becomes an outcome you had already budgeted for — and that, far more than any framework in §2, is what lets three related founders argue about the plan without arguing about each other.

## 8. Writing the strategy down

A strategy that lives only in the CEO's head is not a strategy; it is a mood the company has to guess at. Three related founders who have talked about the plan for two years will each swear they agree, and will each be describing a different company — and the disagreement will surface, disguised, as an argument about a feature, a district, or someone's hours. **Writing is what converts an assumed agreement into a real one, and the value of the document is almost entirely in the arguments it forces before it is signed.**

Two failure modes bracket this, and both are common in small companies. The first is **no document**: strategy is transmitted by conversation, decays at about the rate of a WhatsApp thread, and cannot be handed to a new joiner. The second is **the wrong document**: a forty-slide deck built for an investor, which is a _story_ (that is [[12-communication-the-ceo-as-chief-storyteller|lesson 12]]'s job) rather than a set of choices, and which nobody in the company can recall under pressure. The antidote to both is small, dull and hard to write.

### 8.1 One page, and why the constraint is the point

The format is one page, printed, on the wall where the three of you actually sit. Not one page because attention spans are short — **one page because a page is a budget.** Five actions do not fit alongside eleven; a diagnosis and its hedge do not fit together; a guiding policy and every exception someone wants for it do not fit at all. The page does the refusing that the room finds it hard to do. This is the same reason Amazon replaced presentations with a fixed-length narrative memo read in silence before discussion: forcing prose of a fixed length exposes sloppy thinking that bullets conceal, and reading it together stops the loudest voice from setting the frame ([Amazon — 2017 letter to shareholders](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2017-letter-to-shareholders)). [[02-how-a-ceo-thinks|Lesson 02]] §6 makes the general case for writing as the CEO's primary instrument. This section is its narrowest and most important application.

The template lives in [[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager|C04]] and is not repeated here. What matters is what each block is for, and the specific way each one fails:

| Block                                               | What it is for                                                            | How it fails in practice                                                                             | The fix                                                                                                                                                                    |
| --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Header — version, set date, review date, holder** | Makes the document a thing with a history rather than a floating file     | Undated. Nobody can tell whether it predates the evidence they are holding                           | Version number, set date, review date, and _supersedes version n−1 dated \_\_\__. Three seconds, and it is what makes the diff possible                                    |
| **Diagnosis**                                       | The one or two sentences everything else answers to                       | Grows to a paragraph, hedges, and becomes uncontestable                                              | If it does not pass all six tests in C04(b) — mechanism, falsifiable, contestable, simple, implies action, uncomfortable — it is not ready                                 |
| **Guiding policy + its refusals**                   | The approach, and what it forbids, in the same breath                     | The "which means we will not…" clause is written last, softly, and forbids only things nobody wanted | At least one refusal must hurt. §4.2's three refusals are the model                                                                                                        |
| **Coherent actions (max 5, each with an owner)**    | The work that the policy implies                                          | Becomes a task list. Six items appear, then nine                                                     | The removal test on every line: take it out, say what breaks. If nothing breaks, it belongs in the COO's quarterly plan ([[T19-quarterly-plan-and-review\|T19]]), not here |
| **Deliberately not doing**                          | The visible cost of the choice                                            | Filled with things nobody was going to do                                                            | At least one item must be something you genuinely want to do, with the reason next to it                                                                                   |
| **Proximate objective**                             | Close enough to act on this week, specific enough that failure is visible | Replaced by a revenue target, which is an output, not an objective                                   | §2.3's form: twelve districts, a measured time-to-first-invoice, three filmable reference dealers, four gatekeepers                                                        |
| **The crux this quarter**                           | The one part of the challenge that is both critical and addressable       | Unchanged for four quarters, which means it is being described rather than attacked                  | Re-ask §3.4's question each quarter, separately, in writing, and compare the three answers                                                                                 |
| **Assumption register (page two)**                  | The bets, with tests, owners and dates                                    | Written once, never re-read, and the shaky rows quietly drop off                                     | §7.5's three rows, minimum two live tests with an owner and a date this quarter                                                                                            |
| **Change rule**                                     | Prevents the quiet edit                                                   | Absent, so the strategy drifts and nobody can date the drift                                         | Diagnosis change = one-way door (C29 entry + board decision under [[C03-decision-rights-matrix\|C03]]). Policy or action change = ordinary quarterly decision, minuted     |

One more rule about the page itself: **write it in the words you would use with the domain-expert director, not the words you would use with an investor.** If a sentence on the kernel could appear unchanged in a pitch deck, it is probably fluff (§1). The kernel is allowed to be blunt about things the deck cannot say — that the arena is thin, that break-even needs 13–27% of a district cluster, that one of the founders is a single point of failure.

### 8.2 Who reads it, and what each reader is allowed to get

The kernel is an **internal** document. It is not the story, not the deck, not the website. Its job is to be argued with by people who can change what the company does; everyone else gets something derived from it. Confusing these audiences is how a company either bores its customers with strategy or lies to itself with marketing.

| Reader                                            | Gets                                                                                                                                                                                          | Must **not** get                           | Why                                                                                                                                                              |
| ------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **The three founder-directors**                   | The whole page and the register, before adoption, with an obligation to disagree in writing                                                                                                   | A finished document presented for approval | A kernel nobody argued with was not tested; C04's own rule is that both co-directors must disagree in writing before it is adopted                               |
| **The COO seat** (today: shared; later: a person) | The whole page. The management-systems row is _their_ row                                                                                                                                     | A parallel strategy of their own           | One strategy, one scorecard. The CEO owns direction; the COO owns whether the operating system can carry it ([[COO-Docs/index\|COO Docs]])                       |
| **The first non-founder hires**                   | The whole page, on day one, read together, questions taken                                                                                                                                    | A sanitised version                        | A field rep who does not know the twelve-district refusal will sell outside it — and be right to, given what they were told                                      |
| **Dealers and prospects**                         | The one-paragraph positioning version, in Hindi, in their words ([[C08-positioning-and-messaging-canvas\|C08]])                                                                               | The diagnosis. Ever                        | "Our diagnosis is that you don't trust software and can't evaluate features" is true, useful internally, and insulting out loud                                  |
| **The CA, the banker, the CS**                    | The proximate objective and the break-even arithmetic                                                                                                                                         | The register's confidence percentages      | They need the number they must plan against, not your uncertainty about it                                                                                       |
| **OMCs, associations, partners**                  | The where-to-play row and the relevant action                                                                                                                                                 | The not-doing list                         | A partner reading what you refuse will read it as instability, not discipline ([[14-partnerships-ecosystem-and-founder-relationships\|lesson 14]])               |

The reader that matters most is the fourth row's inverse: **the future employee who has to make a small decision at 4 PM on a Thursday with nobody available to ask.** If the page is written well, they can. That is the entire operational test of a strategy document, and it is a higher bar than "the directors agreed".

### 8.3 How often it changes — and which parts change at which speed

The most common mistake is treating the whole page as one object with one review date. It is four objects with four clocks.

| Part                                | Cadence                                                     | Who decides                                | Recorded where                                                                                                                                                   |
| ----------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Diagnosis**                       | Annually, and otherwise only on a **one-way-door** decision | All three directors, minuted               | [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log\|C29]] entry written _before_ the outcome is known, plus a board minute ([[C17-board-pack-agenda-and-minutes\|C17]]) |
| **Guiding policy and its refusals** | Quarterly review, changed rarely                            | CEO proposes, directors ratify             | Quarterly minute; new C04 version                                                                                                                                |
| **Coherent actions and the crux**   | Quarterly, expected to change                               | CEO, with the owner of each action         | New C04 version; the actions become the input to [[C28-annual-plan-and-ceo-okr-sheet\|C28]]                                                                      |
| **Assumption register**             | **Monthly** — the only part that should move most months    | The named owner of each row updates status | Section (c) of C04 and the `Strategy Kernel` tab of [vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx](toolkit/vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx)                                                   |
| **Not-doing list**                  | Whenever an item is removed — never silently                | CEO, with a written reason                 | The reason goes in C29. An item removed without a reason is drift wearing a decision's clothes                                                                   |

Two anchors make the cadence real rather than aspirational. **A dated review on the page itself** — set date plus ninety days — so the review happens because it was scheduled, not because someone is anxious. And **one fixed annual date**: April, when PPAC republishes the outlet files (§6.7) and the Indian financial year turns, so the sizing refresh, the kernel rewrite and the annual plan happen in one block instead of drifting apart.

### 8.4 It changes on evidence, not on mood

This is the rule the whole section exists to install, and it is enforced by a single mechanism: **every proposed change to the kernel must name the evidence that arrived.** Not the feeling, not the article, not the competitor's launch tweet — the evidence, with a date and a source. If the proposer cannot name it, the answer is no, and the answer costs nobody anything because the review date is at most ninety days away.

The two failure modes are symmetrical and equally fatal:

- **Strategic churn.** The kernel changes every time a founder reads something, meets an impressive person, or has a bad week. Nothing compounds, because compounding requires that the same thing be done repeatedly. The tell is a company that has been "pivoting" for four quarters and has no diff, because nothing was ever written down to diff against.
- **Strategic fossilisation.** The kernel does not change even as its assumptions fail, because changing it would mean admitting the last year was aimed wrong. The tell is a register whose rows have said "Untested" for three quarters, and a crux that has not moved (§3.4).

The register is the cure for both, because it converts "should we change?" from a debate about conviction into a lookup: **which assumption failed, and what did we write down in advance that we would do about it?**

| Someone wants to change the strategy because…                            | Is it evidence?                           | What to do                                                                                                                                                                              |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| A pre-registered test failed — 2 of 15 dealers named another dealer (A1) | **Yes**                                   | Execute the written consequence. This is the system working, not a crisis                                                                                                               |
| A competitor launched something                                          | Not yet                                   | Note it. Evidence is what it does to _your_ funnel over a quarter, not what it does to your mood in an afternoon                                                                        |
| An inbound lead arrived from another state and it is large               | **No** — this is the refusal being tested | Log the no. If out-of-district inbound reaches a pre-agreed volume, _that_ is evidence and it goes on the register as a row                                                             |
| A dealer asked for a feature                                             | No                                        | Goes to the no-list with his name against it ([[C25-product-bets-and-the-no-list\|C25]]). Ten dealers asking is a different sentence                                                    |
| A director is losing confidence                                          | **Yes, but not about the strategy**       | Founder alignment is a real problem with a real mechanism ([[03-the-ceo-core-value-and-the-founder-contracts\|lesson 03]] §8). Do not let it be resolved by silently rewriting the plan |
| Revenue is behind plan                                                   | Usually no                                | Behind plan is an _output_. Ask which assumption it implicates and check that row. If none, the plan was optimistic, which is arithmetic, not strategy                                  |
| The break-even arithmetic came out worse than expected (§6.5)            | **Yes**                                   | This already happened, and §6.5 named the three available responses. That is what a partial falsification is supposed to produce                                                        |
| Nine months passed and nothing was tested                                | **Yes — about you**                       | The problem is not the strategy. It is that the register has no owner with a date, and that is a CEO failure ([[16-decisions-delegation-and-not-being-the-bottleneck\|lesson 16]])      |

A last protection, borrowed from [[02-how-a-ceo-thinks|lesson 02]]: run a **pre-mortem on the kernel once a year** — assume it is eighteen months later and the strategy has plainly failed, then have each director write the reasons independently before anyone speaks. Imagining that the outcome has already happened measurably improves people's ability to name causes ([Klein — Performing a Project Premortem, HBR, September 2007](https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem); [Gary Klein](https://www.gary-klein.com/premortem)). Everything that surfaces becomes a register row or a not-doing item. Nothing surfaces that is not already latent in the room.

### 8.5 Version control, and why the diff is the real artefact

Keep every superseded version, dated, in the same folder. Never edit in place. **Next year's most useful document is not version 4 — it is the diff between versions 1 and 4**, because the date on which a belief changed, and what changed it, is the only record you will have of how the company learns.

Three things the diff tells you that nothing else does. **Whether the diagnosis is stable** — a diagnosis rewritten three times in a year means it was never found, and the company has been executing against a placeholder. **Whether refusals hold** — refusals that quietly disappear between versions are the single earliest signal of loss of focus, and they never leave with an announcement. **Whether evidence actually moves you** — if the register shows tests completed and the kernel is unchanged, either the tests were designed to confirm (§7.4) or the kernel is not really what the company runs on.

Practically, at VSYST: the kernel is a markdown file in the vault, versioned in git like everything else, with the version number in the filename or the header, and the C29 entry linking the change to the evidence that caused it. Git already gives you the diff for free. What git does not give you is the _reason_, and that is what C29 is for.

### 8.6 The chain from this page to the money — where lesson 19 takes over

The kernel is the top of a chain, and each link belongs to a named owner and a named artefact. Where the chain is broken, work continues to happen and stops meaning anything.

```
DIAGNOSIS  ──▶  GUIDING POLICY  ──▶  COHERENT ACTIONS   (this lesson, C04/C05)
                                            │
                                            ▼
                                    ANNUAL PLAN + CEO OKRs   (lesson 19, C28)
                                            │
                                            ▼
                                 QUARTERLY PLAN + SCORECARD   (COO Docs, T19)
                                            │
                                            ▼
                              WEEKLY CADENCE + WHO DOES WHAT   (COO Docs)
```

The test that the chain holds is a single question asked upward: **take any objective on the quarterly plan and ask which coherent action it serves; take that action and ask which clause of the guiding policy it executes; take that clause and ask which part of the diagnosis it answers.** Anything that cannot be traced up three levels is either the operating system doing its own necessary work — which is fine, and belongs to the COO — or it is drift.

[[19-planning-okrs-and-capital-allocation|Lesson 19]] owns everything below the second box: how the annual plan is built for a company that cannot forecast, how the kernel becomes key results without becoming a wish list, how confidence is used as a signal rather than a promise, and how a bootstrapped company allocates the small amount of capital it actually controls. This lesson's only job is to make sure that what arrives at the top of that chain is a real choice rather than a mood — because **an excellent planning system pointed at a bad diagnosis is just a faster way to be wrong.**

## 9. At VSYST — applying this now

- **You already have a strategy. It is just not written down, so nobody can disagree with it.** The twelve-district instinct, the white-glove onboarding, the refusal to chase Pune leads — all of it exists in the founders' heads in three slightly different versions. Writing it down does not create the strategy; it exposes where the three versions differ. That exposure is the whole point, and it will take one uncomfortable evening.
- **Write the diagnosis separately, all three of you, before anyone speaks.** §3.4's question — _what is the hardest part of making this business work, not the most urgent_ — answered in writing, opened together. If the three answers match you have a diagnosis. If they differ, you have found the argument that every resource-allocation fight for the next year would otherwise have been a proxy for.
- **The diagnosis in §9.1 is a draft written to be attacked, not adopted.** If all three of you read it and nod, something has gone wrong — either it is too soft to be contestable, or nobody has actually engaged. C04's rule stands: both co-directors must disagree with it **in writing** before it is signed.
- **Your assumption-to-knowledge score is roughly one K in fifteen (§7.3), and that is the number to move this quarter — not revenue.** Revenue at n = 3 tells you nothing. The ratio tells you whether the company is learning or merely working.
- **Run A3 first, this month.** Three signed letters, a real price, money actually collected. It is the only assumption whose failure invalidates the other two, it is the one that will get postponed, and every week it is postponed the product gets better at a problem nobody has agreed to pay for.
- **Nothing in §7.5 costs money.** Fifteen conversations, three stopwatched onboardings, three signed letters. All three experiments could be finished before the next quarterly review, and none needs a hire, a tool or a line of code. If they do not happen, the reason is not resources.
- **The break-even arithmetic is already a partial falsification and it deserves a decision, not another model.** §6.5 said twelve districts on subscription alone need 13–27% penetration to cover costs. §6.5 also gave three responses — hold price and widen later, raise realised price through faster time-to-value, or add the second layer. **Pick one, write which, date it in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]].** Not choosing is choosing the first one by default, and doing it without noticing is the failure mode.
- **Put one cell on the wall and one cell in the workbook.** On the wall: the break-even dealer count. In the `Strategy Kernel` tab of [vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx](toolkit/vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx): `K / (K+A+U)`, re-scored at day 90. Two numbers, and between them they answer "are we a business?" and "are we learning?".
- **Name the watcher for the OMC signal today.** §7.7 says the domain-expert director watches monthly, through the same relationships coherent action 4 builds. It costs nothing until it matters, and it will matter with no notice.
- **What not to do this quarter:** do not rewrite the diagnosis more than once; do not add a sixth coherent action; do not remove a refusal without writing the reason; do not send the kernel to anyone outside the three of you; do not build anything that is not blocking an onboarding; and do not let a single warm evening with a friendly dealer count as evidence (§7.4).

### 9.1 The draft kernel — copy this, argue with it, then sign a changed version

This is a **filled draft**, not a template, so that the work in front of you is editing rather than starting from a blank page. Every line is derived from something earlier in this lesson and can be traced back to it. Every line is also wrong in at least one place — that is what the blanks and the confidence percentages are for.

Copy it into [[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager|C04]], change what you disagree with, fill the blanks, print it, and put it on the wall.

```
STRATEGY KERNEL — VSYST Technologies Pvt. Ltd.
set <date>   ·   review <date + 90d>   ·   version 1   ·   held by: CEO
supersedes: nothing. This is the first written version.

DIAGNOSIS
  Indian fuel dealers do not lack software. They lack a reason to change a
  paper-and-Tally process that has never visibly failed them — and the person
  who must decide to change it is a 45-65 year old owner with low software
  trust, a switching cost measured in ledger migration and staff retraining,
  and thin operating cash.
  Our binding constraint is therefore NOT features and NOT price. It is
  adoption friction and trust, and both are earned at district level, one
  dealer at a time, through people the owner already believes.

  Confidence: __%
  Evidence it rests on: vault research on owner acquisition and the
    education gap; 15 regional vendors already selling at Rs 15,000-25,000
    one-time and none of them losing on features; ~3/4 of Indian SMEs already
    running business on WhatsApp.
  What would prove this wrong: dealers who buy quickly on a feature
    demonstration without a personal referral; or a competitor winning this
    segment through paid acquisition alone.

GUIDING POLICY
  We will win DISTRICT CLUSTERS COMPLETELY rather than India thinly —
  starting with the 12 districts of Raipur + Durg divisions, where a founder
  can drive to the customer and back in a day — by making the FIRST FOURTEEN
  DAYS of a dealer's life the product we optimise above everything else, and
  by borrowing trust from the three channels the owner already believes
  (the dealer next door, the association office-bearer, the pump's CA)
  instead of trying to manufacture it with marketing.

  Which means we will NOT:
    - sell outside the 12 districts, even when a large inbound lead arrives
      from another state (it goes on a waitlist, with a dated written reason)
    - build features requested by prospects who have not completed onboarding
    - compete on price against Rs 15,000 one-time desktop licences
    - spend money on brand marketing before referral density exists
    - sell to the manager or the accountant when the owner is available

  Confidence: __%

COHERENT ACTIONS  (5 max. Owner named. Removal test applied.)
  1. CHOOSE THE CLUSTER AND PUBLISH THE REFUSAL.
     Raipur + Durg divisions, 12 of Chhattisgarh's 33 districts. Everything
     outside goes on a written, dated waitlist.
     owner: CEO
     if removed: actions 2-4 lose their density — the same founder is driving
     to Bilaspur and to Pune in one week, and onboarding capacity collapses.

  2. MAKE TIME-TO-FIRST-INVOICE THE COMPANY'S ONE OBSESSED-OVER NUMBER.
     Founder-run white-glove onboarding including ledger migration. Measured
     with a stopwatch on every dealer, hours tagged by cause.
     owner: CEO
     if removed: action 3 produces reference dealers who are not actually
     live, which destroys action 4 the first time a gatekeeper asks a hard
     question.

  3. MANUFACTURE THE FIRST THREE REFERENCE DEALERS DELIBERATELY.
     Chosen for credibility with peers, not for revenue. Over-served. Each
     filmed at his own pump, in the local language.
     owner: domain-expert director
     if removed: action 4 has nothing to point at, and A1 stays untested.

  4. BUY TRUST WHOLESALE THROUGH GATEKEEPERS.
     One district association office-bearer, one OMC territory manager, two
     CA firms serving pumps. Each given something valuable before anything
     is asked.
     owner: domain-expert director
     if removed: acquisition reverts to cold calling, which this diagnosis
     says does not work on this buyer at any volume.

  5. SHIP ONLY ONBOARDING-BLOCKING WORK.
     Everything else goes to the no-list with the requesting dealer's name
     against it, so the no-list becomes the roadmap input.
     owner: CEO
     if removed: action 2 starves, because engineering time is the same
     time.

WE ARE DELIBERATELY NOT DOING
  - No second segment. Lubricant distributors and bulk-diesel operators wait,
    even though the product already fits them.
      (because: two segments at three people means two half-motions, and the
       reference density that makes action 3 work does not accumulate.)
  - No thirteenth district, and no second state.
      (because: density is the strategy. Coverage is its opposite.)
  - No salesperson hired before A2 is settled.
      (because: you cannot hand over a process you have not measured, and a
       rep sent out with founder charisma as the method will fail publicly.)
  - No payments take-rate and no lending product.
      (because: the RBI net-worth floor alone rules it out for years, and it
       needs a credit history that does not exist yet. Year three, on a
       written trigger — not this year.)
  - No freemium tier, no free pilots. Priced always, paid sometimes later.
      (because: a price of zero has to be invented from nothing, and a price
       agreed once can be raised.)
  - No published pricing page before ten paid pilots.
      (because: the tier boundaries are still placeholders.)
  - THE ONE WE ACTUALLY WANT TO DO: no rebuild of the reporting/analytics
    layer, however much better we know it could be.
      (because: no dealer has ever stalled before his first invoice because
       of a chart. This is the item that proves the list is real.)

PROXIMATE OBJECTIVE
  By <date, ~2 quarters out>: 12 districts named and published · a measured
  median time-to-first-invoice with hours tagged by cause · 3 filmable
  reference dealers live and paying · 4 gatekeeper relationships opened ·
  A1, A2 and A3 each moved out of "Untested".

THE CRUX THIS QUARTER
  Onboarding -> first correct invoice. Everything downstream of it is idle
  behind it, and nothing upstream of it is scarce yet.
  (Re-ask section 3.4's question next quarter. If the crux has not moved in
   four quarters, we have been describing it rather than attacking it.)

CHANGE RULE
  Diagnosis change  = one-way door. C29 entry written BEFORE the outcome is
                      known, plus a directors' decision under C03. Never a
                      quiet edit.
  Policy / action change = ordinary quarterly decision, minuted.
  Every proposed change must name the evidence that arrived, with a date.
  Superseded versions are kept, never overwritten.
```

### 9.3 Three things in that draft that are probably wrong

Adopting the draft unchanged would be the worst possible outcome of this lesson, so here are the places to start cutting. **The twelve-district boundary is a driving-time artefact, not a market fact** — it came from a map and a day's drive, and §6.5's 50% district share is a population proxy that nobody has replaced with an OMC number; the honest cluster might be six districts or eighteen. **"Fourteen days" has no evidence behind it at all** — it is a round number standing in for a measurement that has never been taken, and A2 exists to replace it with a real one; until then, do not put it in front of a dealer as a promise. And **the diagnosis names trust as the constraint, which is comfortable for a company whose second director has trust to lend** — check that convenience honestly, because §7.2's reverse income statement pointed at a different constraint entirely (founder-weeks of onboarding capacity), and a diagnosis that happens to make your existing assets the answer deserves one more round of suspicion than one that does not.

## 10. Exercises

Five exercises, in order. The first one is the only one that cannot be done alone, and the last one is the only one that leaves the building — which is also the only one that will actually change what you believe. Do not skip to §10.2 because the draft in §9.1 is already written; a kernel you edited is not a kernel you argued for.

**10.1 — The crux, answered separately and opened together (30 min, all three directors).** Send one question by WhatsApp with a deadline: _"In writing, by tonight — what is the hardest part of making this business work? Not the most urgent. The hardest."_ No discussion first, no examples, no hints, because a hint is a leading question (§3.2). Open all three answers together, read them aloud in turn, and write the three down side by side. Then apply §3.4's rule: if an answer is a task ("finish the billing module"), push — _and if that shipped tomorrow, what would still be hard?_ — until the answer stops being work and starts being a property of the world. If the three converge, draft the diagnosis from the converged answer immediately, while the agreement is still fresh; if they diverge, **stop and resolve that first**, because every resource argument for the next year is really this argument in disguise. Finish by running all six of C04(b)'s tests — mechanism, falsifiable, contestable, simple, implies action, uncomfortable — on the draft sentence, and write down which test it fails first. **Artefact:** three separately-written crux answers, one drafted diagnosis of one or two sentences with the six test results marked against it, and a [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] entry recording whether the three of you agreed on what business you are in.

**10.2 — Fill C04 end to end, then get two written disagreements (45 min, plus a night).** Copy §9.1's draft into [[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager|C04]] and change it. Replace the diagnosis with 10.1's version. Rewrite the guiding policy in your own words and then test the refusals one at a time: for each, name the specific opportunity it forbids and say whether you would actually turn that opportunity down next Tuesday — if you would not, the refusal is decoration, so cut it or commit to it. Apply the removal test to all five actions and write, next to each, the sentence saying what breaks; delete any action where nothing breaks and send it to the COO's quarterly plan ([[T19-quarterly-plan-and-review|T19]]) instead. Check §9.3's three suspects specifically. Fill every confidence percentage — the number you would actually bet at, not the number that looks serious. Then circulate it and **do not sign it until both co-directors have disagreed with something in writing**; take at least one change from each. Print the signed version and put it where the three of you sit. **Artefact:** a signed, dated, printed **C04 version 1** with a diagnosis that passed six tests, a guiding policy with at least one refusal that hurts, five actions each with an owner and a removal-test sentence, a not-doing list containing something you want to do, and two written disagreements on file.

**10.3 — Fill the choice cascade and test the interlock (40 min).** Open [[C05-choice-cascade-worksheet|C05]] and fill all five rows — winning aspiration, where to play, how to win, capabilities, management systems — using §4.6's filled version as a starting draft rather than an answer. Rule: **every row gets a refusal in the second column**, and the where-to-play row gets three, one on each of §4.2's axes (segment, geography, buyer). Then run the interlock test the way §4.6 does it: pull each row out in turn and say what gets harder in the other four. A row whose removal changes nothing is a description, not a choice. Then do the part that produces the real work — take the how-to-win row and write Martin's question underneath it: **what would have to be true for this to work?** List every belief, then rank them by shakiness rather than by how easy they are to check, and circle the top three. Those three go straight into 10.5. Finally, confirm the management-systems row hands off cleanly: it should name the COO course's artefacts, not invent parallel ones ([[COO-Docs/index|COO Docs]]). **Artefact:** a completed **C05** with five choices, at least seven refusals, an interlock note for each row, and a ranked "what would have to be true" list with the top three circled.

**10.4 — Build the market map from primary sources, and put one number on a wall (45 min).** Open [[C06-market-map-sizing-and-segments|C06]] and the `Market Map` tab of [vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx](toolkit/vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx). Download the PPAC files yourself rather than copying §6's numbers — the point of this exercise is that the model is _yours_ — and paste the source URL and the as-on date into the cell beside every input ([PPAC state-wise retail outlets](https://ppac.gov.in/uploads/page-images/1787131130_Statewise_Retail_Outlets.xls); **VERIFY LIVE**, these files are republished on a rolling basis). Fill the five inputs from §6.5's formula: `outlets`, `coco_share`, `outlets_per_GSTIN`, `blended_price_per_month`, `penetration`. Mark each input K, A or U using §7.3's letters; you should find that two are K and three are not. Run the grid — three price points × four penetrations — and then run the reverse income statement backwards from your real monthly cash cost ([[C13-runway-burn-and-scenario-planner|C13]]) to the break-even dealer count. Convert that count into **founder-weeks of onboarding** at your honest onboarding rate, which is the line that will surprise you. Then do the free fix: count distinct dealer _firm names_ against outlets in your own twelve districts and replace `outlets_per_GSTIN` with a fact. **Artefact:** a filled **C06** and `Market Map` tab with a source URL and as-on date beside every input, a K/A/U mark on each of the five, a break-even dealer count **written on a physical wall**, and the same count expressed in founder-weeks.

**10.5 — Write the falsification list, pre-register the failing results, and start one experiment today (30 min at the desk, then three days in the field).** Take the top three from 10.3 and write them into section (c) of [[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager|C04]] and the `Strategy Kernel` tab, using §7.5's columns. Each row must be **stated so that it could be false** — "dealers like referrals" cannot be false; "fewer than 4 of 15 dealers will name another dealer as the source of their last business tool" can. For each row write, before running anything: the failing result **with a number in it**, the sample size and the stopping rule, the cheapest test on §7.4's ladder that could still produce that failing result, the owner, the date, and — the column everyone omits — **what changes on Monday if it fails**. Give the paper to a co-director to hold (§7.4, rule 3). Then score the whole kernel with §7.3's letters, count K, A and U, and put `K / (K+A+U)` in a single cell with today's date. Then stop writing and **start the A3 experiment this week**: pick eight dealers who have completed or could complete onboarding, write the one-page letter with a loudly-labelled grandfathered founding price, and ask three of them for money. **Artefact:** a three-row assumption register with pre-registered failing results, sample sizes, owners, dates and written consequences; a dated assumption-to-knowledge score in the workbook; a [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] entry naming which of §6.5's three responses to the break-even arithmetic you have chosen; and one signed one-page letter in front of a real dealer before the next review date.
