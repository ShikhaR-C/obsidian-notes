# 20 — The CEO's Own Operating System, Succession and What Winning Looks Like

_Phase 4 · Running the Company and Yourself · Months 6–24. After this lesson you can maintain the one instrument this course has so far ignored — yourself; manufacture accountability when there is no boss, no investor and no independent director; run a 360 in a family company where upward criticism costs the person giving it; get feedback nobody wants to give you and survive the ten seconds after hearing it; run a learning loop that actually calibrates judgment instead of accumulating anecdotes; treat sleep, exercise and mental health as operating parameters; run the CEO's version of the two-week test — which measures queued **decisions**, not stalled tasks; assess honestly whether you are still growing with the seat at each revenue threshold; write the bus-factor succession document the company needs tomorrow, the role-succession plan it needs this year, and the seat-succession plan it will need one day; think about outcome shape without fantasy; and say, in checkable evidence, what winning looks like._

Nineteen lessons pointed outward. Strategy, customers, capital, governance, team, culture, story, product, partners, cadence, decisions, numbers, risk, plan — every one of them an instrument the CEO operates. This lesson asks the question the other nineteen carefully avoided: **is the person operating them still fit to?**

There is no comfortable way to say the next part, so here it is plainly. **A CEO is a depreciating asset unless deliberately maintained, and there is nobody in the company whose job it is to maintain you.** The COO's mechanisms are maintained by the COO. The code is maintained by tests and reviews. The books are maintained by the CA. The compliance calendar has an owner and a due date. You have a calendar full of other people's needs, a role that changes shape under you every time the company doubles, an information environment that degrades the more senior you become, and — in a family-founded, bootstrapped Pvt Ltd with no outside board — literally nobody with the standing and the incentive to tell you that you have stopped being good at this.

This lesson is the maintenance schedule. Three parts, in order: **keep the instrument working** (§1–§5), **prove the company no longer depends on you for decisions** (§6), and **plan for the day it belongs to somebody else** (§7–§9). Then §10 states what winning looks like in evidence a stranger could check, and §11 says the one thing a course cannot.

Its sibling is [[20-the-autopilot-test-and-scaling-the-machine|COO lesson 20]], which this lesson deliberately mirrors and deliberately does not repeat. The COO's definition of done is _the machine runs without heroics for two weeks_. The CEO's is harder and more personal: **the company outgrows the person who started it, on purpose.**

## Explain-it-like-I'm-5

A family runs a flour mill on the edge of a small town — one chakki, two stones, a motor, and a queue of women with steel tins from six in the morning. The father has run it for thirty-one years. His son pours the grain, weighs the flour, takes the money and is perfectly competent at all of it.

But only the father can hear the stones. When the grind starts running hot, the sound changes — not loudly, not in a way you could write on a board — and he stops the motor and dresses the stones before the flour starts smelling faintly of burning, which is the smell that loses you a customer for a year. Nobody taught him this. Nobody has asked him to teach it.

One winter he is in hospital for three weeks with something the doctor says has been building for a decade. The mill does not stop. The son pours grain, weighs flour, takes money. The stones go two weeks past dressing. Six regular customers quietly start walking the extra half-kilometre to the new roller mill, and four of them never come back.

Here is the part that matters, and it is not the part the family talks about afterwards. The mill's problem was never the stones. It was that **the man never wrote down what he heard, never trained his ear into anybody else's, never asked anyone whether his ear was even still good** — and never once, in thirty-one years, took two weeks off on purpose to find out what would break. He found out by accident, in a hospital bed, at the worst possible time, and he paid the fee in customers.

That is this lesson. What you know that nobody else does. Whether you still know it. Who finds out if you stop. And the fortnight you take deliberately, so that you learn it at a time of your choosing and at a price you can afford.

## 1. The last instrument: yourself

### 1.1 Why the asset depreciates

Depreciation sounds insulting; it is just arithmetic. An asset loses value when the world it was built for changes faster than the asset does. Four forces do that to a founder-CEO, and all four run continuously whether or not you are paying attention.

| Force                                    | What it does                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      | Why it is invisible from the inside                                                                                                       |
| ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **The job changes under you**            | The skills that got the company to its first paying dealer are not the skills that run it at fifty, and not remotely the skills that run it at five hundred. Greiner's classic model has organisations passing through phases of growth that each **end in a crisis whose resolution requires a different kind of leadership** than the phase that produced it ([Greiner, HBR](https://hbr.org/1998/05/evolution-and-revolution-as-organizations-grow))                                           | Nothing announces the transition. The company just gets slightly harder to run each quarter, which feels like the market getting harder   |
| **The information degrades**             | The higher the seat, the more filtered its inputs. People tell CEOs what they think CEOs want to hear, and stop reporting problems that reflect on them. This is §3's whole subject                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               | You still receive plenty of information. It is just increasingly the wrong information, delivered confidently                             |
| **The feedback loop is wicked**          | Strategic judgment is exercised in a domain where the feedback is slow, noisy, confounded and sometimes never arrives at all. Practice in such an environment does not reliably produce skill — it produces confidence (§4.3)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     | Twenty years of experience feels like twenty years of learning. Often it is one year of learning repeated twenty times                    |
| **The body and the mind carry the load** | 62.5 hours a week, 6.9 hours of sleep a night and exercise on a minority of days is what the best available time study found chief executives actually doing ([HBS study of 27 CEOs, summarised by MGMA](https://www.mgma.com/articles/test-of-time-harvard-study-reveals-how-ceos-spend-their-days-on-and-off-work); the underlying research is [Porter and Nohria, HBR 2018](https://hbr.org/2018/07/how-ceos-manage-time)). That is a maintenance schedule for a machine you intend to replace | The decline is gradual, and the first thing it takes is judgment quality — which is exactly the faculty you would need in order to notice |

The corollary that founders resist: **being the founder protects you from being replaced, not from being wrong.** Wasserman's data on nearly 10,000 founders found that by the third round of financing **52% of founder-CEOs had been replaced**, most of them not voluntarily ([_The Founder's Dilemmas_](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691158303/the-founders-dilemmas), Princeton 2012; the paradox that replacement risk _rises_ after the founder succeeds is set out in [Wasserman's HBS working-knowledge interview](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/the-founding-ceos-dilemma-stay-or-go) and the underlying paper, [_Founder-CEO Succession and the Paradox of Entrepreneurial Success_](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=14658)). VSYST has no investors and no independent director, so nobody will replace you. **That is not good news. It means the only mechanism capable of noticing you have outgrown the seat, or the seat has outgrown you, is one you have to build yourself** — which is §2.

**Three rules about the schedule itself.** It goes in the same calendar as everything else, with the same defended blocks as [[15-the-ceo-operating-cadence-and-calendar|lesson 15]] gives the six CEO values — a mechanism that lives in your intentions is not a mechanism. It produces artefacts other people can see, because the entire failure mode of self-maintenance is that it becomes a private hobby nobody can hold you to. And it is **the first thing you will cut when the quarter goes badly**, which is precisely the quarter where cutting it costs the most: the CEO of a company in trouble is the single input most worth maintaining.

## 2. The CEO's own scorecard, and manufacturing a boss

Row 7 is the row founders delete, and the reason to keep it is structural: rows 1–6 can each be gamed by a CEO with good intentions — revenue by discounting, runway by deferring a hire you needed, bets by declaring victory early, talent by not hiring, governance by filing on time and deciding nothing. **Row 7 is the row you cannot game, because you chose it while you were still honest.**

### 2.2 Three mechanisms available before there is a real board

A statutory board of three family founder-directors is a board in law and not an accountability mechanism in fact: it cannot fire you, has no interest in doing so, and shares your blind spots by construction. India's family businesses are measurably weak here — PwC's 12th Family Business Survey found **over half** of Indian family businesses lack cross-industry board representation and **30% maintain family-only boards** ([Forbes India on PwC's 12th survey](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/news/indian-family-businesses-high-on-ambition-low-on-governance/2991239/1); [PwC India](https://www.pwc.in/services/entrepreneurial-and-private-business/pwcs-12th-family-business-survey.html)). Three mechanisms manufacture the missing pressure, in increasing order of cost. Run all three; they catch different things.

| Mechanism                                               | What it actually is                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     | What it costs                | What it catches that the others don't                                                                                                                         | Trigger at VSYST                                                                 |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Advisory board with a standing agenda slot**          | 2–3 outsiders — one fuel-distribution or OMC-side, one software/GTM, one finance — meeting **quarterly against a written agenda circulated 72 hours ahead**, with a permanent last item: _"the CEO's scorecard, rows 3 and 7"_. Advisory boards carry no statutory duty and are not a board of directors ([Rho — startup board composition](https://www.rho.co/blog/startup-board-composition)); typical early-stage advisor equity is small, vested and documented — **VERIFY LIVE** with the CS, since equity to a non-employee in an Indian Pvt Ltd is a different instrument from an ESOP under §62(1)(b) ([EquityList — Section 62](https://www.equitylist.co/blog-post/section-62-companies-act)) | Equity + genuine preparation | **Strategy that has stopped being tested.** An outsider with domain standing is the only one who will say "your diagnosis is two years old"                   | Months 3–9, once [[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager\|C04]] exists to be argued with |
| **A founder peer group**                                | 3–5 founders at comparable stage, monthly, with one hard rule: **each brings one real number and one thing going badly.** Formal versions exist (EO, YPO, Vistage chapters run in Indian metros — fees **VERIFY LIVE** on their sites); the version that costs nothing is a WhatsApp group plus a monthly video call with Raipur, Nagpur, Bhopal and Bengaluru on it                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                    | An evening a month           | **Stage-specific blind spots.** Peers catch "you are still building instead of selling" faster than any advisor, because they did it last year                | Months 1–3. There is no reason to wait                                           |
| **A written self-review read aloud to the co-founders** | §2.3. The cheapest of the three and the one most likely to be skipped, because it is the only one where the discomfort is unavoidable and immediate                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     | Half a day, twice a year     | **Drift you have rationalised.** You cannot read your own row-7 number aloud to your brother and co-founder twice running without one of you saying something | **Now.** Month 1                                                                 |

The **standing agenda slot** is the load-bearing detail of the first row and the reason it works. An advisory board without one becomes a friendly quarterly update in which everybody agrees the plan is sensible. A permanent agenda item that is _about the CEO_, printed on the agenda, circulated in advance, and minuted, converts politeness into obligation: the advisors are now doing a job you asked them to do rather than volunteering a criticism nobody invited.

### 2.3 The written self-review, read aloud

Half a year, two pages, five questions, read out loud in one sitting to the other two directors. Written first, then read — because writing it changes what you are willing to claim, which is [[02-how-a-ceo-thinks|lesson 02]] §6's argument about thinking in prose, aimed at yourself.

```
CEO SELF-REVIEW — <period>            written <date>, read aloud <date>

1. WHAT I SAID I WOULD DO
   The 3–5 things I committed to at the start of this period (from C28 /
   C01). For each: DONE / PARTLY / NOT DONE — one line, no explanation.

2. WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED TO THE SCORECARD
   Rows 1–7, opening value → closing value. Row 7 stated in full.
   The one row I am least willing to show: ______

3. THE DECISIONS I GOT WRONG
   Minimum two, from C29, with the review date already past. For each:
   what I believed, what was true, and whether the process was bad or
   only the outcome. (A good decision with a bad outcome stays here.)

4. WHAT I DID THAT ONLY I COULD DO — AND WHAT I DID THAT I SHOULD NOT HAVE
   Hours from the C27 calendar audit, in two columns. The second column
   is the delegation backlog for next half.

5. WHERE I AM THE CONSTRAINT
   One paragraph. If I were an outside CEO arriving on Monday, what is
   the first thing I would change about how this company is run?

THE ASK: one thing each of you will tell me that you have been not saying.
```

**How to read it aloud without it becoming theatre.** Three procedural rules, and they matter more than the format. You read it; nobody interrupts. Then each co-founder answers only the final ask, and **you may not respond to their answer in that meeting** — you write it down, and reply, in writing, within a week. And the whole thing is filed at `ceo-workbook/self-review-<period>.md` where the next one can be compared against it, because the value is not in any single review but in reading two of them side by side a year later.

That last constraint — no reply in the room — exists because the natural response to criticism is explanation, explanation reads as defence, and defence teaches the other person that the honest answer costs them a twenty-minute argument. Charge that price twice and you will never hear an honest answer again. §3.4 is the general version of this skill.

### 2.4 The 360 for a CEO with three reports

A 360 collects feedback from the people above, beside and below you ([360-degree feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/360-degree_feedback)). Two things make the standard version useless at VSYST. There is nobody above. And with three or four raters, **anonymity is a fiction** — everyone can identify everyone from the phrasing, and pretending otherwise just adds a layer of theatre on top of the fear.

There is also an honest caveat to state before prescribing anything: the research on feedback is genuinely contested. Self-ratings run systematically higher than others' ratings; raters can and do manipulate the instrument; and there is a serious argument that frequent critical feedback is a poor mechanism for improving performance at all ([360-degree feedback: research findings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/360-degree_feedback); Buckingham and Goodall, [_The Feedback Fallacy_](https://hbr.org/2019/03/the-feedback-fallacy), HBR 2019). This lesson's position: at three to eight people, **a 360 is not a measurement instrument, it is a permission-granting ritual.** Its output is not a score. Its output is that saying the true thing to the CEO became a normal, scheduled, sanctioned event rather than an act of courage.

Now the Indian family-company problem, stated without euphemism. Upward criticism here is costly along two axes at once: **hierarchy**, where subordinates in high power-distance settings do not volunteer negative assessments of a superior, and **relationship**, where the superior is also an elder brother, a cousin, or the person who will be at every wedding for the next forty years. Cultures differ sharply and predictably in how directly negative feedback is given, independent of how directly they communicate generally ([Erin Meyer, _The Culture Map_](https://erinmeyer.com/books/the-culture-map/)). Asking "any feedback for me?" in that setting reliably returns "no sir, everything is fine," and the person is not lying — they are correctly reading the price.

Six design choices that produce truth anyway:

| Choice                                                                | Instead of                    | Why it works here                                                                                                                                                                         |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Ask about a behaviour, in a window**                                | "How am I doing?"             | "In the last month, when did I cut someone off in a meeting?" is answerable with a fact. The general question is answerable only with a verdict on a superior, which nobody will give     |
| **Collect it in writing, before the meeting**                         | Round-table verbal            | Writing removes the audience. Nobody has to be the first person in the room to say a hard thing                                                                                           |
| **Use a third party to collate**                                      | Reading raw responses         | The COO, an advisor, or the peer-group member you trust reads all responses and gives you the **themes with the phrasing changed**. Attribution is what people are afraid of, not content |
| **Say what will happen to it, in advance**                            | Leaving it open               | "I will pick two items, tell everyone which two, and report in ninety days." A person who knows the destination of their answer will risk a truer one                                     |
| **Go first, and go harder on yourself**                               | Inviting criticism            | Read §2.3's self-review first. If the CEO's own list of failures is more severe than anything a report would have dared, the ceiling on acceptable honesty visibly rises                  |
| **Reward the first honest answer publicly, immediately, and by name** | Thanking everyone generically | The first person who says something uncomfortable is running an experiment on whether you meant it. **What happens to them is the entire policy**, whatever the handbook says             |

**When to start:** the moment there is one non-founder employee. Before that, §2.3's read-aloud with the co-founders _is_ the 360, and the skip-level version in §3.2 waits until there is somebody to skip over.

## 3. Getting feedback nobody wants to give you

### 3.1 Why the CEO is the last to hear anything

Not because people are dishonest. Because the incentives are stacked, and they stack the same way in every company that has ever existed:

- **Reporting a problem implies ownership of it.** The person closest to the problem is usually the person it reflects on.
- **Bad news travels up more slowly than good news** and loses precision at every hop. At three people there are no hops, which is a genuine advantage you are about to lose.
- **You are the source of salary, equity and approval.** Every honest sentence is priced against that, whether or not you intend it to be.
- **You react.** Not badly — just visibly. A frown, a "hmm, but", a slightly faster reply. People calibrate on it within two encounters.
- **In a family company, the reporter also has to sit at dinner.** [[03-the-ceo-core-value-and-the-founder-contracts|Lesson 03]] §4 #8 calls this the dinner-table failure. It applies to feedback more than to anything else.

The result is a leader with an excellent view of what is going well. The mechanisms below are not about being more approachable — approachability is a trait, and traits are not mechanisms. They are about **removing the cost from the person giving the feedback and putting it on a process**.

### 3.2 The five mechanisms that break it

| Mechanism                                            | How to run it                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             | What it catches                                                                                                                                                                                        | Fit at VSYST today                                                                                                                                        |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **A specific question, not an open one**             | Replace "any feedback?" with one narrow, time-boxed, behavioural question per week: _"What did I decide too fast this month?"_ · _"Which of my priorities do you think is wrong?"_ · _"What did you want to say in Tuesday's meeting and didn't?"_ · _"Where am I the bottleneck this week?"_                                                             | Everything, cheaply. This is the highest-yield item on the list and it costs one sentence                                                                                                              | **Now.** Ask one, of one person, every week. Log the answers in the same place each time                                                                  |
| **The skip-level**                                   | Meet the people who report to your direct reports, without those reports present, roughly annually per manager; explain the purpose to the managers first; take notes visibly; share themes with the manager but never attribution ([Kim Scott, Radical Candor — skip-level meetings](https://www.radicalcandor.com/blog/skip-level-meetings/))           | A manager whose team has quietly given up on them — invisible from above, obvious one level down                                                                                                       | **Not yet.** No layer exists. Trigger: the first person who manages another person                                                                        |
| **An anonymous channel that visibly does something** | A form nobody has to sign, reviewed on a fixed date, with the **response published** — "three items came in, here is what we did with each, one of them we disagree with and here is why"                                                                                                                                                                 | Things that cannot be said with a name on them: compensation fairness, a founder's behaviour, a relative's performance                                                                                 | **Set it up at ~8 people.** Before that it is transparently non-anonymous and pretending otherwise damages trust                                          |
| **The exit interview the CEO does not conduct**      | Conducted by the COO or an outsider, 2–4 weeks _after_ the last day, with the transcript to the CEO unedited. ### 3.3 What each one actually costs you

Be honest about the price, because founders who install these and then quietly starve them do more damage than founders who never install them. A skip-level costs a manager's trust if you handle attribution carelessly. An anonymous channel costs your composure the first time it is used to say something unfair. An exit interview costs you a story about why the person left that was more flattering than the truth. And the specific-question habit costs nothing at all except the ten seconds in §3.4 — which is why it is the one to start with.

### 3.4 The ten seconds after — which is the whole skill

Everything above is plumbing. **The entire mechanism lives or dies in the ten seconds after somebody tells you something you did not want to hear**, because that is the interval in which the other person decides, permanently, what honesty costs at this company.

There are exactly three moves available, and two of them close the channel:

| The move          | What it sounds like                                      | What the other person learns                                          |
| ----------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Explain**       | "Right, but the reason I did that was —"                 | Feedback here triggers a debate I will lose. Next time, don't.        |
| **Contextualise** | "That's fair, though you weren't in the IOCL call, so —" | My information is considered incomplete by default. Next time, don't. |
| **Receive**       | "Thank you. Can you give me one more example?"           | It landed. He wrote it down. Next time, tell him earlier.             |

The protocol, and it is four steps long because anything longer will not survive an actual ambush:

1. **Say thank you.** Not "thanks, but". Full stop after "thank you".
2. **Ask exactly one question, and make it a request for more of the same thing** — "when else?" or "can you give me a second example?" Never "who else thinks this?", which turns the conversation into an investigation of the reporter.
3. **Write it down in front of them.** Visibly. This is the single highest-signal act available to you: it says the information has been accepted into the system rather than into an argument.
4. **Say what happens next, with a date.** "I'll think about this and come back to you by Friday." Then come back on Friday, including if the answer is that you disagree.

**On feedback you think is wrong.** You are allowed to disagree; you are not allowed to disagree in the first ten seconds. Take it, sit with it a week, and then reply in writing with your reasoning. Two things follow. Some of it turns out to be right, and the week is what let you see that. And the part that is genuinely wrong is still **true as a perception** — if a competent person watching you closely concluded that, the behaviour reads that way, and the perception is now a fact about your company whatever the intent was.

**On the physiology, briefly.** The reason this is hard is not character, it is that criticism from someone you employ registers as a threat and the threat response is faster than deliberation. The only reliable countermeasure is a script rehearsed before you need it — which is what the four steps are. Rehearse them out loud once. It feels ridiculous and it works.

## 4. Learning as a discipline, not a hobby

### 4.1 The loop

Reading books is not learning; it is input. Learning is a loop, and the loop has to close somewhere that costs you something. The CEO's version:

```
DECIDE     → write the decision, the reasoning, the expected outcome,
             and the review date, BEFORE you know the answer      (C29)
EXPOSE     → put the reasoning where someone can attack it: the
             self-review, the peer group, the advisory board       (§2)
COLLECT    → the specific question, weekly, of one person          (§3.2)
SCORE      → at the review date, grade the DECISION, not the
             OUTCOME — separately                                  (§4.2)
CHANGE     → one named behaviour or one named mechanism per
             quarter. Not five. One.                               (§4.4)
```

Every other input — books, podcasts, courses, conference talks — feeds the DECIDE step and nothing else. That is not a dismissal; it is a filing instruction. **Input without the other four steps produces vocabulary, not judgment**, and vocabulary is what makes a founder sound impressive in a meeting while making the same mistake for the fourth time.

### 4.2 The decision-journal review — the only genuine calibration you have

[[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] and [[02-how-a-ceo-thinks|lesson 02]] §5 own the format; this section owns the **review**, which is the half everybody skips and the only half that calibrates anything. The reason the journal matters is memory: without a contemporaneous written record of what you believed, hindsight silently rewrites it, and you learn nothing except that you were mostly right ([Farnam Street — the decision journal](https://fs.blog/decision-journal/)).

Once a quarter, three hours, alone first and then with the co-founders. Open every entry whose review date has passed and fill four columns:

| Column                  | The question                                               | Why this column and not an easier one                                                    |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Expected**            | What did I write down would happen?                        | Copied verbatim from the entry. No paraphrasing — paraphrase is where hindsight enters   |
| **Actual**              | What happened?                                             | Facts, with a source: the number, the churn, the email                                   |
| **Process score (1–5)** | Given what I knew **then**, was this a good way to decide? | The only score that generalises. A 5 here with a bad outcome is a well-run bet that lost |
| **Outcome score (1–5)** | Did it work?                                               | Kept separate on purpose. Merging the two columns is how founders learn superstitions    |

Then the three questions that produce the actual learning, asked of the whole set rather than of any entry:

1. **Where was I most confident and most wrong?** That pair is your personal bias signature, and it repeats. (Forecasting research shows that calibration — knowing how sure to be — is trainable and separable from raw expertise; see the Good Judgment Project's [track record](https://goodjudgment.com/resources/the-superforecasters-track-record/).)
2. **Which decisions did I take too slowly, and what was the cost of the delay?** Almost always the reversible ones. Cross-check against [[16-decisions-delegation-and-not-being-the-bottleneck|lesson 16]]'s two-way-door rule.
3. **Which entries have no review date, or a review date I have moved twice?** A decision you will not schedule a verdict on is one you have already decided not to learn from.

**Score at least two decisions "process = 2 or worse" every quarter, or the review is not honest.** If a whole quarter of your decisions were well-made, either the quarter contained no real decisions or you are grading yourself. Both are findings.

### 4.3 The honest part: CEO judgment is a wicked learning environment

Deliberate practice — focused, effortful, feedback-rich repetition with a coach — is the best-evidenced route to expertise in domains like chess, music and surgery ([Ericsson, Prietula and Cokely, _The Making of an Expert_](https://hbr.org/2007/07/the-making-of-an-expert), HBR 2007). It is worth knowing that **it does not transfer cleanly to strategic judgment**, and a course that told you otherwise would be selling you something.

The distinction that matters is between _kind_ and _wicked_ learning environments. A kind environment gives immediate, accurate, unambiguous feedback: the note is flat, the move loses the piece, the patient's numbers respond. A wicked one gives feedback that is delayed, noisy, confounded by everything else happening at once, sometimes absent, and occasionally actively misleading — and in wicked environments, experience produces confidence far more reliably than it produces skill. This is the core of the argument that early specialisation and pure deliberate practice are the exception rather than the rule in complex, unpredictable domains ([David Epstein, _Range_](https://davidepstein.com/range/)).

Being a CEO is about as wicked as it gets:

| Property of the feedback       | Why it is wicked here                                                                                                           | The partial countermeasure                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Delayed**                    | A strategy choice made in month 6 returns its verdict in month 24 — after the market, the team and the product have all changed | Write the expected outcome and the review date at decision time (§4.2). It is the only way to hold the question still                                                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| **Confounded**                 | Revenue moved. Was it the pricing decision, the new district, the dealer who referred three friends, or diesel prices?          | Prefer decisions you can attach a **falsifying** observation to ([[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager\|C04]] row 3 of the scorecard) — evidence that would prove you wrong, not evidence that would look consistent with you being right                                                                                                               |
| **Low-n**                      | You will make perhaps a dozen genuinely consequential decisions a year. That is not a training set                              | Borrow other people's n: the peer group, the advisory board, and post-mortems of companies that failed the way you might ([CB Insights — top reasons startups fail](https://www.cbinsights.com/research/report/startup-failure-reasons-top/))                                                                                                     |
| **Survivorship-biased inputs** | Almost everything written about how to be a CEO is written by, or about, the ones it worked for                                 | Weight process advice over outcome stories. Prefer the sources that show their data ([Wasserman](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691158303/the-founders-dilemmas), [Spencer Stuart](https://www.spencerstuart.com/research-and-insight/2025-sp-1500-ceo-transitions-behind-the-ceo-moment)) over the ones that show their journey |
| **Self-serving**               | You control the narrative of your own past decisions, and nobody audits it                                                      | The journal, written before, read aloud after (§2.3)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |

The practical consequence: **do not expect to get better at this the way you got better at writing code.** You will get better at the _process_ — framing, deciding at the right level, writing it down, killing bets on time, hearing bad news early — and the process is what compounds. Raw predictive accuracy about markets will improve much less than it feels like it does. Design your habits for a wicked world: smaller reversible bets, faster falsification, written predictions, and profound suspicion of any lesson you learned from exactly one instance.

### 4.4 The inputs, and what each is actually for

| Input                                             | Cadence that works at VSYST                 | What it is genuinely good for                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          | What it cannot do                                                                                                                                               |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Books, one at a time, with notes in the vault** | One a month, read against a live problem    | Frameworks and vocabulary you can apply to a decision you are holding right now. | **Structured founder programmes**                 | Opportunistic                               | Peer cohort + a forcing function. **The rule that keeps this from becoming another to-do list: one change per quarter.** Read what you like; change one named behaviour or install one named mechanism per quarter, write it in the self-review, and let the next review check whether it stuck. A CEO who is "working on" six things is working on none of them.

## 5. Energy, health and the long game

### 5.1 Why this section is in a business course and not a wellness app

Skip this section and the rest of the lesson does not work. Every mechanism above — the honest self-review, the ten seconds after criticism, the quarterly decision scoring, the discipline to kill a bet you announced — is a demand on the same faculty: deliberate, effortful, non-defensive thinking. That faculty is the first thing that degrades under short sleep, chronic stress and physical decline, and it degrades **without producing the sensation of having degraded**. You do not feel less rational. You feel more certain, more irritable, and more sure that the problem is other people.

So treat this as an operating parameter, not a virtue. The framing this course uses throughout applies here exactly: **a mechanism, not an intention.** "I'll try to sleep more" is an intention. "The laptop is off at 11 and the phone charges in another room" is a mechanism, and it either holds or it visibly does not.

Two boundaries before we start. This section is not medical advice and cannot be — see a doctor. And it deliberately does not repeat [[18-risk-crisis-and-the-hard-things|lesson 18]] §8, which covers what happens to a founder _during_ an acute crisis: the seventy-two hours where sleep is genuinely not available, and how to run yourself through them without making a permanent decision in a temporary state. This section is about the other 350 days, where the damage is slow and therefore invisible.

### 5.2 The specific risk profile of this specific job in this specific country

Generic founder-health advice is written for someone sitting in an office in a temperate city with a gym in the basement. Your job has a different shape: a technical CEO who writes code at a desk, then drives to districts to sell, then eats whatever is available at 10 PM, then handles a rate-window escalation because the confirmation window runs 10 PM–6 AM. Name the actual exposures.

| Exposure                                 | Why this role, specifically                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  | What the evidence says                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          | The countermeasure that survives a real week                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Sitting**                              | A CEO who also ships code is seated for the productive part of the day and seated again for the travelling part                                                                                                                                              | 31% of the world's adults — 1.8 billion people — do not meet the minimum activity threshold, and sedentary behaviour is independently associated with higher mortality and disease risk ([WHO — physical activity](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/physical-activity)). WHO's 2020 guidance for adults is **150–300 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity a week** ([WHO, 2020](https://www.who.int/news/item/25-11-2020-every-move-counts-towards-better-health-says-who)) | 30 minutes of walking, six days, non-negotiable, scheduled like a customer call. Not a gym membership you will use for five weeks                                                                                                                            |
| **Short and shifted sleep**              | The rate-confirmation window is 10 PM–6 AM. Support escalations, deploys and dealer WhatsApp all land in it. HbA1c, lipids, BP, thyroid, Vitamin D, liver. | **Road travel**                          | Selling district by district in Chhattisgarh means highway kilometres, often at night, often driven tired after a full day                                                                                                                                   | MoRTH's _Road Accidents in India 2023_ recorded **172,890 deaths**, the highest ever, with more than two-thirds of victims aged 18–45 ([MoRTH 2023, reported by Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/india-news/india-road-accidents-deaths-injuries-report-road-highway-ministry-nitin-gadkari-125082801527_1.html))                                                                                                                                                           | This is the single largest acute risk in the job and the cheapest to reduce: no self-driving after a full sales day, a rule about night highway travel, and a driver on long district runs costed into the sales motion rather than treated as an indulgence |
| **Ambient chronic stress**               | Pre-revenue, bootstrapped, family capital, three people. There is no month in which nothing is urgent                                                                                                                                                        | WHO classifies burn-out in ICD-11 as an **occupational phenomenon** — exhaustion, mental distance/cynicism about the job, and reduced professional efficacy — resulting from "chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed" ([WHO, 2019](https://www.who.int/news/item/28-05-2019-burn-out-an-occupational-phenomenon-international-classification-of-diseases))                                                                                                             | §5.5, and the observation that burnout's clinical description — cynicism plus reduced efficacy — is indistinguishable from what founders privately call "being realistic about this market"                                                                  |

**The honest reading of that table:** two of the five rows are things a founder can fix in an afternoon by writing a rule down (travel, and who is on call for the rate window). Those are the two to fix first, because they are the two where the downside is not gradual.

### 5.3 Three floors, set once, defended like a release gate

Not a routine. Three numbers, chosen once, and a fourth rule about who enforces them.

| Floor              | The parameter                                                                                        | How you know it broke                       | Who enforces it                                                                                                                                           |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Sleep floor**    | A fixed lights-out, seven days, with a stated exception budget — e.g. four nights a quarter, counted | The exception budget is spent by week three | Your phone's charging location. Willpower at 11:40 PM is not a control                                                                                    |
| **Movement floor** | 30 minutes, six days, any form. The floor is the point, not the optimum                              | Two consecutive weeks below four days       | A calendar block with a name that is not "exercise" — make it a walking phone call with the peer group (§4.4) so missing it costs somebody else something |
| **Hard stop**      | One evening a week and one full day a fortnight with no laptop, no dealer WhatsApp, no code          | You cannot name when the last one was       | The co-founders, in the §2.3 self-review. Put the count in the document                                                                                   |

**The exception budget is the load-bearing idea.** A rule with no exceptions gets broken once and then abandoned entirely, because a broken absolute rule teaches you the rule was fake. A rule with four counted exceptions per quarter survives a genuine crisis, records it, and shows you in the review that you spent six. That is the same design as a service-level budget in engineering, applied to a person.

**And one anti-rule:** do not build a routine that requires the company to be calm. It will not be. Every floor above has to work in the worst week of the quarter or it is decoration.

### 5.4 The loneliness is structural, not personal

There is a particular flavour of isolation attached to this seat, and founders consistently misread it as a personal failing. It is a structural consequence of four facts that are all true at once, and no amount of being a friendly, well-liked person removes any of them.

- **Nobody in the company can be told the whole picture.** The cash number that scares you is the number that would make your first employee start looking. The doubt about the IOCL path is the doubt that would spook the domain director. You are the only node that holds all of it.
- **Everyone you talk to daily is downstream of your decisions.** Your relationship with every employee is mediated by the fact that you set their salary and can end their job. That is not a barrier you can charm your way past.
- **The people who love you cannot follow the detail.** Family will support you and cannot tell you that your diagnosis is stale, because they have no way to evaluate it.
- **In a family company, the two categories collapse.** Your co-founders are your relatives. The one relationship that would normally be "colleague, not family" is both, which means a work disagreement has a second cost at every wedding for the next forty years.

HBR argued more than a decade ago that CEO loneliness is a real and under-acknowledged occupational condition rather than a personality trait ([Saporito, _It's Time to Acknowledge CEO Loneliness_](https://hbr.org/2012/02/its-time-to-acknowledge-ceo-lo), HBR 2012). The reason to name it structurally is practical: **structural problems have structural fixes and personal problems get willpower applied to them.** §2.2's peer group and §5.6's one outside relationship are the fixes. Trying harder to feel less alone is not.

### 5.5 Identity fusion: when "the company" and "I" become one object

There is a research construct for what happens to founders, and it is worth knowing the name. **Identity fusion** describes a state in which group membership stops being something you _have_ and becomes something you _are_ — personal and social identity remain simultaneously active and merged, and the research consistently associates it with willingness to make extreme, costly sacrifices for the group ([identity fusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_fusion), developed by Swann and colleagues).

For a founder that is a double-edged instrument, and the edges are not symmetrical:

| What fusion gives you                                                                                    | What fusion costs you                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| The persistence to keep going through a year with no revenue, which a rational employee would not do     | Every piece of criticism of the company lands as criticism of you. §3.4's ten seconds becomes ten times harder                                                                                                       |
| Credibility with dealers and partners — they can tell the difference between a founder and a salesperson | You cannot kill a product bet, because killing it feels like an admission about yourself rather than a decision about a product ([escalation of commitment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escalation_of_commitment)) |
| A story you can tell without effort ([[12-communication-the-ceo-as-chief-storyteller\|lesson 12]])       | A bad quarter becomes a referendum on your worth as a person, which is not a state anyone makes good capital-allocation decisions in                                                                                 |
| Willingness to do the unglamorous work nobody assigned                                                   | **No fallback identity.** If the company ends — by failure or by sale — there is no remaining answer to "who are you?"                                                                                               |

The diagnostic question is uncomfortable and takes four seconds: **finish the sentence "I am **\_\_**" three times without mentioning VSYST, DZZLO, fuel, software or being a founder.** If you cannot, that is not a character flaw; it is a concentration risk in the same sense as having one customer. And the remedy is the same as for any concentration risk: build a second position before you need it, not after. One thing you are competent at and identified with that the company cannot take away or give back.

### 5.6 One relationship outside the company where the truth can be said

This is the smallest, cheapest, most-skipped item in the whole lesson.

**You need exactly one relationship in which you can say the true thing about the business without managing the listener's reaction.** Not a confidant for feelings — a place where the sentence "I think the IOCL path is a fantasy and I've been pretending otherwise for four months because the alternative is admitting I wasted the year" can be said out loud, first, before it is a decision.

The specification matters more than the person:

| Requirement                               | Why                                                                                         | Who fails it                                                                               |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Outside the cap table and the payroll** | Their livelihood must not move when your answer moves                                       | Co-founders. Employees. Anyone holding equity                                              |
| **Outside the family dinner table**       | Nothing you say may show up at a family function                                            | Relatives, including supportive ones                                                       |
| **Able to follow the detail**             | Must be able to evaluate the claim, not just absorb the distress                            | A friend from school who has never run a company can hold the feeling but not the argument |
| **Under no obligation to be encouraging** | The default social contract with founders is cheerleading, and cheerleading is useless here | Anyone who thinks their job is to keep your morale up                                      |
| **Recurring, scheduled, and durable**     | A relationship you only invoke in a crisis will not be there in a crisis                    | Ad-hoc "let's catch up sometime"                                                           |

In practice, at VSYST, that is one of three: a **founder peer** from §2.2 with whom you have an explicit two-way version of this arrangement; a **paid professional** — coach or therapist — where the confidentiality is contractual and the obligation to flatter you is zero; or a **spouse or partner who is genuinely outside the business** and who you have explicitly told that their job in this conversation is not to reassure you. All three work. Having none of them is the common case and it is a defect in the operating system, not a personality.

### 5.7 Mental health, handled as an operational concern

Handle this the way you would handle a database that is quietly running at 92% disk. It is a system with a measurable state, known failure modes, known remedies, and a cost of ignoring it that is not paid by you alone.

**What the research actually supports, and what it does not.** The best-known study here surveyed 242 entrepreneurs against 93 comparison participants and found **49% of entrepreneurs reporting one or more lifetime mental health conditions**, with higher rates of depression, ADHD, substance use and bipolar diagnosis than the comparison group ([Freeman et al., _Are Entrepreneurs "Touched with Fire"?_](https://michaelafreemanmd.com/Research_files/Are%20Entrepreneurs%20Touched%20with%20Fire-summary.pdf); the peer-reviewed extension is [Freeman et al., _Small Business Economics_, 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11187-018-0059-8)). Be careful with it: the samples are small, self-reported, self-selected and American, and the direction of causation is genuinely unresolved — it is at least as plausible that people with certain traits become founders as that founding produces the conditions. **What the study does not license is "founders are broken."** What it does license is the operational conclusion: at these base rates, planning as though this will never touch your three-person company is planning badly.

**Say the plain thing.** Professional help is a tool, in the same category as hiring a CA because you should not do your own TDS. Nobody thinks retaining a lawyer means you are bad at contracts. A founder who takes a structured six sessions to stop postponing a hard conversation with a co-founder has bought a better company for a few thousand rupees, and a founder who does not is paying for it anyway, in decisions.

**What exists in India, concretely:**

| Resource                                            | What it is                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                | Cost                                  | When to use it                                                                                                                   |
| --------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Tele-MANAS — 14416 / 1-800-891-4416**             | The Government of India's National Tele Mental Health Programme, launched October 2022, 24×7, run through state cells with counsellor-first triage and escalation to psychiatrists, available in English and 20 regional languages ([MoHFW / PIB](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressNoteDetails.aspx?NoteId=153277&ModuleId=3&reg=3&lang=1); [Tele MANAS](https://telemanas.mohfw.gov.in/home)) | Free                                  | Immediately, including at 3 AM, including on behalf of an employee. Put this number in the employee handbook                     |
| **A private psychiatrist or clinical psychologist** | Ordinary outpatient care; Raipur has both, and video consultation removes the geography problem entirely                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  | Per session — **VERIFY LIVE** locally | Anything persistent: sleep that does not return when the crisis ends, sustained loss of interest, a fortnight of not functioning |
| **NIMHANS and the national programme**              | The apex institute, and the National Mental Health Programme framework it anchors ([NIMHANS](https://nimhans.ac.in/))                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     | Public                                | Referral, and for understanding what the system will and will not provide                                                        |
| **The legal frame**                                 | The Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 establishes a right to access mental healthcare and prohibits discrimination — relevant when you write your own HR policy ([PRS Legislative Research — the Mental Health Care Bill, 2013, which became the Act](https://prsindia.org/billtrack/the-mental-health-care-bill-2013))                                                                         | —                                     | Before you write any leave or insurance policy that touches this. **VERIFY LIVE** with the CS                                    |
| **A coach**                                         | A different tool for a different job — behaviour and process, not distress (§4.4)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         | Paid or peer-swapped                  | The delegation you keep not doing; the conversation you keep postponing                                                          |

**The three signals worth acting on**, stated as thresholds rather than feelings, because feelings are exactly what is unreliable here: sleep does not recover within a week of the crisis ending; you have stopped being able to make reversible decisions at all, which shows up as an unusually long list of open items in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]]; or somebody outside the company has said something to you twice. Any one of them is a reason to make an appointment, not a reason to try harder.

### 5.8 The annual reading

Once a year, on a fixed date, in the same week as the two-week test in §6 — because both are instrument readings and both get skipped for the same reason.

1. **A full blood panel and a physical.** Written result, filed. Not "I feel fine".
2. **The four floors, scored honestly** — sleep, movement, hard stop, exception budget spent. Numbers, in the §2.3 self-review.
3. **The three-sentence identity test from §5.5.**
4. **One question to the person in §5.6:** "What have you watched me do this year that you'd tell me to stop?"

Four items, one morning, once a year. The company's servers get more attention than this. That is the whole argument.

## 6. The two-week test, CEO edition

### 6.1 The COO's test proves the machine runs. It does not prove anything about you

[[20-the-autopilot-test-and-scaling-the-machine|COO lesson 20]] sets the operating system's definition of done: the COO disappears for two weeks and the machine keeps running without heroics — orders dispatch, invoices raise, the close happens, the compliance calendar clears, escalations get handled by whoever owns them. That test measures **task continuity**, and it is the right test for that job.

Run the same fortnight on the CEO and you will get a misleadingly good result. Nothing stalls. Orders still flow, code still ships, dealers still get onboarded, because none of that was ever routed through you. You come back, everything is fine, and you conclude the company is resilient.

**It isn't, and the test measured the wrong thing.** The CEO is not in the task path. The CEO is in the **decision path**. So the CEO's version of the test asks one question and ignores everything else:

> **In fourteen days with the CEO genuinely unreachable, what accumulated in the queue that nobody was willing to decide?**

A company where nothing stalls but eleven decisions are waiting on your return is not a functioning company. It is a company that has been holding its breath. And the fortnight only revealed it — the queue forms every single week, silently, in units of two or three days, and gets absorbed into "we'll ask him when he's free."

The engineering analogy is exact and worth keeping in mind, because you are a technical CEO and it will make you take this seriously: this is a **game day**. You do not learn whether a system survives the loss of a component by reasoning about it; you remove the component on purpose, at a time you chose, with everyone watching, and you find out ([Principles of Chaos Engineering](https://principlesofchaos.org/); [Netflix Chaos Monkey](https://netflix.github.io/chaosmonkey/)). The CEO is a single point of failure with a [bus factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bus_factor) of one for an entire category of decision. §8 asks what happens if that component never comes back. §6 asks the cheaper question first: what happens if it is merely gone for a fortnight?

### 6.2 What to instrument before you leave

If you leave without instrumentation you will come back to anecdotes, and anecdotes about your own indispensability are the least reliable data you will ever collect. Set up five things. It takes ninety minutes.

| Instrument                        | What it is, concretely                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     | Why it is required                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **The decision queue**            | One shared page — a vault note, a pinned WhatsApp message, a sheet. Anyone who thinks "we should ask him" writes a row instead: **date · question · who wanted it · what they'd have done if forced · did they in fact do it**                             | This is the entire measurement. Without it you get "everything was fine" from people who are being kind                                                                                                                                                         |
| **The standing authorities note** | A one-page written statement of who may decide what while you are gone, **with dated rupee limits**: spend up to ₹X without asking; discount up to Y%; sign a dealer contract on the standard template; hire a contractor; make a public statement, or not | Ambiguity does not produce autonomy, it produces waiting. If you have not written the limit down, the safe move for a loyal person is always to wait                                                                                                            |
| **The break-glass rule**          | Exactly two or three named conditions that justify contacting you, each with a named person authorised to make the call. E.g. a security incident, a bank or statutory notice with a deadline inside the fortnight, or a death or hospitalisation          | Without it, either nobody contacts you when they should, or someone contacts you on day two about a pricing question and the experiment is over                                                                                                                 |
| **A written pre-registration**    | Before leaving, you privately write your prediction: which three decisions you expect to queue, and who you expect to freeze. Sealed, in the vault, dated                                                                                                  | This is the [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log\|C29]] discipline applied to the test itself. Comparing your prediction to the actual queue is where the learning is — being wrong about _who_ freezes is the most useful finding in the whole exercise |
| **The outside-notification list** | Who is told you are unreachable and who is not. Co-founders and team: told, with the reason. Dealers, IOCL, Easebuzz, the CA and the CS: told only that a named person is the contact for the period. Never "the CEO is unavailable" to a counterparty     | A partner who hears "he's away for two weeks" reads it as instability. A partner who hears "for the next fortnight, please deal with X on this" reads it as a functioning company                                                                               |

### 6.3 The protocol

Fourteen days. The number matters — a week is short enough that a competent, loyal team will simply hold everything, and holding for five working days costs almost nothing. The second week is where the queue becomes intolerable and someone decides. **The test lives in the second week.**

1. **Pick the date sixty days out and put it in the plan.** Not a quiet month. A month with real decisions in it — a pricing question live, a hire in progress, a partner conversation open. A fortnight scheduled into a dead patch tests nothing.
2. **Write the standing authorities note and read it aloud** to the co-founders and the team. Every limit spoken, every number stated. Then ask each person to say back, in their own words, one decision they are now allowed to make that they would previously have asked you about. If nobody can produce an example, the note is too vague — rewrite it.
3. **Set the unreachability specification honestly.** Phone off or handed over; no email; no dealer WhatsApp; **not** "I'll only check once a day." Once a day is not a test — it is a slower version of normal operation, and it lets you supply the missing decisions in a way you will not notice you are supplying.
4. **Name one deputy for the seat**, not for the tasks. Usually the domain-expert director. Their job is not to do your work; it is to say "we're deciding this ourselves" when the queue starts forming, and to keep the queue page honest.
5. **Go.** Physically elsewhere if possible; travel is not required, invisibility is.
6. **Come back and do not open the queue for one day.** Sleep first. Reading the queue jet-lagged and defensive is how the test produces a fight instead of a finding.
7. **Debrief in two sittings.** Sitting one, alone with the queue page and your sealed prediction. Sitting two, with the co-founders and the team, using §6.4's table. Both sittings produce written output; the second one produces changes to [[C03-decision-rights-matrix|C03]].

### 6.4 How to read the result

Sort every queue row into one of five buckets. The buckets are the finding — the raw count is not.

| What queued                                                                                                                                        | What it actually means                                                                                                                                                                              | The fix, and where it lives                                                                                                                                                              |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Genuinely irreversible, high-stakes, yours** — a partnership commitment, a founder-level equity change, a strategy pivot, a statutory signature  | **Correct.** This is the residue that should queue. A CEO who returns to a queue of _zero_ has either delegated the vision away or scheduled the fortnight into an empty month                      | Nothing. Celebrate this row and count it                                                                                                                                                 |
| **Reversible, but nobody had a stated limit** — a ₹40,000 spend, a 12% discount, a contractor for a month                                          | The **standing authorities note was incomplete**, which means [[C03-decision-rights-matrix\|C03]] is incomplete. Not a people problem; a document problem                                           | Add the limit to C03 permanently, with a number. [[16-decisions-delegation-and-not-being-the-bottleneck\|Lesson 16]]                                                                     |
| **Reversible, a limit existed, they still waited**                                                                                                 | A **trust or safety problem**, and it is yours. Somewhere in the last year, someone decided inside their authority and you second-guessed it publicly. People remember the one time, not the policy | Find the incident. Name it out loud. Then let the next three decisions inside authority stand even when you would have chosen otherwise — §10's fifth criterion                          |
| **Information-blocked** — they could not decide because only you had the number, the context, the history or the relationship                      | A **knowledge concentration** problem, and the direct precursor to §8's bus-factor risk. Every row here is something that is not written down                                                       | Write it down this week: the relationship map ([[14-partnerships-ecosystem-and-founder-relationships\|lesson 14]]), the pricing rationale, the reason a clause is in the dealer contract |
| **Should never have reached the CEO at all** — an operating question in the COO's domain that got escalated because escalating to you is the habit | A **routing** problem. The org has learnt that the shortest path to an answer runs through the founder, which will not scale past ten people                                                        | Route it back visibly, in public, once. "This is X's call and I'm not going to override it." [[COO-Docs/index\|COO course]] owns the SOP                                                 |

Then three questions over the whole set:

1. **How long did the first genuine decision wait before someone took it anyway?** If the answer is "day eleven", your delegation works only under duress. If the answer is "nobody did, in fourteen days", the delegation in [[16-decisions-delegation-and-not-being-the-bottleneck|lesson 16]] exists on paper and nowhere else.
2. **Who decided, and was it the person the authorities note named?** Frequently it is not — an unofficial deputy emerges, and finding out who your organisation actually trusts is worth the fortnight on its own. Whoever it was is a genuine finding for §7 and §8.
3. **How many rows were decided _and never told you about_?** Count these separately and treat a high count as the single best result in the test. A decision taken, executed and simply reported as history is the thing you are trying to manufacture.

**One rule about the debrief that determines whether you ever get an honest queue page again:** you may not, in the debrief, overturn a decision that was taken inside a stated authority — even one you would have made differently, even one that turned out badly. Explain what you would have weighed, ask them to weigh it next time, and let the decision stand. Overturn one and the next test's queue page will be blank, not because nothing queued but because nobody will write it down.

### 6.5 The failure modes, named in advance

| Failure mode            | What it looks like                                                                                             | Why it happens                                                                                                                                   |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **The fake fortnight**  | Checking WhatsApp "just for the dealer group", answering one email a day, one call "because it was IOCL"       | The founder cannot tolerate not knowing. This is §5.5's identity fusion showing up as an operating behaviour                                     |
| **The dead month**      | Scheduling it into Diwali week or a month with nothing live                                                    | Optimising for a good result instead of a true one. Every instinct will push you here                                                            |
| **The proxy CEO**       | Appointing someone to make all your decisions in your absence, so no queue forms                               | Measures whether one person can substitute for you, which is a different and much easier test. The queue is supposed to distribute, not transfer |
| **The heroic team**     | Everybody works twenty hours a day so that nothing visibly slips, and nobody writes anything on the queue page | Same failure the COO course names: heroics disguising a broken mechanism. Ask explicitly in the debrief, "what did you cover for?"               |
| **The unrepeated test** | Doing it once, getting a bad result, feeling bad, and never doing it again                                     | The number that matters is not this year's queue but the difference between this year's and next year's                                          |

### 6.6 What VSYST can actually do this year

Two weeks, three people, pre-revenue, in a company where the CEO also writes the code. The honest answer is that the full test is not runnable yet — and the honest answer is not "so skip it". It is a staircase:

| Stage                         | Version of the test                                                                                                                                                                                                                           | Trigger                                                                                                                         |
| ----------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Now — three founders**      | **The silent week.** Five working days in which you attend nothing, decide nothing and answer nothing, but stay physically present. The queue page runs. This is embarrassing in a good way: you will watch people walk to your desk and stop | Immediately. It costs one week and needs no hires                                                                               |
| **First non-founder hire**    | The silent week becomes a real week away, with a written standing-authorities note                                                                                                                                                            | Month 1 of that person's employment. Doing it early sets the norm before habits form                                            |
| **~5–8 people, revenue live** | The full fourteen days, instrumented as §6.2, once a year, on a fixed date, in a month with real decisions in it                                                                                                                              | Once the dealer base is large enough that a fortnight of your absence has a measurable revenue consequence — which is the point |
| **A COO exists**              | Yours and the COO's fortnights are **deliberately not the same fortnight**, and each of you debriefs the other                                                                                                                                | The COO hire, [[10-building-the-team-hiring-equity-and-firing\|lesson 10]]                                                      |

**Before any of them, do the ten-minute version today.** Write down the five decisions you believe only you can make. Then, against each, write the name of the person who would decide it if you were unreachable for a fortnight, and the limit inside which they may. Any row where that second column is blank is a live bus-factor item, and it belongs in §8's document — not next year, this week. The seven levels of delegation ([Management 3.0 — Delegation Poker](https://management30.com/practice/delegation-poker/)) are a useful vocabulary here: for each row, are you at _tell_, at _consult_, at _advise_, or at _delegate_? Most founders discover they are at _consult_ for almost everything, and _consult_ is indistinguishable from _tell_ whenever the founder is unavailable.

## 7. Growing into each stage, or not

### 7.1 The pattern, stated without euphemism

The skills that take a company from nothing to its first ₹1 crore of annual revenue are not the skills that take it to ₹10 crore, and are close to irrelevant at ₹100 crore. This is not a criticism of founders; it is a property of organisations. Greiner's model has each phase of growth ending in a crisis that can only be resolved by a **different kind of leadership** than the one that produced the phase ([Greiner, HBR](https://hbr.org/1998/05/evolution-and-revolution-as-organizations-grow)). Bain's twenty-year, 8,000-company study of the "founder's mentality" says the same thing from the other end: growth creates complexity, complexity is the silent killer of growth, and the crises arrive at **predictable** stages ([Zook and Allen, _The Founder's Mentality_](https://www.amazon.com/Founders-Mentality-Overcome-Predictable-Crises/dp/1633691160); [Knowledge at Wharton on Zook](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/podcast/knowledge-at-wharton-podcast/160609b_kwradio_zook/)).

The founder-specific version of the trap is worth naming separately, because it does not look like incompetence from the inside. It looks like **doing more of what worked**. The Peter Principle's cynical formulation — people rise to the level of their incompetence — misses the founder case, because a founder does not rise into a new job by being promoted ([Peter principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_principle)). The job rises around them while they stand still, and the transition is announced by nothing at all.

| -------------------- | ------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |

A CEO here who still owns a function is starving another one                                                     | Deciding at a level two rungs below your seat, which feels productive and quietly halts the layer beneath you            |

**And one structural fact this table cannot fix, which belongs in this lesson because it is a _CEO_ problem and not a sales problem.** At roughly ₹60,000 per GSTIN per year, DZZLO is priced in the "rabbit" band of Christoph Janz's framework — the band where the arithmetic assumes tens of thousands of customers acquired mostly through inbound and self-serve — while the go-to-market is sales-assisted, district by district, which is the motion for "deer" and "elephants" at ten to a hundred times the price ([Janz, _Five ways to build a \$100 million business_](https://christophjanz.blogspot.com/2014/10/five-ways-to-build-100-million-business.html)). That mismatch is invisible at ₹0 and fatal somewhere in the ₹1–10 Cr band, and it resolves in exactly one of three ways: price rises, the sales motion gets much cheaper per dealer, or the ambition caps out at a good ₹10 Cr business. **Choosing which is a CEO decision, not a sales decision**, and it is the single most likely reason a founder gets stuck at a stage boundary here. It belongs in [[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager|C04]] and in the next quarterly strategy review.

### 7.3 The self-assessment, at each threshold

Run this on a fixed date once a year and again whenever revenue crosses a stage line. Seven questions, written answers, filed with the §2.3 self-review. The point is not the score; the point is that the answers are in writing where next year's version can be compared to them.

1. **Is the company's constraint still something I am good at?** Write the constraint in one sentence first ([[05-strategy-i-diagnosis-and-the-strategy-kernel|lesson 05]]'s diagnosis), then answer.
2. **What did I personally do this year that only I could have done?** If the honest list is short and mostly relationships, that is fine at ₹0 and a problem at ₹10 Cr.
3. **What am I still doing that a competent hire would do better?** And what is the actual reason it is not hired — cash, or that I enjoy it?
4. **Which of last year's five biggest decisions would a better CEO have made differently?** Use the scored [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] entries, not memory.
5. **Am I learning at the rate the company is growing?** Concretely: name three things you can do now that you could not do twelve months ago. If you cannot name three, the answer is no.
6. **Would I hire me for this job, as it will exist eighteen months from now?** Write the job description for that future seat first, then answer it as if you were reading your own CV cold.
7. **What would I have to be true for me to hand this seat over, and is any of it true yet?** If the answer has been "nothing has changed" for three consecutive years, that is itself the finding.

### 7.5 The argument on the other side, fairly stated

There is a real case for founders staying, and it is not sentimental.

| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Founders hold irreplaceable accumulated knowledge, moral authority to abandon their own assumptions, and a time horizon no hired executive has ([Horowitz, _Why We Prefer Founding CEOs_](https://a16z.com/why-we-prefer-founding-ceos/))                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      | Founders systematically overweight their own indispensability, and the seat's demands change faster than most people do (§7.1)                                                                                                  | Both true. The question is never "founders vs professionals" in the abstract, only "this founder, this stage"                                                                 |
| Founder-led companies materially outperform on shareholder returns over long horizons in Bain's data, and the small minority retaining all three founder's-mentality traits account for an outsized share of net value creation ([Forbes India on Zook](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/rotman/the-power-of-the-founders-mentality-chris-zook-of-bain-co/50743/1); [Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/podcast/knowledge-at-wharton-podcast/160609b_kwradio_zook/)) | The same research says the traits, not the person, are what outperform — and traits can be lost while the person stays                                                                                                          | The finding is about **founder's mentality**, not about founders. **The house call for VSYST.** For the next three to five years the founder-CEO is almost certainly right for the seat, for a specific and defensible reason: the company's binding constraints are product-market fit in a domain the founders know and nobody else does, founder-led sales, and capital discipline — all three of which are founder work. **What the founder must not do is treat that as permanent.** The commitment this course asks for is narrower and harder than "step aside one day": run §7.3 honestly every year, write the answers down, and let three consecutive years of the same answer to question 5 mean something.

### 7.6 If the assessment says you have stopped growing

Three responses, and only the third is dramatic. Most founders assume it is the only one, which is exactly why the assessment gets avoided.

1. **Grow into it.** Specific, dated, one thing at a time — the §4.4 rule of one change per quarter, aimed at the named gap rather than at self-improvement in general.
2. **Hire around it.** Bring in the function you are weak at and give it genuine authority. This is the standard resolution and it is not a defeat: a CEO who cannot sell hires a sales leader; a CEO who cannot run an operating system hires a COO. The failure mode is hiring the person and keeping the decisions.
3. **Hand over the seat.** §8(c). Rare, correct sometimes, and vastly better done deliberately at a time of your choosing than under duress at a time of someone else's.

## 8. Succession, seriously

"Succession planning" sounds like something a listed company does with a consultant. It is three different problems with three different urgencies, and founders conflate them, decide the whole subject is premature, and do none of it. Separate them and the first one turns out to be urgent, cheap and doable this week.

|                                   | (a) Bus-factor succession                                                                                        | (b) Role succession                                                            | (c) Seat succession                                                    |
| --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **The question**                  | If the CEO is unavailable **tomorrow**, can the company function, pay people and meet its statutory obligations? | Which of the CEO's functions moves to whom, and when?                          | Should this person still be CEO, and if not, how is the handover done? |
| **Horizon**                       | Tomorrow                                                                                                         | This year and next                                                             | Years 3–15, or never                                                   |
| **Trigger**                       | None. It is already live                                                                                         | Each hire                                                                      | §7.3's assessment, or a stage boundary, or a life event                |
| **Cost to prepare**               | One day, roughly ₹0                                                                                              | An afternoon per function                                                      | Months, and it is mostly emotional                                     |
| **How often it is actually done** | Almost never                                                                                                     | Piecemeal and unwritten                                                        | Rarely, and usually too late                                           |
| **Lives in**                      | [[C30-pre-mortem-crisis-playbook-and-succession\|C30]] succession section                                        | [[C03-decision-rights-matrix\|C03]] + [[C01-ceo-charter-and-90-day-plan\|C01]] | Board minutes ([[C17-board-pack-agenda-and-minutes\|C17]])             |

### 8.1 (a) Bus-factor succession — the document the company needs tomorrow

A three-person company with one technical CEO who holds the AWS root account, the DSC, a bank mandate, the IOCL relationship and the entire pricing rationale in his head has a [bus factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bus_factor) of one across several unrelated axes at once. The point of this document is not death. It is **any two-week unavailability you did not schedule** — an accident on a district highway (§5.2), a hospitalisation, an arrest, a family emergency in another state, a phone stolen with the authenticator app on it.

Write it once, in one sitting, and store it in two places: the vault, and a physical sealed envelope with the Company Secretary, updated every six months on the same date as the §2.3 self-review.

**Section 1 — Statutory and corporate.** Every one of these is a **VERIFY LIVE with the CS** item, because they are exactly the provisions that change and exactly the ones where getting it wrong is personal to the directors.

| Item                                | The thing to record                                                                                                                                                                                                       | The provision or fact behind it                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Minimum board**                   | Can the company still act if one director is unavailable? A private company must have **at least two directors**, and at least one director must have stayed in India **182 days or more** in the previous financial year | Companies Act 2013 §149(1) and §149(3) ([CAIRR — §149](https://ca2013.com/149-company-to-have-board-of-directors/)). With three founder-directors, VSYST has one director of headroom and no more — **VERIFY LIVE**                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| **Board-meeting quorum**            | What quorum your Articles require, and whether two directors can validly pass a resolution without the third                                                                                                              | Your AoA + the Act. Ask the CS to write the answer in one sentence and put it in the document                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |

- **The one-page "first 72 hours" runbook**: who convenes, who tells the team, who tells the dealers and in what words, who tells IOCL, and who is the single acting decision-maker until the board meets. [[C24-crisis-comms-and-statement-kit|C24]] owns the wording.

**Read it aloud to the other two directors, once, when it is done.** A document nobody has read is not a control. This is the same rule as §2.3, applied to a different artefact.

### 8.2 (b) Role succession — handing over functions one at a time

The realistic pattern is not one dramatic handover; it is a decade of small ones, each triggered by a hire. Two rules make the difference between a handover and an abdication.

**Rule one: hand over the decision plus the limit, not the task.** Giving someone the work while keeping the decision produces a person who does your job and cannot finish it. §6.4's second and third buckets are what that looks like when measured.

**Rule two: the artefact must exist before the person arrives.** You cannot hand over pricing to a hire if the reason for the pricing is unwritten. The template exists precisely so the handover transfers judgment and not just tasks.

### 8.3 (c) Seat succession — handing over the CEO role

**When.** Not "when you get bored". Four honest triggers: three consecutive years of the same answer to §7.3 question 5; a stage boundary the company keeps failing to cross and the constraint is demonstrably you; a change in what you want your life to be, stated plainly rather than dressed up as strategy; or a health or family reality that makes the seat unsustainable. Ram Charan's long-standing complaint is that succession is treated as an event rather than a process, and that companies with no meaningful plan discover this at the worst possible moment ([_Ending the CEO Succession Crisis_](https://hbr.org/2005/02/ending-the-ceo-succession-crisis), HBR 2005). The transition data at the top of the market is a reminder that these are routine, planned, minuted events in well-run companies ([Spencer Stuart — CEO transitions](https://www.spencerstuart.com/research-and-insight/2025-sp-1500-ceo-transitions-behind-the-ceo-moment)).

**How it is done well.** Six things, in order:

1. **The board decides, and it is minuted.** Even a three-person family board. A CEO change that happens in a conversation and never appears in the minutes is not a governance act; it is a rumour with consequences ([[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties|lesson 09]], [[C17-board-pack-agenda-and-minutes|C17]]).
2. **Name the successor and the date, together.** An unnamed date produces a lame-duck CEO and a paralysed company.
3. **Overlap deliberately, and bound it.** Three to six months with the outgoing CEO in a defined, _narrower_ role, ending on a stated day.
4. **Hand over the relationships in person.** Every counterparty in §8.1's map hears it from you, before they hear it anywhere else, with the new person in the room.
5. **Write down what the outgoing founder may and may not do afterwards.** This is the clause everyone skips and it is where family transitions fail: which decisions the founder no longer has, and what happens when an employee walks past the new CEO to the founder's desk. The answer must be "the founder walks them back", and it must be written down _before_ the first time it happens.
6. **Do the statutory part properly.** Director appointments, resignations, designation changes, KMP if applicable, DIR-12 and any bank-mandate and DSC consequences. **VERIFY LIVE with the CS on every statutory director or KMP change** — the forms, the timelines and the thresholds are the most-amended part of the Act and this course will not be current by the time you need it.

**What happens to the founder afterwards.** The honest options are a handful, and pretending there are more is how founders end up in a role that damages the company:

| Afterwards                                                                  | Works when                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  | Fails when                                                                                                                                  |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Chairman / board seat only**                                              | The founder genuinely wants governance rather than operations, and the boundary is written                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  | The founder keeps a desk in the office and an opinion on everything. In a family firm this is the default outcome unless actively prevented |
| **A narrower operating role** — CTO, chief product officer, chief scientist | The founder's comparative advantage was always the craft, not the seat. Zoho's Sridhar Vembu handed the group CEO role to co-founder Shailesh Kumar Davey in January 2025 and moved to chief scientist, explicitly to work on deep R&D ([Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/companies/news/sridhar-vembu-steps-down-as-zoho-ceo-shailesh-kumar-davey-to-succeed-125012701252_1.html); [Zoho Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoho_Corporation)) | The narrower role is a face-saving fiction and the founder still runs the company through it                                                |
| **Out entirely**                                                            | The founder has a next thing, and §5.5's identity question has an answer that is not this company                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           | There is no next thing. Then "out entirely" lasts four months                                                                               |
| **Shareholder only**                                                        | Ownership and management have been genuinely separated, with the governance to match                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        | The family treats shareholding as continuing management authority — which is the specific failure mode of Indian family firms               |

**The family-business dimension, which is the one that actually applies here.** VSYST is family-founded with three founder-directors, and in family firms succession is typically _assumed_ rather than _decided_. India is measurably worse than the global average on both halves of this: PwC's family business research finds only about a fifth of Indian family businesses have a robust, documented and communicated succession plan, that a larger share of Indian firms than global peers lack a clear plan at all, and that senior-generation resistance to transitioning leadership runs at roughly twice the global rate ([PwC India — family businesses](https://www.pwc.in/assets/pdfs/services/private-and-entrepreneurial-services/family-businesses-the-evolving-bharat-story.pdf); [PwC's 12th Family Business Survey](https://www.pwc.in/services/entrepreneurial-and-private-business/pwcs-12th-family-business-survey.html)) — **VERIFY LIVE**, these numbers move with each survey edition. The related governance finding is in §2.2: a large share of Indian family businesses run family-only boards with no cross-industry representation ([Forbes India on PwC](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/news/indian-family-businesses-high-on-ambition-low-on-governance/2991239/1)).

Three rules that prevent the assumed succession:

- **Separate the three claims explicitly, in writing: ownership, board seat, and executive job.** A family member may inherit shares without inheriting a directorship, and hold a directorship without holding an operating role. Conflating them is the root of most family-business failure, and the place to write the separation down is the founders' agreement ([[C02-founders-agreement-and-vesting-checklist|C02]]) while everyone is still friendly.
- **Write the criteria for the CEO seat before you know who the candidate is.** Written first, applied later — the same principle as [[C19-executive-hiring-scorecard-and-loop|C19]]'s scorecard, and for the same reason: criteria written after the candidate is known are not criteria.
- **Say out loud, once, in a board meeting, and minute it: "the CEO seat is not hereditary at this company."** If that sentence is untrue, then say the true one instead and let everyone plan accordingly. What destroys family firms is not either policy; it is the ambiguity.

**The board's obligation in all three cases.** Even a board of three family directors owes the company the duty in §166 to act in good faith in the best interests of the company and to exercise due and reasonable care ([[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties|lesson 09]], [[C18-director-duties-and-governance-checklist|C18]]). Concretely, at VSYST, that reduces to three minuted items a year: confirm the bus-factor document exists and has been refreshed; confirm each director holds a current DSC and has filed a share nomination; and confirm there is a named acting decision-maker for an unplanned CEO absence. Three lines in the minutes. There is no defensible reason for them to be missing.

## 9. Exits, and the honest conversation about them

The reflex in a bootstrapped family company is that this section does not apply. Nobody is selling. There is no investor with a fund life to satisfy, no liquidation preference, no board member asking about "the path to liquidity". The founders intend to run this thing for twenty years. So the exit conversation gets postponed, which feels like modesty and is actually the mistake.

Here is why it is a mistake. **Outcome shape is not decided at the end. It is decided continuously, by accident, through a hundred small choices made now** — what you promise employees, whose money you take and on what terms, whether the code is legally owned by the company or by a person, whether the dealer contracts can be assigned to someone else, whether the books would survive a stranger reading them, whether the company can operate without you for a fortnight. Every one of those is being decided this quarter whether or not you have named the shape. "We'll figure it out later" does not defer the decision; it hands the decision to the defaults, and the defaults are wrong for most shapes.

There is a second reason, and in a family firm it is the bigger one. **Each of the three of you already has a private answer to "what is this company for in ten years", and none of you has said it out loud.** One of you may be building a permanent business to hand to children. One may be building an asset to sell at forty. One may not have thought about it and will discover their answer only when a number is on the table. That divergence costs nothing today and costs everything in year four, in the week an offer arrives, when it turns out you were building three different companies in the same office.

So: no deck, no banker, no valuation. Just a named shape, written down, reviewed once a year, and the preparation that shape requires.

### 9.3 What each shape does to the ESOPs promised in lesson 10

[[10-building-the-team-hiring-equity-and-firing|Lesson 10]] tells you to use equity to hire people you could not otherwise afford. That is correct advice and it comes with an obligation this course has to discharge: **an ESOP is an option, and an unexercised option in an unlisted Indian private company is worth exactly zero until either a liquidity event occurs or the company buys it back.** Everything below follows from that one sentence.

After a term sheet arrives it is the buyer's clause, not yours, and the buyer's default is the one that costs them least                                                                     |
| **B′. Strategic — asset sale** | The business is sold; the shell company survives holding the cash. Options are options _on the shell_. If the scheme does not address an asset sale, holders can watch the company be sold and receive nothing | Write the asset-sale case into the scheme explicitly. This is the most common nasty surprise in the whole subject                                                                                                                                         |
| **C. Acqui-hire**              | Usually nothing for non-founding holders. | **D. Cash business**           | Nothing. Then there is the tax, which is where good intentions turn into a bill an employee cannot pay. Indian ESOPs are taxed **twice**: once as a perquisite at **exercise**, on fair market value minus exercise price, taxed as salary with TDS by the employer, and again as **capital gains** on eventual sale ([Treelife — ESOP taxation in India](https://treelife.in/taxation/esop-taxation-in-india/)). The cruelty is in the first one: the employee owes real cash tax at exercise on a paper gain in a company whose shares they cannot sell to anybody.

There is relief, and it is narrow. Section **192(1C)** lets an _eligible start-up_ — one that holds DPIIT recognition **and** an **80-IAC** certificate, not merely DPIIT recognition — defer the TDS on the ESOP perquisite until the earliest of a period after allotment, the sale of the shares, or the employee leaving ([TaxGuru — TDS on ESOP perquisite by eligible start-ups](https://taxguru.in/income-tax/tds-esop-prerequisite-eligible-start-ups.html); [Treelife](https://treelife.in/taxation/esop-taxation-in-india/)). Note what it is not: it is a **deferral, not an exemption**, and the rate is fixed by the exercise-year position rather than the payment-year one.

**VERIFY LIVE with the CA before you rely on any of that, and treat this paragraph as out of date on principle.** Two live reasons: the deferral period and the eligibility conditions have both moved more than once, and the Income-tax Act, 1961 has been replaced by the Income-tax Act, 2025 with effect from 1 April 2026, so section numbers cited anywhere — including in this course — need re-checking against the current Act ([CAclubindia](https://www.caclubindia.com/articles/esop-for-foreign-owned-indian-subsidiaries-tax-deferral-extended-to-60-months-in-2026-55829.asp)). Section references are the fastest-rotting sentences in an Indian business course.

One more, specific to shape A. If your liquidity mechanism is a **buyback**, understand what changed: for buybacks on or after **1 October 2024**, the entire consideration is treated as a **deemed dividend in the shareholder's hands**, taxed at their slab rate, with TDS by the company, and the cost of acquisition is treated as a capital loss rather than netted off ([Treelife — buyback tax](https://treelife.in/taxation/buyback-tax-in-india/); [Mondaq — the new tax landscape for buy-backs](https://www.mondaq.com/india/shareholders/1511692/the-new-tax-landscape-for-buy-backs-implications-of-the-finance-no-2-act-2024); [EquityList](https://www.equitylist.co/blog-post/buyback-shares-tax-india)). For a bootstrapped company that intended buybacks as its only route to shareholder liquidity, that is a material change to the plan, and it is the sort of thing that quietly invalidates a promise made three years earlier. **VERIFY LIVE with the CA** — this is precisely the kind of provision that moves with each Finance Act.

### 9.4 The one real data point, and the rule against fantasy valuations

Founders' private valuations are anchored on headlines about companies with completely different economics, and the anchoring is almost entirely unconscious. So here is one concrete, verifiable anchor for a company shaped roughly like the one VSYST wants to be.

**Wingify.** Delhi. Founded 2010. Bootstrapped, no external capital at any point. Built VWO into a profitable global product with over 6,000 customers and roughly **\$50M annualised revenue**. In January 2025, Everstone acquired a majority stake at about **\$200M**, with the founder retaining a board seat and shareholding and the CEO and leadership team retaining substantial equity ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/01/23/everstone-acquires-bootstrapped-indian-startup-wingify-for-200m); [Businesswire — Everstone](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250124810184/en/Everstone-Capital-Acquires-Majority-Stake-in-Wingify-Strengthens-Its-Tech-Investment-Portfolio); [Entrackr](https://entrackr.com/news/everstone-to-acquire-paras-chopras-wingify-for-200-mn-8647840)).

Sit with those numbers rather than the multiple. **Fifteen years. Global customers. Profitable. A category-defining product.** That is what it took, and the outcome was a majority-stake PE deal rather than a headline strategic acquisition — and it is, by any honest measure, one of the great Indian bootstrapped outcomes. Anything you privately imagine for a three-person, pre-revenue, single-district company should be calibrated against that, and the calibration should be humbling in a useful way.

Three rules follow:

1. **The only valuation that exists is one somebody actually paid.** Everything else is an opinion with a rupee sign attached. Do not put a number on the company in your own head, in a founder conversation, or worst of all in a conversation with an employee you are hiring with equity.
2. **If a number must be written, write the assumptions that produce it, and file it in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]]** with a "what would change my mind" line. A number without its assumptions is a wish; a number with them is a testable claim.
3. **Never price the company to an employee.** "The pool is worth ₹X crore" is a sentence that will be quoted back to you, verbatim, by someone who has just resigned. Talk about percentage of the pool, the strike price, the vesting schedule, and the honest sentence about when it becomes money — and nothing else.

The other side of the argument, fairly stated: some experienced operators say you should know your number, because an offer arriving without a pre-formed view leads to an emotional decision under time pressure. That is a real risk and the answer is not a valuation — it is the pre-commitment discipline from [[19-planning-okrs-and-capital-allocation|lesson 19]] §7. Write down, now, in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]], what conditions would make selling right (a named constraint you have failed at for two years, a strategic buyer whose distribution you cannot replicate, a health or family reality). That is what you re-read when an offer lands — not a spreadsheet.

### 9.5 The conversation with your co-founders, who are also your family

Same mechanics as §2.3, because they are the mechanics that work: **written independently first, read aloud second.** One hour, once a year, three questions. Each of you writes your own answers before anyone speaks. The reason for writing first is not ceremony; it is that in a family, the first person to speak sets the range, and the other two adjust toward them without noticing.

1. **In ten years, what do you want your relationship to this company to be?** Owner, operator, both, or neither. One sentence. "Both" is the default answer and is usually the least examined one.
2. **If someone put a number on the table tomorrow, what number would make you say yes?** Not a valuation — a personal number, the one at which your own life changes. The three answers will differ by a factor you will find shocking, and _that gap is the entire finding._ It is not a problem to be resolved on the spot. It is a fact to be known before it matters.
3. **If two of you want to sell and one does not, what happens?** This is the drag-along and tag-along question ([drag-along](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drag-along_right), [tag-along](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tag-along_right)), and the place it belongs is [[C02-founders-agreement-and-vesting-checklist|C02]], written while everyone is friendly and nobody has a number in front of them. **VERIFY LIVE with the CS** on how these clauses interact with your Articles for a private company.

Add the family-specific fourth question if the honest answer to §8.3's hereditary question was not a clean no: **do children have a claim on shares, on a board seat, on a job — and are those three answers the same answer?** They almost never should be.

**The house call for VSYST.** Write **"independent and default-alive"** as the working shape, because it is true today and because it is the only shape that is entirely within your control. Do the common core in §9.2 this quarter. Build the liquidity mechanism into [[C02-founders-agreement-and-vesting-checklist|C02]] before the first ESOP grant, not after. Run the three questions annually with the §2.3 self-review. And keep the other shapes as unexercised options rather than as plans — which is exactly what they are.

## 10. What winning looks like

A founder who never defines done never stops, and — worse — never notices arriving. The absence of a definition is not humility. It is what produces the specific pathology this lesson has been circling for nine sections: a person who has been running hard for six years, cannot say whether it worked, and has substituted motion for evidence because motion at least has a texture.

So here is the definition of done for this course. Six items. Every one of them is **checkable by a stranger** — not felt, not sensed, not "we're in a much better place now". A stranger walks into the office, asks to see six things, and either they exist or they do not.

Notice what the six have in common before you read them. **None of them requires outside capital. None requires a senior hire you cannot afford. None requires luck, a market turn, an OMC deal, or anything a competitor or a customer or a government has to do for you.** All six are within the reach of three people in Raipur inside twenty-four months, on the company's own cash. That is the argument for this particular list over a more impressive one: it is a list you can actually be held to.

### 10.1 The six

| ----- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **1** | **A written strategy that survived a year of contact and one honest revision**                               | [[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager\|C04]] exists in **two dated versions**, a year apart, plus a short written record of what changed, what evidence changed it, and what was abandoned                                                                                                                                                                         | A strategy that never changed. Which means either it was never tested, or it was written vaguely enough that no observation could contradict it. A kernel that survives a year _unedited_ is a warning, not an achievement             | **No.** Version 1 does not exist yet                            |
| **2** | **Enough paying dealers to be default-alive**                                                                | The `Runway & Scenarios` tab of [vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx](toolkit/vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx) shows the company reaching profitability on current revenue and current growth, **without raising anything** ([Paul Graham — Default Alive or Default Dead?](https://paulgraham.com/aord.html))                                                                   | Revenue that looks like traction because it is growing, from a base small enough that any number grows. | **3** | **A board that meets on schedule with real minutes, and one voice in the room that is not family**           | Four dated sets of minutes in twelve months, each recording at least one thing that was _decided_ rather than reported, and at least one recorded disagreement. Nobody's judgment is that good; a clean sheet means the scoring is dishonest, or only easy decisions got logged, or the entries were written after the outcome was known (§4.2)      | **No.** Journal started, not yet a year old, not yet scored     |

Six noes. That is the correct and expected state for a pre-revenue company that is a few months old, and writing them down as noes is more useful than any encouraging paraphrase, because a _no_ is a thing that can become a _yes_ on a date.

### 10.2 What is deliberately not on the list

Every item below is something founders use as evidence of winning. Each is excluded on purpose, and the reason matters more than the exclusion.

| Not on the list                                        | Why not                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **A revenue target beyond default-alive**              | Beyond "the company does not die without permission", revenue is a _choice about shape_ (§9), not a definition of winning. ₹2 Cr with three happy founders and ₹20 Cr with a fractured board are not obviously ranked |
| **Headcount**                                          | The single most misleading pride metric in Indian startups. Headcount is a cost you chose, described as an achievement                                                                                                |
| **Capital raised**                                     | It is a liability with a story attached ([[08-capital-runway-and-fundraising\|lesson 08]]). Announcing it as a win is the industry's most successful category error                                                   |
| **A valuation**                                        | It does not exist until somebody pays it (§9.4)                                                                                                                                                                       |
| **Press, awards, panels, lists**                       | These correlate with a founder's availability for interviews. Nothing else                                                                                                                                            |
| **Being busy, or the founder's own effort**            | Effort is an input. This is a list of outputs, and the whole argument of §6 is that the CEO's effort and the company's health can move in opposite directions                                                         |
| **An app-store rating, download count, or user count** | Vanity in a model where the _dealer_ is the payer and their customers ride free ([[17-the-numbers-a-ceo-watches\|lesson 17]]). Free users are a cost until proven otherwise                                           |
| **The absence of problems**                            | A company with no visible problems has an information problem (§3.1)                                                                                                                                                  |
| **An exit**                                            | It is an event, not a verdict. Companies are sold for reasons that have nothing to do with whether they were run well                                                                                                 |

And one thing that _is_ on the list implicitly, in every one of the six: **an artefact with a date on it.** Every criterion is satisfied by a document a stranger can read, not by a state of mind you can report. That is not bureaucracy. It is the only defence against the specific failure this lesson exists to prevent — a founder grading their own work from memory, in a room with no one who will disagree.

### 10.3 How to score it

Once a year, on a fixed date, filed with the §2.3 self-review and the §7.3 assessment. Fifteen minutes. Three rules:

1. **Binary. Yes with the evidence named, or no.** No partial credit, no "mostly", no "in progress". Partial credit is the mechanism by which founders talk themselves into having done something they have not done, and it is the only rule here that people cheat.
2. **Name the evidence, not the effort.** "Yes — minutes dated 14 Apr, 12 Jul, 9 Oct, 15 Jan; dissent recorded 12 Jul; advisor R.K. present at all four." Not "yes, governance has really improved."
3. **Read the six aloud to the other two directors**, with the evidence, in the same sitting as the self-review. Same reason as everywhere else in this lesson: a claim you have to say to a person's face is a different claim from one you write alone.

Then pick **one** no and make it next year's. Not all of them. The six are ordered roughly by how early they become possible — 1, 5 and 6 are available to VSYST **this year, at three people, pre-revenue, for effectively no money**, which is precisely why they are the ones to start with and precisely why they are the ones founders postpone in favour of the ones that depend on revenue arriving.

### 10.4 What the six add up to

Read them together and they say one thing. **A company that thinks, decides, remembers and continues without its founder in the room.**

Criterion 1 is the company thinking. Criterion 2 is it continuing without permission from anyone. Criterion 3 is it being told the truth by someone who is not related to it. Criterion 4 is it deciding. Criterion 5 is it remembering, in a form that can correct itself. Criterion 6 is the proof of all five at once, under the only test that cannot be faked: absence.

Which is the sentence at the top of this lesson, arrived at from the other end. The COO's definition of done is that the machine runs without heroics ([[20-the-autopilot-test-and-scaling-the-machine|COO lesson 20]]). The CEO's is that **the company outgrows the person who started it, on purpose, while that person is still there and still wants the job.** Doing it on purpose is the whole distinction. It happens either way. The mill in the Explain-it-like-I'm-5 discovered its bus factor in a hospital bed, at the worst possible time, and paid the fee in customers who never came back. The choice this lesson offers is not whether you find out. It is whether you find out on a date you chose.

## 11. A closing note to the reader

This is the last prose in the course, so it should be honest about what a course is.

Twenty lessons have handed you frameworks, tables, statutory references, thirty toolkit templates and a workbook. All of it is scaffolding. **None of it is the job.** It is worth being precise about the gap, because founders who confuse the two spend a year producing excellent artefacts for a company that is quietly dying, and they do it while feeling productive.

**What this course could not teach you.**

**The answer, on your facts.** Every framework here is the _shape_ of a question. Rumelt can tell you what a diagnosis is; nobody can tell you what yours is. Helmer can tell you what a power looks like; only you can look at DZZLO and decide honestly whether it has one or whether you have a preference dressed as a moat. The course gets you to the right question faster. It does not answer it, and it cannot, because it has never met your dealers.

**Timing.** This is the big one, and it is the one nobody admits. Twenty lessons are almost entirely silent on _when_. When to make the first non-founder hire. When to stop a district that is not working, as opposed to giving it one more month. When to say no to a partner who might be the company's making. When to hand over a function, a role, or the seat. Timing is a large fraction of what CEO skill actually is, and it is the least transferable part. It comes from reps, and reps come from time.

**What it feels like.** Reading [[18-risk-crisis-and-the-hard-things|lesson 18]] on letting someone go is not remotely the same as the Tuesday morning you do it, to a person whose children you know, in a city where you will see them at weddings for the next thirty years. Nothing written can carry that weight across. The techniques still work; they will just cost you more than the page suggested.

**Whether you are any good at this.** No course grades you. Only two things have an opinion — the decision journal in §4.2, scored honestly over years, and the six criteria in §10 — and both take a long time to say anything, and both can be gamed by the only person who reads them. That is an uncomfortable design and there is no better one available.

**What your co-founders will do under pressure** — which, here, is what your _family_ will do under pressure. No survey, no framework and no worked example knows. You will find out once, at short notice, and the ground rules you wrote while everyone was calm will be worth more than every other page in this course combined.

**And whether you should be doing this at all.** §7.6 asked it seriously. The honest answer for some readers is no, and a course that never says so is selling something.

**What only doing the job teaches.**

That calibration is a function of trials, not of reading — you cannot become well-judged by consuming better judgment. That most decisions are far smaller than they feel in the moment and a handful are far larger, and you cannot reliably tell which is which from the inside, which is the entire justification for writing them down. That a company becomes what you repeatedly do and never what you announce. That the ratio of problems solved by a mechanism to problems solved by you personally, in your presence, on a phone call, is the number that actually measures whether you are building a company or performing one. And that being liked and being trusted are different, that the second one survives hard decisions, and that founders spend the first two years optimising for the wrong one.

**How to use this thing from here.** Do not re-read it front to back. It was written to be opened at a section when something happens: a partner asks for exclusivity, open [[14-partnerships-ecosystem-and-founder-relationships|lesson 14]]; a co-founder stops delivering, open [[18-risk-crisis-and-the-hard-things|lesson 18]] §5; someone offers money, open [[08-capital-runway-and-fundraising|lesson 08]] before you feel flattered; you cannot say what the company is doing this quarter, open [[19-planning-okrs-and-capital-allocation|lesson 19]]. The glossary, the reading list and every source in the course are collected in [[CEO-Docs/21-reference-glossary-reading-list-and-sources|lesson 21]]. The books in it will teach you more than this course did; this course's contribution was mostly translating them into rupees, into the Companies Act, and into a company of three people in Raipur selling to petrol pumps.

**One last thing, and it is the only claim in twenty lessons with no citation, no table and no template behind it.**

You will be wrong a great deal. Not occasionally — often, about things that mattered, in ways that will be obvious to everyone including you within eighteen months. Every mechanism in this course exists to make that survivable rather than to make it rare, because rare is not on offer. The founders who last are not the ones who were right more often. They are the ones who **found out sooner, said so out loud in a room where it counted, and had built a company underneath them that could absorb it.** That is the whole discipline. The rest is detail, and the detail is in the other twenty lessons.

Nothing in this course happens because you read it.

So do the smallest thing that has a date attached, and do it before you close this file. Open the plan. Pick a fortnight sixty days out, in a month with real decisions in it, and write it down where the other two directors can see it (§6.3, step 1).

Then find out what you have actually built.

## 12. Exercises

Five. Do them in order — 12.1 produces the raw material for 12.4, and 12.2 sets the annual date that 12.5 is filed against. Between them they produce the four artefacts this lesson exists to create: the bus-factor document, the first written self-review, a scheduled and instrumented two-week test, and a filled succession section in [[C30-pre-mortem-crisis-playbook-and-succession|C30]].

**12.1 — The bus-factor succession document (45 min, plus a week of follow-up errands).** Work through §8.1's three sections in one sitting and write down what is _true today_, not what should be true. Section 1, statutory: minimum board, quorum, resignation mechanics, whether each founder has filed an SH-13 share nomination, whether each has a will — mark every unknown as **VERIFY LIVE with the CS** and put the CS call in the calendar before you stop. Section 2, access: DSC holder and expiry for each director, the exact current bank mandate, every root and owner account with who else can reach it, MFA backup codes, the password manager's emergency mechanism, insurance policy numbers. Section 3, in your head only: the relationship map from [[14-partnerships-ecosystem-and-founder-relationships|lesson 14]] with a named second contact for each counterparty, the pricing rationale, the five decisions from §6.6, and the one-page first-72-hours runbook. Expect roughly half the rows to come back blank on the first pass — the blanks _are_ the finding, and each one becomes a task with an owner and a date. **Artefact:** `bus-factor-succession.md` in the vault, dated and versioned, plus a task list of the blanks; then read it aloud to the other two directors before the week ends. Store a copy in a sealed envelope with the CS and set a six-month refresh reminder on the same date as 12.2.

**12.2 — The first written self-review, read aloud (45 min).** Fill §2.3's five-question template for the period since the company started — written alone, in full sentences, before anyone sees it. Question 1 gets DONE / PARTLY / NOT DONE with no explanations attached; the urge to explain is the thing being trained out. Then book thirty minutes with both co-founder-directors and **read it aloud**, in one go, without editing as you speak. Ask each of them for one thing you got wrong in the document itself. Then practise §3.4: say "thank you, let me think about that", write it down, and say nothing else for the rest of the sitting. **Artefact:** a dated `ceo-self-review-<period>.md` in the vault with their two comments appended verbatim, and the next review date fixed six months out in the calendar — the same date that anchors 12.1's refresh, §7.3's assessment and 12.5's scoring.

**12.3 — Schedule the two-week test and build its instrumentation (30 min).** Open the annual plan ([[C28-annual-plan-and-ceo-okr-sheet|C28]]) and pick the fortnight: sixty days out, in a month with real decisions in it, not a dead patch. At VSYST's current size run §6.6's **silent week** version, and schedule it now rather than "after the next release". Then build the five instruments from §6.2 in one sitting: the decision-queue page with its five columns; the standing-authorities note with **dated rupee limits** written in numerals; the break-glass rule with two or three named conditions and a named person authorised to invoke each; your sealed written prediction of which three decisions will queue and who will freeze; and the outside-notification list separating who is told you are away from who is only told who to contact. Read the standing-authorities note aloud and make each person state back one decision they may now make alone — if nobody can, the note is too vague and gets rewritten before you leave. **Artefact:** a date in [[C28-annual-plan-and-ceo-okr-sheet|C28]], a live queue page, the standing-authorities note in the vault, and the sealed prediction filed in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] as a dated entry nobody opens until the debrief.

**12.4 — Fill the succession section of C30 (45 min).** Three blocks, one per horizon in §8. **(a)** Link the document from 12.1 and record its refresh date, plus the named acting decision-maker for an unplanned absence. **(b)** Copy §8.2's role-succession table and fill the two right-hand columns for your company — the trigger in a number, and the artefact that must exist _before_ the handover, per rule two. Mark which of those artefacts do not yet exist; those are next year's writing tasks. **(c)** Write the seat-succession block _before there is any candidate_: the four triggers from §8.3 in your own words, the six-step handover sequence, and — the one that matters most here — the written separation of the three claims: **ownership, board seat, executive job**, with the criteria for the CEO seat drafted while the seat is not vacant. Then take the hereditary question to the next board meeting, decide it either way, and minute the sentence you decided on ([[C17-board-pack-agenda-and-minutes|C17]]). **Artefact:** the completed succession section of [[C30-pre-mortem-crisis-playbook-and-succession|C30]], a follow-on amendment note for [[C02-founders-agreement-and-vesting-checklist|C02]], and one line in the board minutes.

**12.5 — Name the shape, and score the six (45 min).** Two halves, one sitting, and this is the closing exercise of the course. First, run §9.5: each of the three of you writes independent answers to the three questions — ten-year relationship to the company, your personal number, and what happens if two want to sell and one does not — then read them aloud in turn. Do not attempt to resolve the gap between the three answers; record it, dated, and put the drag-along/tag-along question on the [[C02-founders-agreement-and-vesting-checklist|C02]] amendment list with a **VERIFY LIVE with the CS** flag. Write the working shape as one sentence. Second, score §10's six criteria **binary — yes with the evidence named, or no**, no partial credit, read aloud with the evidence. Then pick exactly one _no_ and make it next year's, with a date. **Artefact:** `outcome-shape.md` in the vault holding the three written answers, the gap, and the one-sentence working shape; plus a dated six-row scorecard filed with 12.2's self-review; plus one [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] entry recording which criterion you chose, which you deliberately deferred, and what would change your mind.
