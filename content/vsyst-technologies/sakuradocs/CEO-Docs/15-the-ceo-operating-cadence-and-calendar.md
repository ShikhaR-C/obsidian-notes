# 15 — The CEO's Operating Cadence and Calendar

_Phase 4 · Running the Company and Yourself · Months 6–24. After this lesson you can audit last month's calendar hour by hour against the four CEO jobs and read the result as a strategy diagnosis; state a defensible target time allocation for VSYST at three, ten, thirty and a hundred people; inventory every meeting you attend with an exit criterion for each and decline the rest with a sentence; lay out your own week as named blocks that survive a travel-heavy month; run the monthly, quarterly and annual beats a CEO personally owns; protect thinking time and customer time with mechanisms rather than intentions; and argue about working hours from evidence instead of from folklore._

## Explain-it-like-I'm-5

There is a two-doctor clinic on the main road in a district town. The senior doctor tells everyone — his family, his junior partner, the medical rep who visits on Thursdays — that the future of the clinic is the small pathology lab he is building at the back. Blood work, sugar, lipid panel, thyroid. No one else in the town does it honestly. That is the plan. Everybody has heard the plan.

Now open his appointment register for last month. Twenty-six working days. Two hundred and forty hours in the building. Two hundred and ten of them are OPD — walk-in patients, one after another, from nine in the morning until the compounder finally shuts the door. Six hours went to arguing with the equipment supplier. Four went to the chemist downstairs. The lab got eleven hours. Eleven.

The doctor's strategy is not the lab. The doctor's strategy is OPD, and he does not know it, because the plan lives in his head and the strategy lives in his register. **The register is the honest document.** He will find this out in eighteen months when the lab is still half-built and a chain diagnostics franchise opens near the bus stand.

What fixes it is small and slightly embarrassing. He writes **LAB** on the door board for Tuesday and Thursday, two to five. He tells the compounder that during those hours he is "not in" — the same words the compounder already uses when the doctor is at the hospital, because that sentence works and "he is busy" does not. Walk-ins during those hours get a token for the evening. And on Saturday evening he spends twenty minutes with the register, marking every hour green or red against the plan. Nine weeks later the lab runs.

That is this whole lesson. Your calendar is your strategy whether you meant it to be or not. This lesson gives you the method for reading it, the blocks that defend it, the meetings you must refuse, and the honest limits of any system for a person with no boss.

## 1. The calendar is the strategy

### 1.1 Why the calendar is the honest document

A strategy document is a statement of intent. A calendar is a record of behaviour. When they disagree, the calendar is right — not because intent is worthless, but because a company's actual resource allocation _is_ its strategy, and in a three-person pre-revenue company the only resource being allocated in any serious quantity is the founders' hours. There is no marketing budget to misallocate. There is no headcount plan to get wrong. There is a technical CEO with roughly two hundred and thirty waking working hours a month, and where those hours land is the entire capital-allocation decision.

This is not a metaphor. The largest systematic study of CEO time — 1,114 CEOs across six countries, diaries parsed by machine learning — found that CEO behaviour sorts into two recognisable types from time-use data alone: "leaders", who spend their hours in multi-function, high-level meetings, and "managers", who spend them in one-to-one meetings with core functions. Firms with leader-type CEOs show higher productivity, the effect appears only _after_ the CEO's hire date, and the authors estimate about 17% of CEOs in the sample are mismatched to their firms ([Bandiera, Hansen, Prat and Sadun, "CEO Behavior and Firm Performance", NBER Working Paper 23248, 2017](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23248)). Read that result the way it deserves: **a stranger with only your diary can classify what kind of CEO you are, and the classification predicts something real.** You do not get to overrule the diary with a deck.

The second reason the calendar is the honest document is that it is the only one that cannot be padded. Paul Graham makes the adjacent point about attention rather than time: what determines your output is "the top idea in your mind" — the thing you think about in the shower — and it is not always the thing you chose. Two things reliably colonise the top slot: "Getting money is almost by definition an attention sink. The other is disputes" ([Paul Graham, "The Top Idea in Your Mind", July 2010](https://paulgraham.com/top.html)). Your calendar is where the top idea leaves fingerprints. If half your month went to a supplier dispute, the dispute was your strategy that month, whatever the kernel says.

### 1.2 The coding scheme — four jobs plus everything else

[[01-what-is-a-ceo|Lesson 01]] reduced the seat to four jobs: **direction, capital, people, story** — Fred Wilson's three (set the vision and strategy and communicate it; recruit and retain the very best talent; make sure there is always enough cash in the bank — [avc.com, 30 August 2010](https://avc.com/2010/08/what-a-ceo-does/)) split so that communicating the strategy gets counted separately from choosing it, because at a founder-led company they consume different hours and fail in different ways. Everything a CEO does that is not one of the four is real work, often necessary work, and still belongs in a bucket that says so.

| Code    | Job                     | What counts                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          | What does **not** count                                                                                                                     |
| ------- | ----------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **D**   | **Direction**           | Writing or re-arguing the kernel ([[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager\|C04]]); the no-list ([[C25-product-bets-and-the-no-list\|C25]]); reading the market; discovery interviews **whose purpose is to learn**; competitive teardown; product bets at the "which problem" altitude; the decision journal | Building the thing you already decided to build. Roadmap grooming. Any conversation where the answer was already fixed before you walked in |
| **K**   | **Capital**             | Runway, burn, the model, pricing, collections, banking, the CA on numbers, investor or lender conversations — and, in a bootstrapped company, **closing revenue**. A deal is cash                                                                                                                    | Building a feature the prospect asked for. Support. Invoicing mechanics (that is running the business)                                      |
| **P**   | **People**              | Recruiting and interviewing; 1:1s; feedback, praise and the hard conversation; comp and equity; onboarding a director into a new responsibility; the founder relationship itself                                                                                                                     | Doing a teammate's task because it was faster. That is X-B or X-R with a story attached                                                     |
| **S**   | **Story**               | Anything said to an audience: all-hands, the monthly stakeholder update ([[C23-stakeholder-and-investor-update\|C23]]), the pitch, website and positioning copy, an association address, a dealer WhatsApp broadcast, a partner narrative                                                            | A one-to-one sales call (that is K). An internal status update (that is X-R)                                                                |
| **X-B** | Build                   | Writing code, designing, debugging, reviewing PRs, infrastructure                                                                                                                                                                                                                                    | —                                                                                                                                           |
| **X-R** | Run                     | Support, ops, admin, invoices, bank chores, tooling, vendor management, chasing a payment gateway                                                                                                                                                                                                    | —                                                                                                                                           |
| **X-C** | Compliance & governance | CA/CS, statutory filings, board and minutes, contracts, the director's duty ([[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties\|lesson 09]])                                                                                                                                                            | —                                                                                                                                           |
| **X-T** | Transit                 | Travel, waiting, airport, the car. **Count it. Do not hide it inside the meeting it enabled**                                                                                                                                                                                                        | —                                                                                                                                           |
| **X-N** | Noise                   | Interrupted, reactive, unclassifiable, or honestly wasted                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            | —                                                                                                                                           |

Two rules make the coding survive contact with a real week:

1. **Code by purpose, not by content.** The same ninety minutes at the same pump with the same dealer is **D** if you went to learn what he does at month-end, and **K** if you went to close him. If you cannot say which, that is itself the finding — it means you took a meeting without a purpose, and §3's decline rule exists for exactly that.
2. **No half-hours.** An hour that plausibly serves two jobs gets coded to **the one you would have defended if the other had been cancelled**. Splitting hours across codes is how an audit turns into a flattering story. Every hour gets exactly one letter.

### 1.3 Running the audit — thirty minutes, once a month

The audit is the cheapest strategy diagnostic that exists, and it needs no tool you do not already have. The method:

1. **Open last month.** Calendar, WhatsApp, call log, git history, the notes folder. A founder-CEO's calendar is incomplete by construction — half your month was never an invite. Reconstruct it: git commits and PR timestamps give you X-B almost exactly; the call log gives you K and D; the WhatsApp scroll gives you X-N.
2. **Log in blocks, not minutes.** Half-hour granularity. Sam Corcos, co-founder and CEO of Levels, tracked _every fifteen minutes_ for two years — 7,922 hours — which is admirable and is not what you should do ([First Round Review, "An Exact Breakdown of How One CEO Spent His First Two Years of Company Building"](https://review.firstround.com/an-exact-breakdown-of-how-one-ceo-spent-his-first-two-years-of-company-building/)). Half-hour blocks reconstructed monthly get you 90% of the signal for 5% of the cost, and a method you will actually repeat.
3. **Code every block with one letter.** Do it fast. Your first instinct is usually right and your second instinct is usually flattering.
4. **Total by code, convert to percentages, and write the four numbers that matter** — D, K, P, S — next to a fifth: everything else.
5. **Put it beside the kernel.** Open [[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager|C04]] from [[05-strategy-i-diagnosis-and-the-strategy-kernel|lesson 05]]. Read the guiding policy and the coherent actions out loud. Then read the percentages out loud. The gap between those two readings is the finding.
6. **Write one paragraph and one change.** Not five changes. One. Then re-run next month.

The audit lives in [[C27-ceo-weekly-template-and-calendar-audit|C27]] and its totals go into the `CEO Dashboard` tab of [vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx](toolkit/vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx) so that twelve months of it become a trend rather than twelve separate embarrassments.

### 1.4 Worked example — a technical founder's month

Illustrative, but built from a plausible VSYST month: 26 working days, one district trip, DZZLO live on both stores, no revenue, three directors. Two hundred and thirty logged hours.

| Code               | Hours   | %        | What was actually in it                                                                               |
| ------------------ | ------- | -------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **X-B** Build      | 96      | 42%      | Voucher-ledger rework, the DIP module, three Expo release cycles, a two-day Mongo index chase         |
| **X-R** Run        | 34      | 15%      | Support WhatsApp, an app-store review rejection, ERPNext data cleanup, Easebuzz onboarding forms      |
| **X-T** Transit    | 26      | 11%      | Two Raipur-district days and one overnight; four hours waiting for a dealer who did not come          |
| **K** Capital      | 22      | 10%      | Sixteen hours in front of five dealers with a price; six hours on the CA, the bank and the burn sheet |
| **X-N** Noise      | 18      | 8%       | Reactive WhatsApp, a vendor demo nobody had asked for, an "intro call" that went nowhere              |
| **X-C** Compliance | 12      | 5%       | ROC paperwork, a GST clarification, an insurance quote                                                |
| **D** Direction    | 9       | 4%       | One good Sunday afternoon on pricing; two dealer conversations that were genuinely for learning       |
| **P** People       | 8       | 3%       | Two long conversations with the domain-expert director; one candidate coffee                          |
| **S** Story        | 5       | 2%       | Website copy rewrite; one WhatsApp broadcast to eleven dealers                                        |
|                    | **230** | **100%** |                                                                                                       |

Now the diagnosis. VSYST's written kernel says: **Indian fuel-distribution dealers, sold district by district, priced per GSTIN, web-only billing, India-only** — and its coherent actions say saturate one district before touching a second ([[06-strategy-ii-moats-positioning-and-the-business-model|lesson 06]] §2). A strategy of founder-led district saturation, executed by a CEO who spent **7% of the month in front of a paying prospect and 42% in an editor**, is not being executed. It is being described.

Say the finding in one sentence, in the form that makes it arguable: _"Last month the company's strategy was 'finish the product', and the written strategy was 'saturate Raipur district' — those are different strategies, and only one of them got funded."_

Note also what the audit does **not** say. It does not say building was wrong. A pre-revenue company with a live product in two app stores has real build obligations, and [[13-product-and-technology-leadership-for-a-technical-ceo|lesson 13]] argues that a technical CEO who stops touching the product early loses something a hired CTO cannot give back. The audit's claim is narrower and harder to escape: **at 42%, build was not a contribution to the strategy — it was the strategy**, and nobody decided that.

Two more things worth reading off the table. **X-T at 11%** — one overnight and two district days consumed more hours than direction, people and story combined; §9 is about that. And **X-N at 8%** — eighteen hours of nothing, which is the cheapest eighteen hours anyone will ever find you.

### 1.5 What the honest benchmarks say

There is no study of how the CEOs of three-person pre-revenue Indian vertical-SaaS companies spend their time, and anyone who tells you there is, is selling something. What exists is adjacent and still useful:

Engineering 4% | Even a CEO deliberate enough to time-track for two years gave strategy one hour in twenty. The Corcos number is the one to sit with. **Twenty-seven per cent on information processing.** Not strategy, not customers, not people — the inbox. That is what a CEO's month becomes if nothing in the system fights it, and §6 and §7 are the fight.

### 1.6 Three failure signatures, and what each one means

Once you have run the audit twice you will recognise your own pattern. Three are common enough to name.

| Signature               | What the audit looks like                                       | Underlying cause                                                                                                                                                                              | The correction                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Build gravity**       | X-B above 35% for a technical founder; D and K in single digits | Building is the only part of the job with a reliable dopamine loop and an unambiguous definition of done. Dealers say maybe; compilers say yes or no                                          | Cap build to named blocks (§4), not "whenever there's time". Put the customer minimum on the dashboard (§6.3) so the missing thing is visible weekly, not monthly                                      |
| **The urgency tax**     | X-R + X-N above 25%; every week feels full and nothing moved    | No mechanism absorbs the operational load, so it lands on whoever has a phone. This is precisely the load the COO course's cadence was built to absorb ([[08-the-operating-cadence\|COO 08]]) | Route it, don't absorb it. One unblock window a day (§6.4). If the load is real and recurring, it needs an SOP or a hire, which is [[16-decisions-delegation-and-not-being-the-bottleneck\|lesson 16]] |
| **The invisible zeros** | One of D/K/P/S at literally 0 for a month                       | Nobody complains when these are skipped, because the counterparty is the future                                                                                                               | The never-zero rule (§2.3). A job at zero for two consecutive months is a governance issue, not a scheduling one — log it in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log\|C29]]                        |

## 2. Where a CEO's time should go at 3, 10, 30 and 100 people

### 2.1 The table, honestly caveated

Read this as a **direction of travel, not a measurement.** These numbers are a house call built from three things: Fred Wilson's reduction of the job ([avc.com, 2010](https://avc.com/2010/08/what-a-ceo-does/)), the shape of the only public founder-CEO time log of any length ([Corcos](https://review.firstround.com/an-exact-breakdown-of-how-one-ceo-spent-his-first-two-years-of-company-building/)), and the leaders-versus-managers result that says high-level, multi-function time predicts firm productivity ([Bandiera et al.](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23248)). They are not from a controlled study of Indian district-sales SaaS companies, because there isn't one. Use them the way you use a route map, not the way you use a speedometer: if you are 5 points off, ignore it; if you are 25 points off, something is wrong.

| Code                  | 3 people (VSYST today) | 10 people   | 30 people  | 100 people | Why it moves                                                                                                                                                                         |
| --------------------- | ---------------------- | ----------- | ---------- | ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **D** Direction       | 15%                    | 20%         | 25%        | 25%        | Rises as the number of things you could do outgrows the number you can fund. At 3 the strategy is nearly forced by constraints; at 30 it is a genuine choice every quarter           |
| **K** Capital         | 25%                    | 25%         | 20%        | 20%        | Highest early because bootstrapped revenue _is_ the fundraise. Falls only when a sales function exists and a finance person owns the model — not before                              |
| **P** People          | 5%                     | 20%         | 30%        | 30%        | The steepest curve in the table. Between 3 and 30 the CEO's job changes from doing things to choosing who does things ([[10-building-the-team-hiring-equity-and-firing\|lesson 10]]) |
| **S** Story           | 5%                     | 10%         | 15%        | 20%        | Rises with the number of people who need to hear the same thing twice — team, dealers, partners, investors, press ([[12-communication-the-ceo-as-chief-storyteller\|lesson 12]])     |
| **X** Everything else | 50%                    | 25%         | 10%        | 5%         | Build, run, transit and noise. The single number that tells you whether you are scaling or just busier                                                                               |
| **Sanity check**      | Build ≤ 30% of total   | Build ≤ 15% | Build ≈ 0% | 0%         | The technical founder's specific tripwire                                                                                                                                            |

### 2.2 What changes at each threshold — and what it feels like

**At three people (now).** Selling and building dominate, and **that is correct.** A pre-revenue company with a live product and no paying dealer has exactly two questions — does it work, and will anyone pay — and both are answered by doing, not by planning. Do not read the table as "spend 15% on strategy"; read it as "15% is not zero". The feeling to expect: **guilt at the wrong thing.** You will feel guilty about the strategy hours because they produce no visible artefact that day, and you will feel virtuous about the build hours because a green CI run is proof of something. Reverse the instinct. The build hours are the ones that need justifying at this stage, because they are the ones you would do anyway.

**At ten people (VSYST's plausible 18–24-month state).** The threshold event is not headcount, it is the first person you manage who is not related to you. Two things break at once: information no longer travels by proximity, and your default of "I'll just do it" becomes actively harmful because it teaches everyone that ownership is provisional. P jumps from 5% to 20% in about one quarter and it feels like a demotion — you spend Tuesday afternoon in conversations that produce nothing you can show anyone. That feeling is the job changing under you, and it is the moment most technical founders quietly re-code themselves as the lead engineer. Expect it, name it, and put the 1:1s in the calendar before the feeling arrives.

**At thirty people.** Build hits zero, and the honest version of that sentence is: **you stop being good at the thing you were best at.** You will still read code; you will not own any. Direction becomes a real allocation problem for the first time because there is now more than one thing the company could credibly do, and every quarter you fund one and starve two ([[19-planning-okrs-and-capital-allocation|lesson 19]]). Story crosses the threshold where saying it once is not saying it at all. The feeling: **remoteness.** You will hear about a dealer's problem third-hand, four days late, and it will bother you more than it should. §6.3's customer minimum exists so that the remoteness is bounded rather than total.

**At a hundred.** The CEO's calendar is almost entirely other people's calendars: leadership 1:1s, the operating review, the board, recruiting the layer below the leadership team, and the story on repeat. Everything else is delegated or dead. This is far outside VSYST's next two years and is in the table only so you can see the slope you are on.

### 2.3 The never-zero rule

The discipline at three people is not a percentage. It is this: **direction, capital and people never hit zero in a month.** Concretely, at VSYST that means a floor, not a target —

| Job             | Monthly floor at three people                                                                                        | The mechanism that guarantees it                                                                                     |
| --------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **D** Direction | One protected half-day, plus the quarterly strategy review                                                           | The Friday strategy block (§4) and the quarterly beat (§5)                                                           |
| **K** Capital   | The monthly business review attended, the runway sheet re-based, and **at least twenty hours in front of prospects** | The two field days a week (§4) and the pipeline number on the dashboard                                              |
| **P** People    | Two founder 1:1s and one candidate conversation, even with no open role                                              | Tuesday's people slot; recruiting-as-a-standing-habit ([[10-building-the-team-hiring-equity-and-firing\|lesson 10]]) |
| **S** Story     | One written artefact that leaves the building                                                                        | The monthly stakeholder update ([[C23-stakeholder-and-investor-update\|C23]]), non-negotiable even with no investors |

A floor is easier to defend than a target because it fails visibly. You either wrote the update or you did not.

## 3. The meeting portfolio

### 3.1 The inventory, with exit criteria

Every recurring meeting is a subscription. The problem with subscriptions is not the price, it is that nobody re-reads them. So the CEO's meeting inventory carries a column no ordinary agenda has: **the exit criterion** — the specific, observable condition under which you would stop attending. Write it when you join the meeting, not when you are sick of it, because by then you will invent one.

### 3.2 The two categories, and why confusing them is expensive

Everything above splits cleanly:

- **The COO's meetings, which the CEO attends as a participant.** Ops meeting, check-in, pipeline review, monthly review, quarterly planning. In these the CEO's job is to arrive prepared, report on his own to-dos, make the one or two decisions only he can make, and _leave the facilitation alone_. The failure mode is specific and common at three people: a technical founder-CEO with a strong sense of how things should run quietly takes over the meeting the COO owns, and within a month the operating system has no owner. The COO course names this from the other side — "every meeting the CEO does not need to attend is the COO doing the job" ([[08-the-operating-cadence|COO 08]] §11).
- **The CEO's own meetings.** The strategy block, the customer block, the founder 1:1s, the board, the stakeholder-update writing block, office hours. Nobody else will schedule these, nobody will chase you when they slip, and their absence is invisible for months. Which is exactly why §6 exists.

### 3.3 The decline rule

> **A meeting whose purpose you cannot state in one sentence gets declined.**

The sentence has to take one of three shapes: _we are meeting to decide X_; _we are meeting to learn Y_; _we are meeting because a relationship needs an hour of my face this quarter_. The third is legitimate — it is most of [[14-partnerships-ecosystem-and-founder-relationships|lesson 14]]'s job, and in Indian B2B a relationship maintained is a pipeline preserved. What is not legitimate is a fourth shape that impersonates the other three: _we are meeting because they asked_.

Three practical mechanics:

1. **Ask for the sentence, in the reply.** "Happy to — what would you like us to decide or work out, so I can come prepared?" Half of low-value meetings evaporate at this question and the other half become better meetings. This is also just meeting hygiene rule 2 from [[08-the-operating-cadence|COO 08]] §9, applied to inbound.
2. **Have the decline sentence written down** so you are not composing politeness under pressure. English: _"Thanks — I'm not able to take this on right now, and I'd rather say that clearly than keep it hanging. If it becomes concrete, send me a note and I'll look at it properly."_ On WhatsApp, where most of this arrives in Raipur: shorter, warmer, and — critically — **not a maybe**. A polite Indian maybe costs you the meeting anyway, three weeks later, with interest ([[07-customers-markets-and-founder-led-sales|lesson 07]] §4.3 on the same phenomenon in the other direction).
3. **Price the meeting before accepting.** A 60-minute meeting with three directors is three person-hours out of a company that has roughly 150–180 productive person-hours a week — about 2% of everything the company can do, spent in one room. And if you still write code, the cost is worse than the arithmetic: on a maker's schedule "a single meeting can blow a whole afternoon, by breaking it into two pieces each too small to do anything hard in" ([Paul Graham, "Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule", July 2009](https://paulgraham.com/makersschedule.html)). A technical CEO lives on both schedules and pays the maker's tax on every manager's meeting.

## 4. The CEO's week, hour by hour

### 4.1 The constraint that decides the grid

Before any productivity principle, one domain fact fixes the shape of a VSYST CEO's week: **the dealer's free hours are not yours.** A petrol pump's owner is on the forecourt or on the phone during the morning rush and again in the evening; the window in which he will actually sit with you, take a call properly, or watch a demo on a phone is roughly **11:00 to 16:00**. That collides head-on with the best deep-work hours of the day, and there is no clever resolution. You choose. The grid below chooses the customer on two days and the build on three, and makes the choice explicit rather than letting it be decided by whoever messages first.

The second constraint is Indian and structural: **pumps trade on Saturday and Sunday.** Support does not stop, and dealers are often _more_ available on a Saturday than a Tuesday. A six-day week with one genuinely protected day off is the honest design; a five-day week that leaks into both weekend days is what happens when you pretend otherwise (§8).

## 5. The monthly and quarterly beats the CEO owns

### 5.1 Four rings, and where the CEO actually sits in each

The company has four rings — daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, plus an annual one that wraps them. [[08-the-operating-cadence|COO 08]] builds all of them and owns their mechanics: who fills the scorecard, who scribes, what the agenda template is, how the close runs. Read that lesson for the machinery. What follows is the _other_ view of the same rings: **which beats the CEO cannot outsource, what he personally has to produce, and what breaks downstream if he skips one.**

The distinction to hold on to is between **producing** and **deciding**. In the daily and weekly rings the CEO mostly consumes — he reads the scorecard, attends the ops meeting, reports his own to-dos. From the monthly ring upwards that inverts. The monthly and quarterly beats exist to make **reallocation** decisions — money, attention, headcount, the roadmap, and occasionally the strategy itself — and reallocation is the one thing the seat is for. A CEO who attends the monthly review as a spectator has delegated capital allocation to whoever built the deck.

| Ring          | Owns                                                            | CEO's role                 | What the CEO personally produces                  | Skipping it costs                                                  |
| ------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Daily         | COO                                                             | Participant                | A five-line check-in like everyone else           | Nothing, for a week. Then "blocked" stops surfacing                |
| Weekly        | COO                                                             | Participant + one decision | CEO to-dos closed; the pipeline's losses          | The week's issues drift into the month                             |
| **Monthly**   | **COO produces the pack, CEO decides on it**                    | **Decider**                | The reallocation decision; the stakeholder update | Two months of unexamined burn. The runway moves and nobody notices |
| **Quarterly** | **Shared — CEO owns the strategy half, COO the execution half** | **Owner of half the day**  | The kernel re-read, the kill list, the OKR grades | The plan and the strategy silently decouple                        |
| **Annual**    | **CEO owns the plan; COO owns the statutory ring**              | **Author**                 | Three priorities, the budget, the headcount line  | You enter a financial year without having chosen it                |

### 5.2 The monthly business review — the CEO's capital-allocation hour

Sixty minutes, once the CA's close pack lands (target: by the 10th). The COO produces it — budget vs actual line by line, the 13-week cash forecast re-based, collections and AR ageing ([[08-the-operating-cadence|COO 08]] §6). The CEO's job in that hour is small, specific and non-delegable: **decide what gets more next month and what gets less.**

Four questions, in this order, and none of them is "how are we doing":

1. **Has the runway number changed, and by how many weeks?** Not the cash balance — the weeks. [[17-the-numbers-a-ceo-watches|Lesson 17]] argues that runway-in-weeks is the number a CEO says from memory, and [[C13-runway-burn-and-scenario-planner|C13]] is where it gets re-based. If it moved by more than two weeks in either direction, that is the meeting's headline.
2. **Which variance is a signal and which is noise?** Every material line gets a one-sentence commentary from the COO. The CEO's contribution is to say which of them changes a decision. Most do not. Say so out loud, so the ones that do stand out.
3. **What are we funding more of, and what are we starving?** In a bootstrapped three-person company the currency is hours, not rupees, so the honest form of this question is: _whose week changes shape next month?_ Answering it with "nothing changes" is legitimate exactly once. Twice in a row means the review is theatre.
4. **What did we learn that the plan did not predict?** One line. It goes into the quarterly review's input pile so that the quarter is not reconstructed from memory on the day.

Two mechanics worth being opinionated about.

**Read the pack before, or read it in the room — but pick one and never fake it.** The COO course's meeting-hygiene rule 2 says agenda and pre-reading go out in advance ([[08-the-operating-cadence|COO 08]] §9). Amazon famously does the opposite: the narrative memo is read _silently in the room_ at the start, precisely because pre-reading is the promise everyone breaks — the practice sits inside the same operating machinery as the WBR and the OP1/OP2 planning cycle ([Bryar and Carr, workingbackwards.com](https://www.workingbackwards.com/)). The house call for VSYST: **pre-read is the rule, and the first fifteen minutes of the MBR are silent reading anyway.** Three directors, one of whom was in a car until 9 PM, will not all have read it. Fifteen silent minutes cost less than a meeting spent narrating a spreadsheet.

**End with a decision, in the decision journal, with a review date.** The MBR is a one-way-door detector: pricing changes, a hire, dropping a district, taking a loan. Anything in that class goes into [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] with the reasoning written _before_ the outcome is known. Bezos's argument for why speed here matters is the useful one — most decisions are reversible, and treating them as if they were not is the specific disease of slow companies ("Day 2 companies make high-quality decisions, but they make high-quality decisions slowly" — [Amazon 2016 letter to shareholders](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2016-letter-to-shareholders)).

### 5.3 The monthly stakeholder update — 90 minutes of enforced honesty

This is the beat most likely to be skipped and the one whose absence is most expensive, because it is the only monthly artefact with **no counterparty who will chase you**. There is no board at VSYST. There are no investors. Which is exactly why the update has to be a calendar block with a deadline attached — Friday of the week the close lands, 90 minutes, in [[C23-stakeholder-and-investor-update|C23]]'s shape.

Send it to a real list, however short: the CA, one mentor, an ex-colleague who has run a company, and — the useful cruelty — each other. Three founder-directors who talk all day will still discover, on reading a written month, that they had different pictures of it.

| Section             | What goes in                                                                                                | Why a CEO cannot skip it                                                                      |
| ------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **TL;DR**           | Three lines. The month in a sentence, the number that moved, the one ask                                    | Forces a judgment about what mattered. Most drafts fail here first                            |
| **KPIs vs plan**    | The five numbers from [[17-the-numbers-a-ceo-watches\|lesson 17]] §2, each against what you said last month | The comparison is the content. A number without last month's promise beside it is decoration  |
| **Cash and runway** | Balance, burn, runway in weeks, and any change in the collection position                                   | The only section an outsider reads first                                                      |
| **Wins**            | Named. "Three pumps in Dhamtari signed" beats "good traction"                                               | Gives the team and the reader something concrete to repeat                                    |
| **Misses**          | What you said you would do and did not, and why                                                             | **The section that makes the document worth writing.** Skip it and you have written marketing |
| **Asks**            | Specific, small, actionable: one introduction, one candidate, one opinion                                   | An ask nobody can act on is a complaint                                                       |

The guidance is well-trodden — highlights, lowlights, KPIs, team, asks, with lowlights paired to a proposed response, and the working claim that companies communicating regularly with investors are roughly twice as likely to raise a follow-on ([Visible.vc — How to Write the Perfect Investor Update](https://visible.vc/blog/how-to-write-the-perfect-investor-update/)) — **VERIFY LIVE** on that statistic before you repeat it to anyone; it comes from a portfolio-services vendor, not a controlled study, and the causation almost certainly runs both ways.

The reason to do it at zero investors is not fundraise-readiness, though it produces that for free. It is that a CEO with no boss has no forcing function for monthly honesty, and this is the cheapest one that exists. [[20-the-ceo-own-operating-system-and-succession|Lesson 20]] §2 calls this _manufacturing a boss_; the monthly update is the first and smallest instance of it.

### 5.4 The quarterly strategy review — the CEO's half of the planning day

The COO facilitates a half-day at the end of each quarter: review the scorecard trends, score the rocks or OKRs, set the next quarter's three to five, adjust budget and headcount, surface risks ([[08-the-operating-cadence|COO 08]] §7, mechanics in [[19-planning-okrs-and-capital-allocation|lesson 19]]). **Split that half-day in two, and put the CEO's half first**, because the order encodes the argument: objectives are supposed to be _downstream_ of a strategy, and if you set them before re-examining the strategy you have merely re-decorated last quarter.

**The CEO's ninety minutes, in order:**

| Step                                                      | Time   | What happens                                                                                                                                                                                             | Output                                                 |
| --------------------------------------------------------- | ------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Read the kernel out loud**                              | 10 min | Someone else reads [[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager\|C04]] aloud — diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent actions. Reading it yourself lets you hear what you meant instead of what you wrote                | Everyone hearing the same sentences                    |
| **What did we learn that the diagnosis did not predict?** | 25 min | Losses, surprises, the thing a dealer said that nobody had a slot for. Pull from the month's "what we learned" lines (§5.2) and the loss log ([[07-customers-markets-and-founder-led-sales\|lesson 07]]) | 3–6 learnings, written                                 |
| **Does the diagnosis survive?**                           | 20 min | The only question that matters. Usually yes. When the answer is no, everything downstream is wrong and the OKR half of the day should not run as planned                                                 | Kernel unchanged (dated), or amended (dated, with why) |
| **The kill list**                                         | 20 min | What are we stopping? Explicitly, by name, with the person who was doing it in the room. [[C25-product-bets-and-the-no-list\|C25]]                                                                       | The no-list updated                                    |
| **The calendar audit, three months at once**              | 15 min | §1's four percentages for each of the last three months, side by side, read against the kernel                                                                                                           | One change to the week's shape                         |

Then hand the room to the COO for the execution half.

**Scoring, briefly, because the mechanics belong to [[19-planning-okrs-and-capital-allocation|lesson 19]].** OKRs grade 0.0 to 1.0 at the end of the cycle, with self-assessment allowed to adjust for circumstances, and the intended landing zone for a genuinely ambitious objective is around **0.7 — not 1.0** ([What Matters — How to Grade OKRs](https://www.whatmatters.com/faqs/how-to-grade-okrs)). A team scoring 1.0 across the board was aiming too low. The CEO's job at scoring time is not the arithmetic; it is the single sentence that only he can say: **do these objectives, scored honestly, add up to the guiding policy — or did we have a productive quarter in a direction we did not choose?**

The three standing audits already ride this slot in the COO's design — access, vendors and tools, risk register ([[08-the-operating-cadence|COO 08]] §7). The CEO adds two of his own, and they take twenty minutes together: **the meeting-portfolio review** (§3 — walk the inventory, test each exit criterion, kill or delegate one meeting) and **the quarterly read of the calendar audit trend**. Both are in [[C27-ceo-weekly-template-and-calendar-audit|C27]].

### 5.5 The annual plan and the statutory ring

Two things happen once a year and they are unrelated except that both live in March.

**The plan.** One full day, in March, before the financial year turns: three priorities for the year, the budget, the headcount line, and the year's one-sentence theme. It is authored by the CEO — this is the least delegable writing in the job — and pressure-tested by the other directors. The template is [[C28-annual-plan-and-ceo-okr-sheet|C28]]; the method is [[19-planning-okrs-and-capital-allocation|lesson 19]]. At scale this becomes a two-pass machine — Amazon runs a bottom-up OP1 in the autumn and a reconciled OP2 after the year's numbers land, feeding S-team goals and the review cadence beneath them ([workingbackwards.com](https://www.workingbackwards.com/)). At three people it is one day and a two-page document, and pretending otherwise is how a small company acquires a large company's overhead without acquiring its output.

**The statutory ring.** Board meetings, the AGM, ADT-1, AOC-4, MGT-7A, DPT-3, the auditor. [[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties|Lesson 09]] owns every date, form, threshold and penalty, and [[13-compliance-calendar-risk-and-insurance|COO 13]] owns the calendar that runs them. The CEO's only job here is the one nobody else can do: **these dates are in your calendar, with a lead time, and you are the one who does not move them.** A board meeting postponed by three relatives with a WhatsApp message is still a Companies Act failure. **VERIFY LIVE with the CS every April** — thresholds and forms move with each Budget and each amendment.

## 6. Protecting the two blocks that always get eaten

### 6.1 Why it is always these two

Of the eight codes in §1.2, two are eaten every single week: **thinking and writing time (D)** and **direct customer time (K/D)**. Not because founders are lazy or badly organised, but because of a structural property they share and nothing else on the list has — **neither one has a counterparty who complains today.**

| Work                       | Who complains if you skip it today                                       | How long before skipping it hurts |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------- |
| Build                      | The compiler, in ninety seconds. The release, tomorrow                   | Hours                             |
| Support                    | A dealer, on WhatsApp, within the hour                                   | Hours                             |
| Compliance                 | The CA in a week; the ROC in a quarter; the state eventually             | Weeks–months                      |
| Payroll and vendors        | People with rent to pay                                                  | Days                              |
| **Customer conversations** | **Nobody. The prospect you did not call does not know he was on a list** | **One quarter**                   |
| **Thinking and writing**   | **Nobody. Ever**                                                         | **Two to four quarters**          |

That is the whole diagnosis, and it explains why exhortation does not work. Telling a founder to "make time to think" is like telling him to feel differently about a structural incentive. The two blocks lose because losing them is free this week and expensive next year, and human beings are extremely reliable about that trade. The only thing that has ever worked is to give the two blocks an **artificial counterparty** — a mechanism that complains on their behalf, today, in a voice the founder will actually hear.

That is the CEO course's version of the COO's rule. The COO says _mechanisms, not intentions_. The CEO version, applied to his own hours: **a block with no counterparty is an intention. Give it one.**

### 6.2 Defending the thinking block — make it an external meeting

The strongest available mechanism is also the cheapest and slightly silly: **treat the block as a meeting with someone outside the company**, and be exactly as reluctant to move it as you would be to move a meeting with a dealer who drove in from Mahasamund.

Five properties make that work, and a block missing any of them will be eaten within three weeks.

| Property                                 | Wrong version                       | Right version                                                                                                                                                               | Why it matters                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| ---------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **A name**                               | "Thinking time"                     | "Pricing: per-GSTIN vs per-pump — decide by 12 Sep"                                                                                                                         | You cannot cancel a decision. You can always cancel thinking                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| **A counterparty**                       | You                                 | A named person expecting an artefact — the other directors, the mentor on the stakeholder-update list, or a calendar invite you actually sent to the domain-expert director | The complaint has to come from somewhere. Borrow one                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| **A location**                           | The same desk where support arrives | Anywhere else. The other room, a café, the car before a field day, the office at 6:30 AM before anyone is up                                                                | Environment is a cheaper commitment device than willpower                                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| **A deliverable**                        | Notes                               | A written page: an updated [[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager\|C04]], a decision-journal entry, a memo, a paragraph of the stakeholder update                                  | "Writing about something, even something you know well, usually shows you that you didn't know it as well as you thought" ([Paul Graham, "Putting Ideas Into Words", Feb 2022](https://paulgraham.com/words.html)). Writing is the test that thinking passed |
| **A question, written the night before** | Open-ended                          | One sentence at the top of a blank page, decided at Thursday's week-close                                                                                                   | Without it, the first forty minutes go to choosing what to think about, which is how a three-hour block becomes a two-hour block                                                                                                                             |

The decline sentence for this block is the same one from §3.3, and it is not a lie: **"I have a meeting."** You do. Do not editorialise it into "I'm just doing some thinking" — that sentence invites negotiation, and in a family firm, where the person asking is your co-founder and possibly your relative, you will lose that negotiation every time.

Two hard cases, because they are the ones that actually arise:

- **A dealer wants the slot.** Take it. Customer time beats thinking time on any given Friday — that is the correct priority order and pretending otherwise is precious. The mechanism is not "never move it", it is **"never move it without rescheduling it in the same act."** Move it in the same click. An unrescheduled block is a cancelled block.
- **Production is down.** Take it, obviously. Then log it. Four cancellations in a quarter is a scheduling problem; ten is a company that has no on-call rotation, which is [[12-product-and-engineering-operations|COO 12]]'s problem to fix, not a willpower problem to feel guilty about.

The intellectual case for the block is Newport's: focused, undistracted work on a cognitively demanding problem is a distinct and increasingly rare capability, and it does not happen in the gaps between other things ([Cal Newport, _Deep Work_, 2016](https://calnewport.com/books/deep-work/)). The practical case is Graham's maker's-schedule argument, already cited in §3.3 — for a CEO who still writes code, a single badly-placed meeting does not cost an hour, it costs the afternoon.

### 6.3 The customer minimum — put a number on the dashboard, not a block in the calendar

Customer time needs a **different** mechanism, and this is the part most founders get wrong by applying the same one twice. A thinking block is defended by treating it as an appointment. Customer time cannot be, because appointments with customers get cancelled _by the customer_ — a pump owner with a tanker at the gate will not keep his 3 PM, and no amount of discipline on your side changes that.

So do not defend it with a block. Defend it with **a number that gets reported**.

> **The weekly customer minimum: five conversations. It sits on the scorecard, next to cash and pipeline, and the CEO reports it in the Monday ops meeting like every other row.**

The mechanism works because it converts an invisible omission into a visible red cell in front of two other directors. Nobody notices an unmade call. Everybody notices a `2 / 5`.

**What counts, defined tightly enough to argue with:**

| Counts                                                                                        | Does not count                                   |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| A conversation of 15+ minutes with a **named** person who pays or could pay                   | A support ticket. That is service, not discovery |
| A pump visit, a phone call, a WhatsApp **voice** exchange that went back and forth            | A WhatsApp text thread about a bug               |
| A demo where **they** talked more than half the time                                          | A demo where you presented for forty minutes     |
| A loss debrief with someone who chose not to buy — the highest-yield conversation on the list | A conversation with someone who cannot sign      |
| A conversation you wrote down within 24 hours                                                 | A conversation you remember having               |

The last row is the one that turns a vanity count into an instrument. **An unwritten conversation did not happen.** Three lines is enough: who, what surprised you, what they would have to believe to buy. Those lines are the raw material for the monthly "what we learned" line (§5.2), the quarterly learnings list (§5.4), the objection bank in [[C10-founder-led-sales-pipeline-and-script|C10]], and the discovery guide in [[C09-icp-and-customer-discovery-guide|C09]].

The underlying claim is not this course's. It is the most repeated instruction in early-stage startup practice: founders must personally and manually recruit their earliest users, because "you can't wait for users to come to you" and "the feedback you get from engaging directly with your earliest users will be the best you ever get" ([Paul Graham, "Do Things that Don't Scale", July 2013](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)). How to run the conversation so it yields something true rather than something polite is [Rob Fitzpatrick's _The Mom Test_](https://www.momtestbook.com/) — ask about their life and their past behaviour, not about your idea — and the practical version is YC's ([Gustaf Alströmer, "How to talk to users", YC Startup Library](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Iq-how-to-talk-to-users)). [[07-customers-markets-and-founder-led-sales|Lesson 07]] owns the method; this lesson owns only the fact that it has a **floor with a number on it**.

**Why five, and why it does not drop.** Five is chosen to be survivable in a bad week and meaningful over a quarter: sixty-five conversations in a quarter is enough to see a pattern and small enough that a founder who is also shipping code can hold it. It does not drop as the company grows because §2's table says customer contact is the last thing a CEO delegates, not the first. What changes at fifteen people is the _composition_ — fewer cold prospects, more top-account reviews and loss debriefs — and at thirty, when you hear about a dealer's problem third-hand and four days late, the same five conversations are the only thing standing between you and the remoteness §2.2 warned about.

### 6.4 Batching the interruptions — the unblock window

The third mechanism protects both blocks at once, and it is the one that makes a founder-CEO tolerable to work with rather than merely productive.

Start from the evidence, because the folklore here is wrong in an interesting way. In a detailed observational study of 24 information workers, people spent an average of **11 minutes 4 seconds** on a working sphere before switching or being interrupted; when an interrupted activity was resumed the same day, it took an average of **25 minutes 26 seconds** to get back to it, with an average of 2.26 other working spheres visited in between ([Mark, González and Harris, "No Task Left Behind? Examining the Nature of Fragmented Work", CHI 2005](https://www.ics.uci.edu/~gmark/CHI2005.pdf)). And the surprising part, from the same group's later experiment: interrupted tasks were completed **faster**, with no measurable quality loss — because people compensate by working faster — but at the price of "more stress, higher frustration, time pressure and effort" ([Mark, Gudith and Klocke, "The Cost of Interrupted Work: More Speed and Stress", CHI 2008](https://www.ics.uci.edu/~gmark/chi08-mark.pdf)).

Read those two results together and the design follows. **The cost of interruption is not primarily output; it is the founder.** A CEO who is interrupted forty times a day will still ship — and will be depleted, irritable and bad at judgment by Thursday, which is precisely when the judgment is needed. §8 is about what that costs over a year.

The mechanism is a **named daily window, advertised, and actually honoured**:

- **45–60 minutes, same time every day** (Grid A puts it at 16:00). Anyone can bring anything. No agenda required — the point is that the low-grade question with no natural home has a home.
- **The CEO is genuinely available in it.** If you are half-present in the window, everyone correctly concludes the window is theatre and goes back to interrupting you at 10 AM. This is the only rule that matters.
- **A written escalation list — four items, no more.** At VSYST, plausibly: _production is down; money is moving out of the company; a dealer is about to walk; a statutory deadline is inside 48 hours._ Anything on the list interrupts anything. Anything not on the list waits for 16:00. Give the list to everyone, including the family members who are not employees.
- **Everything else is async by default.** The channel map is the COO's — which topics live in which channel, and the rule that WhatsApp is external-only ([[08-the-operating-cadence|COO 08]] §10). The CEO's job is not to write that map. It is to be **the first person who obeys it**, because a channel map the CEO ignores is a channel map that does not exist.

At fifteen people the same mechanism becomes a weekly hour anyone in the company can book, which is Grid B's office hour. The shape scales; the principle does not change.

## 7. The Mochary-style CEO operating system

### 7.1 What it is, and why a course for a Raipur founder bothers with it

Matt Mochary coaches CEOs — Coinbase, OpenAI, Reddit, Discord, Substack are on the client list — and wrote down what he tells them ([mocharymethod.com](https://www.mocharymethod.com/)). _The Great CEO Within_ circulated for years as a shared Google Doc before it became a book, and the doc is still readable free ([The Great CEO Within — Google Doc](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1ZJZbv4J6FZ8Dnb0JuMhJxTnwl-dwqx5xl0s65DE3wO8/preview); good chapter notes at [The Rabbit Hole](https://blas.com/the-great-ceo-within/) and [Nat Eliason](https://www.nateliason.com/notes/the-great-ceo-within-matt-mochary)).

It earns a section here for one reason: **it is the only widely adopted operating system written specifically for the seat that has no boss.** Every other productivity method assumes something above you — a manager, a deadline someone else set, a performance review. Mochary's assumes nothing above you and builds the missing enforcement out of calendar blocks and written artefacts.

It also has to be read with a correction applied. It was written for venture-funded American technology companies with hired executives, a board, and a coach on retainer at five figures a month. VSYST has three founder-directors, no revenue, one product and a domain where the customer is a pump owner who answers voice notes. Some of the system transfers exactly; some of it is overhead you cannot afford. §7.6 says which is which.

| Practice                      | The rule as written                                                             | Assumes                                 | Fit at VSYST today                                                                                                               |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Top goal**                  | Two hours a day, in the calendar, on the top goal only, every workday           | You know what your one goal is          | **Keep, unmodified.** §7.2                                                                                                       |
| **Energy audit**              | Log the week hour by hour, mark green/red, aim for 75–80% green                 | Something can be delegated              | **Adapt.** §7.4 — with a hard correction for technical founders                                                                  |
| **Inbox discipline**          | Check email twice a day; two-minute rule; everything else becomes a next action | Email is the main inbound channel       | **Adapt.** In India the main channel is WhatsApp. §7.5                                                                           |
| **Issue / Proposed Solution** | Bring problems written, with a proposed fix                                     | A team that writes                      | **Keep** — already the COO's meeting-hygiene rule 3 ([[08-the-operating-cadence\|COO 08]] §9)                                    |
| **Agreement tracker**         | Every commitment logged and reviewed                                            | Enough people that commitments get lost | **Not yet.** Three directors and a decision log ([[T09-decision-log-and-adr\|T09]]) cover it. Trigger: ~8 people                 |
| **Coach / peer group**        | A trained coach, weekly                                                         | ₹8–9 lakh a month                       | **Drop the retainer; keep the function.** [[20-the-ceo-own-operating-system-and-succession\|Lesson 20]] §3 builds a free version |

### 7.2 The top goal — two hours a day, and the harder half

The rule is exactly this: **"Schedule two hours each day (i.e., put an event in your calendar) to work on your top goal only"**, and do it every workday ([Nat Eliason's notes on _The Great CEO Within_](https://www.nateliason.com/notes/the-great-ceo-within-matt-mochary)). Grid A in §4.2 already implements it at 06:30–08:30, for the reason given there: in a family firm with an office attached to the family's business, that is the only slot with a lock on it.

The two hours are the easy half. **The hard half is having exactly one top goal**, and most founders fail here rather than at the calendar. Three tests a candidate top goal must pass:

1. **It is a company outcome, not your project.** "Ship the DIP module" is a project. "Ten paying dealer GSTINs in Raipur district" is an outcome. Projects are how build gravity (§1.6) disguises itself as focus.
2. **It would make several other things unnecessary or easier.** If achieving it changes nothing else on the list, it is a task with a promotion.
3. **You can say it in one sentence to a dealer without explaining the company.** If it needs preamble, it is not yet a goal, it is a theme.

| Candidate top goal for VSYST this quarter                                      | Verdict                          | Why                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| "Finish the voucher-ledger rework"                                             | **No**                           | A project you enjoy, dressed as a goal. It belongs in the build blocks, not the top-goal block                                                                                       |
| "Get DZZLO to feature parity with the incumbent"                               | **No**                           | Parity is a competitor's roadmap wearing your name. [[06-strategy-ii-moats-positioning-and-the-business-model\|Lesson 06]] §2                                                        |
| "Ten paying dealer GSTINs in Raipur district, invoicing through DZZLO monthly" | **Yes**                          | An outcome; makes pricing, positioning and the fundraise question all easier; one sentence                                                                                           |
| "Close the Easebuzz onboarding and turn on web billing"                        | **Maybe — for one quarter only** | Legitimate as a top goal only because revenue is physically blocked without it. It expires the day it ships                                                                          |
| "Sign the IOCL relationship"                                                   | **No, not as top goal**          | One-way door, long clock, mostly outside your control. It is a [[14-partnerships-ecosystem-and-founder-relationships\|lesson 14]] bet with its own block, not a two-hours-a-day goal |

The top goal is written at the top of [[C28-annual-plan-and-ceo-okr-sheet|C28]] and re-decided once a quarter in §5.4's ninety minutes — **not** whenever it stops being fun.

### 7.3 The weekly review — one hour that writes next week

Grid A puts it Friday 17:00–19:00. Its defining property, and the reason most weekly reviews die within a month: **it must produce next week's calendar, not this week's feelings.** A review that ends in reflection is a diary. A review that ends with six blocks placed in a calendar is a mechanism.

The hour, in order — the full form is in [[C27-ceo-weekly-template-and-calendar-audit|C27]]:

| Step                         | Minutes | Reads                                                        | Writes                                                                                                          |
| ---------------------------- | ------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Close the loop on CEO to-dos | 10      | Last week's list                                             | Done / carried / killed. Anything carried twice gets killed or scheduled, never carried a third time            |
| Code the week                | 10      | Your own calendar, half-hour blocks                          | Four percentages — D, K, P, S — into the `CEO Dashboard` tab                                                    |
| The two floors               | 5       | Customer conversations logged; top-goal hours actually spent | Two numbers. Red if under                                                                                       |
| One learning                 | 10      | The week's customer notes                                    | One line, which feeds §5.2's monthly review                                                                     |
| Decisions and the journal    | 10      | Anything decided this week                                   | [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log\|C29]] rows, with review dates                                      |
| **Place next week**          | 30      | Grid A                                                       | **Actual calendar events**: top-goal blocks, two field days, strategy block, money block, 1:1s, unblock windows |
| The one thing                | 5       | All of the above                                             | One sentence: what next week is for                                                                             |

Two rules keep it honest. **Code the week before you plan the next one** — planning first lets you write an aspirational week and then never check it. And **the review happens even in a bad week**, especially in a bad week; a review skipped after a bad week is how three bad weeks become a bad quarter without anyone noticing.

### 7.4 The energy audit — and the correction a technical founder must apply

The exercise: go through a working week hour by hour, mark each block **green** if it energised you and **red** if it drained you, and work towards spending **75–80% of your time on the green** ([Nat Eliason's notes](https://www.nateliason.com/notes/the-great-ceo-within-matt-mochary); Mochary publishes an Energy Audit worksheet among the free resources at [mocharymethod.com](https://www.mocharymethod.com/)).

For a founder-CEO this is not self-care. It is **a hiring specification you can write a year before you can afford the hire.** The red list, sorted by hours, is the first job description in the company — and reading it that way removes the guilt that otherwise attaches to the exercise.

But apply the correction, because for a technical founder the naive version of this advice is actively dangerous. **Your green list is almost entirely build.** Code is the most reliably energising work you do: fast feedback, unambiguous done, no one saying maybe. Optimise naively for green and you will spend 80% of your week in an editor, feel wonderful, and re-code yourself as the lead engineer of a company with no customers — which is exactly the failure §2.2 described. So:

> **Green is a tiebreak, not a criterion.** Between two things that equally serve the strategy, do the green one. Never between a green thing and a strategic one.

And sort red into three piles, because they get different treatment:

| Red work                        | Example at VSYST                                                                                                                                                                                                                   | Treatment                                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Red and delegable now**       | ERPNext data cleanup, app-store submission chores, expense filing, chasing vendor paperwork                                                                                                                                        | Route to the COO seat or an SOP this quarter ([[15-sops-and-playbooks\|COO 15]]). This is the cheapest energy you will recover                                                                                                |
| **Red and delegable at a hire** | First-line support, invoice follow-ups, the payment-gateway back-and-forth                                                                                                                                                         | Goes on the job description now, hired when the trigger in [[10-building-the-team-hiring-equity-and-firing\|lesson 10]] fires. Until then, batch it into one named ugly block a week rather than smearing it across five days |
| **Red and yours, permanently**  | The hard conversation with a co-founder; a firing; signing something you are personally liable for; the director's statutory duty ([[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties\|lesson 09]]); telling a dealer the answer is no | **Accept it and schedule it deliberately, early in the day.** This is the part of the job that does not get more pleasant with seniority. [[18-risk-crisis-and-the-hard-things\|Lesson 18]] is the whole subject              |

Run the audit **once a quarter, alongside the calendar audit** — not weekly. Weekly, it becomes mood tracking.

### 7.5 Inbox and WhatsApp discipline

Mochary's email rule is blunt and correct: **"If you check your email incessantly, multiple times an hour, you are wasting hours of productivity"** — check twice a day, apply the two-minute rule to anything that can be finished in two minutes, and convert everything else into a next action rather than leaving it in the inbox ([Nat Eliason's notes](https://www.nateliason.com/notes/the-great-ceo-within-matt-mochary)). The number that makes this urgent is already in §1.5: the one public founder-CEO time log of any length spent **27% of two years on information processing, mostly email** — more than strategy, recruiting and engineering combined ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/an-exact-breakdown-of-how-one-ceo-spent-his-first-two-years-of-company-building/)).

**Then apply the Indian correction, which is not a detail.** At VSYST, email is where the CA, the ROC portal, the app stores and the payment gateway live. WhatsApp is where the _business_ lives — dealers, drivers, the domain-expert director's thirty-year network, support, and quite often the family. A discipline that governs email and ignores WhatsApp governs perhaps a fifth of the inbound.

| Channel                         | Realistic SLA                                             | Rule                                                                                                                                                          | Who owns the rule                                            |
| ------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| WhatsApp — a dealer or prospect | Same day; within the hour during 11:00–16:00              | Answer inside a field day or the unblock window. Voice note in, voice note out — it is faster and dealers prefer it                                           | CEO                                                          |
| WhatsApp — support              | Never the CEO's, once anyone else exists                  | Route to the support number/SOP ([[10-customer-operations-support-and-success\|COO 10]]). Until then, the unblock window                                      | COO                                                          |
| WhatsApp — internal             | Next unblock window                                       | **Nothing is decided on WhatsApp until it is in the vault.** A decision that lives only in a chat scroll is a decision three people will remember differently | COO's channel map ([[08-the-operating-cadence\|COO 08]] §10) |
| WhatsApp — groups               | No SLA                                                    | Notifications **off** for every group, on for saved contacts only. This single setting is worth more than any app                                             | CEO                                                          |
| Email                           | Twice a day, 12:00 and 17:30                              | Two-minute rule, then convert to a next action                                                                                                                | CEO                                                          |
| Phone call                      | Immediate only if on the four-item escalation list (§6.4) | Everything else gets a WhatsApp back offering a time                                                                                                          | CEO                                                          |

Three specifics that matter more in Raipur than the general advice does:

- **Separate the business number from the personal one.** One phone, two numbers, or two phones. Without it, §8's boundary problem has no physical enforcement point at all, and "silence WhatsApp after 9 PM" becomes a rule you cannot follow without also silencing your family.
- **The blue tick is a commitment device you did not choose.** A dealer who sees the message read at 22:40 has learned that you answer at 22:40. Read receipts off, or do not open it.
- **Batch the voice notes.** Six voice notes answered in one twenty-minute pass beats six answered across a day, and the difference is exactly the 25 minutes 26 seconds of resumption cost from §6.4 multiplied by six.

### 7.6 The honest limits of any productivity system for someone with no boss

This section exists because the alternative — presenting a system as if it solves the problem — would be the kind of motivational fog this course is supposed to avoid. Four limits, each with a source that argues it properly.

**Limit 1 — the system treats a symptom whose cause is elsewhere.** Cal Newport's critique of the personal-productivity tradition is that systems like GTD organise the flood of incoming work but do nothing about its volume, because the volume is produced by how the _organisation_ distributes work, not by how the individual files it; his conclusion is that we must "acknowledge the futility of trying to tame our frenzied work lives all on our own" ([Cal Newport, "The Rise and Fall of Getting Things Done", _The New Yorker_, 17 November 2020](https://www.newyorker.com/tech/annals-of-technology/the-rise-and-fall-of-getting-things-done)). For a CEO this cuts two ways, and the second way is the useful one: **you are the organisation.** If your week is drowning, the fix is usually an SOP, a routing rule or a hire — a mechanism in the company — not a better personal system. That is [[16-decisions-delegation-and-not-being-the-bottleneck|lesson 16]], and it is upstream of everything in this section.

**Limit 2 — the evidence says these systems improve how you feel more than what you produce.** The largest meta-analysis of time management — 158 studies, 53,957 participants, 490 effect sizes — finds time management moderately related to job performance (r ≈ .25) and negatively to distress, and then reports the awkward headline: "time management seems to enhance wellbeing — in particular, life satisfaction — to a greater extent than it does performance," with the effect on life satisfaction about 72% stronger than on job satisfaction. The authors state plainly that this "challenges the common perception that time management first and foremost enhances work performance" ([Aeon, Faber and Panaccio, "Does time management work? A meta-analysis", _PLOS ONE_, January 2021](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0245066)). Take that at face value. A well-run week will probably make you less miserable more reliably than it makes VSYST win. **That is still worth having** — §8 argues it is one of the highest-return investments a founder makes — but it is not a substitute for choosing the right thing to do, which is [[05-strategy-i-diagnosis-and-the-strategy-kernel|lesson 05]]'s job, not this lesson's.

**Limit 3 — efficiency creates capacity, and capacity attracts work.** Oliver Burkeman's long essay against the time-management industry makes the argument that every gain in personal efficiency is absorbed by new work rushing in to fill it, so the felt experience of busyness never improves — and that the deeper trouble is the fantasy of getting on top of everything at all ([Oliver Burkeman, "Why time management is ruining our lives", _The Guardian_, 22 December 2016](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2016/dec/22/why-time-management-is-ruining-our-lives)). The operational consequence for a CEO is concrete: **the hours you free up will be taken by something unless you decide in advance what takes them.** That is why every mechanism in §6 names its beneficiary. Freed hours with no named owner become build hours.

**Limit 4 — there is no enforcement, because there is no boss.** This is the structural fact the whole lesson keeps circling. Nobody will notice if you skip the strategy block, miss the customer minimum, do not send the update, or quietly stop scoring the quarter. Mochary's system does not solve this; nothing solves this. What you can do is **manufacture counterparties**, which is what this lesson has been doing all along:

| The undefended thing | Manufactured counterparty                                                          | Section                                                             |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Thinking time        | A named deliverable someone expects                                                | §6.2                                                                |
| Customer time        | A red cell on the scorecard, read aloud on Monday                                  | §6.3                                                                |
| Monthly honesty      | A written update sent to a real list                                               | §5.3                                                                |
| Quarterly direction  | The kernel read out loud, by someone else, in a room                               | §5.4                                                                |
| Everything else      | A co-founder explicitly given permission to ask, and a mentor with a standing date | [[20-the-ceo-own-operating-system-and-succession\|Lesson 20]] §2–§3 |

The last row is the one people skip and the one that does the most work. **Give one other person the standing right to ask you three questions** — did you hold the strategy block, how many customer conversations, did the update go out — and agree that "I was busy" is not an answer. That is a five-minute conversation and it is worth more than the rest of the system combined.

## 8. Working hours, sustainability and the Indian reality

### 8.1 The claims, said out loud

India has had this argument in public, twice, loudly.

| Claim               | Who, when                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            | What was actually argued                                                                                  | The pushback                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **70 hours a week** | Infosys co-founder N. R. Narayana Murthy, podcast published 26 October 2023 ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2023/10/30/infosys-founder-narayana-murthy-young-indians-70-hour-work-week))                                                                                              | A _national productivity_ argument: young Indians should work 70-hour weeks to raise the country's output | Health objections; and the observation that national productivity is mostly a function of capital, infrastructure and management quality, not of individual hours                                                                                                                                                                               |
| **90 hours a week** | L&T chairman S. N. Subrahmanyan, January 2025 ([Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/latest/trends/story/90-hour-work-week-debate-larsen-toubro-lt-chairman-sn-subrahmanyan-work-life-balance-corporate-life-narayana-murthy-anand-mahindra-harsh-goenka-460692-2025-01-14)) | A _competitiveness_ argument, delivered with the line "how long can you stare at your wife?"              | Anand Mahindra on quality over hours; Harsh Goenka calling it "a recipe for burnout"; trade unions; and a company clarification that the remarks were "casual in nature" and not policy ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/world/asia/india/90-hour-work-week-row-lt-clarifies-on-chairman-sn-subrahmanyans-remarks-amid-criticism-1.500016075)) |

Notice what neither claim is. Neither is an argument about **a three-person pre-revenue company's output.** They are arguments about a nation's GDP and a 60,000-person contractor's competitiveness, and they are being made _by people instructing other people to work those hours_. The founder-specific version — "a founder must simply outwork everyone" — is a different claim and deserves to be tested on its own evidence rather than borrowed from those two.

### 8.2 What the evidence actually says

**Output stops tracking hours at around fifty a week.** The cleanest natural experiment on the question comes from British munitions plants in the First World War, where hours were varied enormously and physical output was counted exactly. The relationship is decidedly non-linear: below **49 weekly hours** output is proportional to hours; above 49 it rises at a decreasing rate; maximum output occurs at about **63 hours**; and — the sentence to remember — **"output at 70 hours differs little from output at 56 hours"** ([John Pencavel, "The Productivity of Working Hours", IZA Discussion Paper No. 8129, April 2014](https://docs.iza.org/dp8129.pdf)). The honest caveat is that this is manual output, and the caveat cuts the wrong way for a CEO: judgment, writing and negotiation degrade _faster_ under fatigue than piecework does, not slower.

**Above 55 hours, the risk stops being about output.** A joint WHO/ILO analysis estimated that working **55 or more hours a week** carries an estimated **35% higher risk of stroke** and **17% higher risk of dying from ischaemic heart disease** compared with 35–40 hours, and attributed **745,000 deaths in 2016** to long working hours — with South-East Asia among the most affected regions ([WHO/ILO, 17 May 2021](https://www.who.int/news/item/17-05-2021-long-working-hours-increasing-deaths-from-heart-disease-and-stroke-who-ilo)). This is not a lifestyle finding. It is a mortality finding, and it lands squarely on the range both Indian claims were advocating.

**Burnout's third symptom is the one you were trying to buy.** The WHO classifies burn-out in ICD-11 as an occupational phenomenon resulting from unmanaged chronic workplace stress, with three dimensions: energy depletion, mental distance and cynicism about the job, and **reduced professional efficacy** ([WHO, 28 May 2019](https://www.who.int/news/item/28-05-2019-burn-out-an-occupational-phenomenon-international-classification-of-diseases)). You cannot buy more output with hours that produce reduced professional efficacy. That is the trade closing on itself.

**Founders start from a worse baseline than the general population.** In a study of 242 entrepreneurs and 93 comparison subjects, **49% of entrepreneurs reported one or more lifetime mental health conditions** and 72% reported at least one lifetime mental-health concern against 48% of the comparison group, with markedly higher rates of depression, ADHD and substance-use conditions ([Michael A. Freeman et al., "Are Entrepreneurs Touched with Fire?", 2015 — summary](https://michaelafreemanmd.com/Research_files/Are%20Entrepreneurs%20Touched%20with%20Fire-summary.pdf); [research index](https://www.michaelafreemanmd.com/Research.html)). Correlational, self-reported, and a selected sample — **VERIFY LIVE** before quoting the numbers as fact. But the direction is consistent enough across the literature that "founders are unusually resilient, so the general findings do not apply" is the least defensible reading available. The general survey of the overwork research reaches the same place ([Sarah Green Carmichael, "The Research Is Clear: Long Hours Backfire for People and for Companies", HBR, 19 August 2015](https://hbr.org/2015/08/the-research-is-clear-long-hours-backfire-for-people-and-for-companies)).

**The honest other side.** There genuinely are sprints — a launch, an OMC deadline, a fundraise, a production incident — where sixteen-hour days are the right call and the evidence about steady-state weeks does not govern. The house call is about the boundary, not the sprint: **a sprint has a written end date and a named recovery week after it. A sprint with no end date is not a sprint; it is a working week you have not admitted to.**

### 8.3 The specific danger of a family founding team

VSYST is three founder-directors who are family. The classic map of that situation is the Three-Circle Model — family, business and ownership as overlapping circles, producing seven distinct interest groups, developed by Renato Tagiuri and John Davis at Harvard Business School in 1978 ([johndavis.com](https://johndavis.com/three-circle-model-family-business-system/)). Its usual application is succession and conflict. Its application here is narrower and more immediate: **all three of you sit in the overlap of all three circles, which means there is no room you can walk into that is not also work.**

Every ordinary boundary that a non-family company gets for free is missing:

| Boundary a normal company has                    | What VSYST has instead                          | The failure it produces                            |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| A commute that separates the days                | An office attached to the family's own business | Work never ends; it just gets quieter              |
| Someone who is not family to escalate to         | Your father, your brother, your spouse          | Disagreements go unresolved rather than up         |
| A performance conversation with a defined ending | A relationship with no ending                   | Feedback becomes either avoided or permanent       |
| A weekend nobody can reach you on                | A dinner table with the whole board at it       | The board meets nightly and keeps no minutes       |
| An HR function                                   | Nothing                                         | Nothing is written, so the record is family memory |

Four mechanisms, and they are cheap:

1. **A protected zone in time and in space.** Sunday is off — the whole day, for everyone, and the CEO going first is the only version that works. And one physical place in the house where company talk does not happen. Pick the dinner table; it is the one that matters.
2. **Hat-tagging, borrowed from the COO course.** Say which hat is speaking before you speak: _"as a director" / "as your brother" / "as the person who owns the code"._ It sounds artificial for about two weeks and then becomes the most useful sentence in the company ([[03-the-coo-core-value-and-the-ceo-coo-contract|COO 03]] §5.7).
3. **Write the decisions down, that night.** Not because anyone is dishonest, but because family memory is warm, confident and unreliable, and a written [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] row costs ninety seconds ([[16-decisions-delegation-and-not-being-the-bottleneck|lesson 16]]).
4. **Put the ownership circle on paper before it is tested.** Founders' agreement, vesting, roles, what happens if someone leaves or dies — [[C02-founders-agreement-and-vesting-checklist|C02]] and [[03-the-ceo-core-value-and-the-founder-contracts|lesson 03]]. This is the single highest-return hour available to a family founding team, and the reason to do it now is precisely that nothing is wrong yet.

One more asymmetry to say plainly. India's four Labour Codes came into force on **21 November 2025** ([ClearTax](https://cleartax.in/s/new-labour-codes); [KPMG Flash Alert 2026-127](https://kpmg.com/xx/en/our-insights/gms-flash-alert/2026/flash-alert-2026-127.html)), carrying a working-hours regime and overtime rules for **workers** — **VERIFY LIVE** the specific limits and the Chhattisgarh state rules with a labour consultant, since the state rules are still settling. They do not apply to you. A founder-director is not a worker, and no statute caps a promoter's hours. **That gap is filled entirely by the culture you set**, which means that if the CEO visibly works seventy hours and the first employee is legally capped at forty-eight, you have not built one company with a policy. You have built two cultures, and everyone will notice which one gets promoted.

### 8.4 What a genuinely sustainable week looks like at pre-revenue — and the honest arithmetic

Add up Grid A from §4.2 honestly, with breaks removed, and it comes to roughly **58–62 logged hours across six days**. That is above Pencavel's 49-hour knot and above the WHO/ILO's 55-hour risk threshold. This course is not going to pretend otherwise or hide it behind a chart.

So state the trade explicitly, which is what an honest operating design does:

> Grid A is a **season**, not a steady state. It is what a pre-revenue founding team with a live product and no runway problem can hold for two to four quarters, on the specific condition that the five non-negotiables below hold. It is not a design for year three, and a founder still running it in year three has not been disciplined — he has failed to build a company.

**The five non-negotiables. If any of them breaks, the hours come down, not the sleep.**

| Non-negotiable                             | The specific rule                                                                                                               | Why this one                                                                                                                          |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Sleep**                                  | Seven hours, most nights. The 06:30 top-goal block means lights out by 23:00, which means the phone charges outside the bedroom | Every other item on this list is downstream of sleep. Cutting it is not a way of finding hours; it is a way of paying for them at 3×  |
| **One whole day off**                      | Sunday. Not "Sunday morning". Not "Sunday, but I'll just check"                                                                 | Pencavel's data shows output at 48 hours over six days slightly _exceeding_ a seven-day schedule — the day of rest is not sentimental |
| **No work in bed**                         | The bedroom is not an office. This one rule does most of the work of a commute                                                  | Restores the boundary the family-firm structure removed (§8.3)                                                                        |
| **One week off a year, booked in advance** | Booked in January for the year, announced to the team, treated as a system test                                                 | §8.5 — this is the whole point                                                                                                        |
| **The weekly review still happens**        | Friday, one hour, §7.3                                                                                                          | It is the tripwire. When the review stops, the system has already failed and nobody has noticed yet                                   |

**Tripwires — the honest signs that a season has become a condition.** Check them at the quarterly review, not in the moment:

- Sleep under six hours for two consecutive weeks.
- Sunday worked three weeks running.
- The customer minimum (§6.3) red for a month, without a decision having been made to let it go red.
- You cannot name the last customer conversation you enjoyed.
- Irritability with a co-founder is the default rather than the exception — which in a family firm is also a domestic problem.
- The weekly review has been skipped twice.

Two or more, for a month: cut the hours. Not "be more efficient". The evidence in §8.2 says the hours above the knot were not producing much anyway, so cutting them is close to free in output terms and enormous in everything else.

### 8.5 The company that cannot be sold, funded or handed over

Here is the argument that makes all of §8 a business question rather than a wellness one.

> **A CEO who cannot take one week off has not built a company. He has built a job that happens to employ other people.**

Three consequences, none of them abstract:

| If you cannot leave for a week | Then                                                                                                                                                                                            | Which shows up as                                                                                                     |
| ------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **You cannot be acquired**     | An acquirer buys cash flows that survive the founder's departure. Diligence exists precisely to find founder dependency, and it always finds it                                                 | A lower multiple, a heavier earn-out, or no deal ([[08-capital-runway-and-fundraising\|lesson 08]])                   |
| **You are harder to fund**     | An investor is buying an asset expected to outlive the founder's current energy. "Key-man risk" is the name, and a company with one irreplaceable person is a company with one point of failure | A term sheet with heavier founder lock-ins, or a pass                                                                 |
| **You cannot hand over**       | Illness, a family emergency, or simply wanting to stop. Succession is not a late-stage topic; it is an early-stage risk with a late-stage bill                                                  | [[20-the-ceo-own-operating-system-and-succession\|Lesson 20]]; [[C30-pre-mortem-crisis-playbook-and-succession\|C30]] |

The test already exists in the sibling course and this lesson defers to it: the **two-week holiday test** — booked and announced in advance as a system test, not taken by accident, because "an unplanned absence produces heroics and a story; a planned one produces data" ([[20-the-autopilot-test-and-scaling-the-machine|COO 20]] §3). The COO course sets the pass condition: the company shipped, sold, supported and complied for two weeks while both of you were away.

The CEO's version starts smaller and starts now. **One week, booked, announced, this financial year.** Before you book it, write the honest answer to one question: _what falls over if the technical CEO disappears for seven days today?_ At VSYST the list is short and predictable — releases and the release gate, production incidents and on-call, the app-store accounts, the AWS root account, the Easebuzz relationship, and whatever only lives in one person's head. Each of those is a row in the second-person table and the credentials escrow that [[13-compliance-calendar-risk-and-insurance|COO 13]] §5 and [[20-the-autopilot-test-and-scaling-the-machine|COO 20]] §4 already specify. Fill the rows; then book the week.

## 9. Travel — the district reality

### 9.1 The map, in hours rather than kilometres

VSYST's written strategy is district-by-district saturation of Indian fuel distribution, starting from Raipur ([[06-strategy-ii-moats-positioning-and-the-business-model|lesson 06]] §2). That sentence is four words of strategy and several hundred hours of driving, and no calendar design survives if it pretends otherwise. Chhattisgarh has **33 districts** across five divisions — Raipur, Durg, Bilaspur, Bastar and Surguja — after five new districts were carved out in September 2022 ([List of districts of Chhattisgarh, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_districts_of_Chhattisgarh); [Rau's IAS compass](https://compass.rauias.com/gk/districts-in-chhattisgarh/)). Raipur sits near the centre of the state, which is the good news and also the trap: it makes half the state look reachable and hides the fact that the other half is a two-night commitment.

Stop thinking in kilometres. A dealer meeting is priced in **door-to-door hours**, and the honest planning constant outside the Raipur–Bhilai corridor is **40–45 km/h door to door**, not the 60 km/h the maps app quotes — because the quote excludes the pump you could not find, the level crossing, the tea stop your domain-expert director will insist on, and the forty minutes the dealer keeps you waiting because a tanker arrived.

**The arithmetic that should change your behaviour.** Take just those ten headquarters. Visited as ten separate return trips from Raipur, that is roughly **3,150 km** of driving — about **70 hours** at 45 km/h, or nearly two full working weeks in a car, before a single conversation happens. **Eighteen hours saved per pass through the state. Two and a half working days. That is the entire case for batching, and it is a bigger number than anything a productivity system will ever hand you.**

### 9.2 The ten rules of a district week

Batching is not "travel less". It is a set of rules that convert scattered days into one concentrated week, and each rule exists because its absence produces a specific, recognisable waste.

| 2   | **The trip does not leave until the meetings are confirmed** — five for an overnight, eight for a two-night run | Driving 300 km to Jagdalpur on the strength of a WhatsApp _"haan ji, aa jaiye"_. **The Bastar caveat, said plainly.** The Bastar-division districts — Bijapur, Sukma, Dantewada, Narayanpur, Kondagaon, Kanker, Bastar — sit in territory that has been security-affected for two decades, and the official picture has changed fast and favourably: the Union Home Ministry's classification of Left-Wing-Extremism-affected districts has shrunk dramatically over 2024–26, with the bulk of Chhattisgarh's Bastar districts moving into legacy or monitoring categories while remaining under the Security Related Expenditure scheme ([MHA, Left Wing Extremism Division](https://www.mha.gov.in/en/divisionofmha/left-wing-extremism-division); [classification summary](https://www.drishtiias.com/daily-updates/daily-news-analysis/classification-of-districts-affected-by-lwe)). **VERIFY LIVE** — this list moves every few months and a two-year-old mental model is the wrong one in both directions. The operating rules for a Bastar trip are unchanged regardless: **travel in daylight, tell someone your route, sleep in the district headquarters and not in a smaller town, and take the domain-expert director or a local dealer with you rather than going as a stranger.** The last one is a sales rule as much as a safety rule.

### 9.3 What the hours in the car are actually good for

A batched district week generates ten to fourteen hours in a vehicle. Those hours are not lost — but they are only usable if you are honest about what a moving car on a Chhattisgarh state highway can and cannot support.

| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Voice-note debriefs** — the three lines per dealer (§6.3), recorded within ten minutes of pulling out of the forecourt, while it is still specific                                                                                                                                       | **Writing code.** A laptop on your knees between Kanker and Kondagaon produces a headache and one uncommitted file                |
| **Booking the next stop** — calls that confirm tomorrow's meetings. Pure **K** time, and the car is the best place in the week for it                                                                                                                                                      | **Decisions that need numbers on a screen.** Runway, pricing, a cap-table question — these wait for the hotel desk                |
| **The catch-up 1:1** with whichever director is in the car. Four uninterrupted hours with a co-founder is a conversation you will not get in Raipur                                                                                                                                        | **Anything requiring a document open in front of two people.** Reading a contract aloud in a moving car is how clauses get missed |
| **Listening** — the podcast, the audiobook, the recording of last quarter's dealer calls. Low bandwidth, high compounding                                                                                                                                                                  | **Support triage.** You will be half-present and the tenant will know. Route it to the unblock window                             |
| **Thinking, with a written question** — the question written the night before (§6.2). The car is the closest thing a founder-CEO gets to Graham's shower: nobody can reach you and there is nothing to click ([Paul Graham, "The Top Idea in Your Mind"](https://paulgraham.com/top.html)) | **The weekly ops meeting, chaired.** Attend it from the car; do not run it from the car ([[08-the-operating-cadence\|COO 08]])    |

**The one non-negotiable.** If _you_ are the one driving, you are driving, full stop. The AAA Foundation's cognitive-distraction work found that hands-free and voice-based systems are not risk-free — mental distraction persists for as long as **27 seconds after the task ends**, and the National Safety Council's position is that hands-free devices are no safer than handheld because the brain, not the hand, is the constrained resource ([AAA Foundation, _Measuring Cognitive Distraction in the Automobile II_](https://aaafoundation.org/research/measuring-cognitive-distraction-automobile-ii-assessing-vehicle-voice-based-interactive-technologies/); [coverage, Insurance Journal, 2015](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/national/2015/10/26/386116.htm)). This is the concrete reason rule 7 exists: **a driver is not a luxury, it is what makes ten hours of transit into ten hours of work instead of ten hours of risk.**

Two practical notes for this specific product and this specific state. **Connectivity is not uniform** — plan for stretches of Bastar and Surguja where data drops entirely. Download what you need before leaving Raipur, keep the demo working offline where the app allows it, and never build a dealer demo that requires a live server round-trip you cannot guarantee. And **the demo happens on the dealer's phone, not yours.** DZZLO is mobile-first and sold to people who live on WhatsApp; a demo on your laptop teaches the dealer that the product is a laptop product ([[13-product-and-technology-leadership-for-a-technical-ceo|lesson 13]] §6.2's cheap-handset rule applies on the road with double force).

### 9.4 Keeping the strategy and money beats alive during a selling week

This is the part that actually fails. A founder goes to Bastar for three days, comes back with nine conversations and a full pipeline, and discovers that the month has no strategy hours, no runway re-base, no stakeholder update, and a weekly review that never happened. The trip was a success and the month was not.

The fix is not heroism. It is deciding **in advance** which beats survive a travel week, in what reduced form, and which are explicitly suspended.

| Beat                                 | What a naive travel week does to it          | The minimum viable version on the road                                                                                                                                                                        | Verdict                                                            |
| ------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Top goal, 06:30–08:30**            | Skipped — "I'm travelling"                   | Held, in the hotel room. A district hotel at 6:30 AM has no family, no office and no support queue — **it is the best deep-work environment a founder-CEO gets all month**                                    | **Survives, and improves**                                         |
| **Money block (Fri AM)**             | Skipped; runway goes un-rebased for a month  | 45 minutes at the hotel desk before checkout: re-base [[C13-runway-burn-and-scenario-planner\|C13]], read the bank balance yourself, chase one collection                                                     | **Non-negotiable. Never suspend this one**                         |
| **Strategy block (Fri 11:00–16:00)** | Silently eaten                               | Split: the _question_ is written before you leave; the _thinking_ happens in the car; the _writing_ happens Saturday morning at home. The rule from §6.2 holds — **move it in the same act, never cancel it** | **Survives, relocated**                                            |
| **Weekly review (§7.3)**             | Forgotten                                    | A voice note on the drive home, transcribed that evening into [[C27-ceo-weekly-template-and-calendar-audit\|C27]]. Six questions, twenty minutes                                                              | **Survives. It is the tripwire (§8.4)**                            |
| **Weekly ops meeting**               | Missed entirely                              | Attend by phone from the car, on time, prepared, reporting your own to-dos. Do not chair it and do not reschedule the company around your travel                                                              | **Survives, dialled in**                                           |
| **Unblock window (16:00)**           | Becomes all-day interruption                 | Moves to 20:00 from the hotel, announced before you leave. The four-item escalation list (§6.4) is unchanged                                                                                                  | **Survives, moved**                                                |
| **Founder 1:1s**                     | Skipped                                      | Held in the car if that director is with you; otherwise 30 minutes by phone after dinner                                                                                                                      | **Survives**                                                       |
| **Build blocks**                     | Fragmented into useless twenty-minute slices | **Suspended, deliberately and out loud.** Announce the release freeze before you go                                                                                                                           | **Suspend. Say so in advance**                                     |
| **Customer minimum (5/week)**        | —                                            | Massively over-delivered: a district week produces a month's worth                                                                                                                                            | **Bank the surplus, but do not let the following week go to zero** |

Two structural devices make this hold.

**Device 1 — the month has a shape, and the district week has a fixed slot in it.** Do not let the trip land wherever the dealers happen to be free; put it in the same week every month so the other three weeks can be planned around it.

| Week       | Shape                                                                                                  | The beat that anchors it                           |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------- |
| **Week 1** | Normal Grid A. Build-heavy. Monthly business review lands here once the CA's close pack arrives (§5.2) | **MBR — the capital-allocation hour**              |
| **Week 2** | **The district week.** Two to four days out, per §9.2                                                  | The customer surplus                               |
| **Week 3** | Normal Grid A, plus the trip's follow-through: proposals out, losses logged, the pipeline updated      | The Friday strategy block, at full length          |
| **Week 4** | Normal Grid A. Stakeholder update written and sent (§5.3); month closed                                | **The written update — the enforced-honesty beat** |

**Device 2 — the re-entry morning is blocked before you leave.** The morning after a district week is not a normal morning, and treating it as one is how nine conversations turn into three remembered impressions. Block it: three hours, before anything else touches you. Write up every dealer, update the pipeline in [[C10-founder-led-sales-pipeline-and-script|C10]], log every loss reason, file the new objections in the bank, and write the one line the trip taught you that the plan did not predict — the line that feeds the monthly review's fourth question (§5.2) and the quarterly learnings list (§5.4). **A trip without a re-entry block was a road trip, not a selling week.**

### 9.5 When not to go at all

The most valuable travel rule is the one that cancels the trip. Travel is the most expensive way to have a conversation, and a founder who enjoys the road — and technical founders often do, because a highway is one of the few places nobody can interrupt you — will find reasons.

Price it honestly first. A two-night Bastar run, self-driven, is roughly:

| Line                                            | Illustrative                            | Note                                                                                                                                                                        |
| ----------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Fuel, ~610 km round trip @ ~12 km/l @ ~₹90–95/l | ≈ ₹4,700                                | **VERIFY LIVE** — Chhattisgarh's fuel VAT makes Raipur pump prices materially different from neighbouring states, which is also a fact your product's users live with daily |
| Tolls                                           | ≈ ₹400–600                              |                                                                                                                                                                             |
| Two hotel nights + food, two people             | ≈ ₹6,000–8,000                          |                                                                                                                                                                             |
| Driver, three days                              | ≈ ₹3,000                                | Rule 7. Cheaper than the alternative                                                                                                                                        |
| **Cash total**                                  | **≈ ₹15,000**                           |                                                                                                                                                                             |
| **CEO hours consumed**                          | **~36 hours, of which ~12 are transit** | This is the real number. At a three-person company it is about 15% of the CEO's productive month                                                                            |

Now the gate. **Go when all three are true; sell remotely when any one is false:**

1. **Density.** At least six named pumps or distributors in the cluster, with owner names — not "there are pumps in Jagdalpur". [[C06-market-map-sizing-and-segments|C06]] is where that list lives; the official retail-outlet counts by state are PPAC's ([PPAC, Retail Outlets](https://ppac.gov.in/infrastructure/retail-outlets)) — **VERIFY LIVE** and treat them as a ceiling, not a pipeline.
2. **An anchor who has already said yes on the phone.** One confirmed meeting with a decision-maker. Without it you are prospecting at ₹1,200 an hour.
3. **A local reference or an escort.** A dealer who will vouch for you, an association contact, or the domain-expert director travelling with you. In Indian fuel distribution, a cold visit from a stranger converts at a fraction of a warm one, and [[14-partnerships-ecosystem-and-founder-relationships|lesson 14]] is about manufacturing exactly this.

If the gate fails, the district is not un-sellable — it is **not yet visit-worthy.** Work it by phone and WhatsApp video until someone says _"aap aa jaiye"_, which is the sentence that converts a district from a prospect list into a trip. And keep the honest counterfactual in view: **the alternative to a Bastar trip is not nothing.** It is thirty-six hours spent making Raipur district — where you can be at any pump inside forty minutes — the reference account that makes every future district cheaper to sell ([[06-strategy-ii-moats-positioning-and-the-business-model|lesson 06]] §2 on saturating one district before touching a second).

## 10. At VSYST — applying this now

Everything above is method. This section is the instruction, and it is deliberately narrow: one week, two defended blocks, one meeting to stop attending. A calendar redesign that changes nine things changes nothing, because by Thursday you will be running the old week with a guilty conscience.

**Start from the honest position.** Three founder-directors, no employees, no CFO, no outside capital, pre-revenue. One product live on both app stores. A live Easebuzz onboarding and a live IOCL conversation. A CEO who still writes the code that runs in production, with an unmerged correctness fix and a tenant-isolation gap sitting in the repo ([[13-product-and-technology-leadership-for-a-technical-ceo|lesson 13]] §12). That company does not have a time-management problem. It has a **visibility** problem: the only work with a natural deadline is the work that ships, so shipping wins every collision, and the four CEO jobs lose silently and without anyone objecting — because at three people there is nobody whose job it is to object.

### 10.1 The week to run from Monday

Take Grid A from §4.2 exactly as written and run it unmodified for **four weeks** before you improve it. Four weeks is the shortest span that produces a fair verdict: one week tells you only which meeting collided with which, and a grid you keep editing is a grid you never test.

Do these seven things before Monday morning. They take about ninety minutes in total.

| #   | Do this                                                                                                                                                                                                 | Time   | Why it is on the list                                                                                                                                            |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | **Put all six named blocks in the calendar as real events, with the job-code letter in the title** — `D — Top goal`, `K — Money`, `D — Strategy`, `K — Field day`, `P — 1:1s`, `X-B — Deep build (Wed)` | 20 min | An unwritten block is an intention. The letter in the title is what makes next month's audit take thirty minutes instead of three hours (§1.3)                   |
| 2   | **Send the two field-day invites to actual dealers** — named people, Tuesday and Thursday, 11:00–16:00                                                                                                  | 30 min | Grid A's customer days are the only blocks with an external counterparty. Booked, they are the hardest blocks in the week; unbooked, they are the softest        |
| 3   | **Announce the 16:00 unblock window and the four-item escalation list** to both co-founders — and to the family members who are not employees                                                           | 10 min | §6.4. The list is _production is down; money is moving out; a dealer is about to walk; a statutory deadline is inside 48 hours._ Everything else waits for 16:00 |
| 4   | **Add `CEO customer conversations — n / 5` as a row on the COO's weekly scorecard**                                                                                                                     | 5 min  | §6.3. It is a number, not a block, and it goes where two other directors will read it                                                                            |
| 5   | **Stop chairing the Monday ops meeting** — tell the COO-seat holder in one sentence that he runs it from this week and you will report your to-dos like everyone else                                   | 5 min  | §3.2's most common failure at three people. You keep the seat; you give up the pen                                                                               |
| 6   | **Write Friday's strategy question tonight** — one sentence, on paper, before the block exists                                                                                                          | 10 min | §6.2. A thinking block with no written question becomes an email block by 11:20                                                                                  |
| 7   | **Block the re-entry morning and the release freeze for next month's district week**                                                                                                                    | 10 min | §9.4's Device 2. Book it now, while it is cheap; in three weeks it will be a negotiation                                                                         |

Two more that take no time and change the week's shape more than any of the seven: **Wednesday takes no meetings at all**, and **Sunday is off, the whole day**. Both are decisions you make once and then merely defend.

### 10.2 The two blocks that are non-negotiable — and the honest reason it is these two

Everything in Grid A is worth having. Only two of them get the word _non-negotiable_, and the criterion for choosing them is not which is most valuable — it is **which one has the weakest natural defence**, because value defends itself and a block with no defender dies without a meeting ever being cancelled.

| Non-negotiable                                 | When                                  | Why it dies quietly                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   | The defence                                                                                                                                                                     | The rule                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **The money block**                            | Fri 09:00–11:00 (**K**)               | There is no CFO, no investor asking, no board packet due, no customer waiting. Nobody on earth chases a bootstrapped founder for his own runway number, and the work is unpleasant, un-shippable and has no completion feeling. It is the most postponable two hours in the company and the only ones that can end it | It is the **only** block that survives a travel week intact (§9.4) — 45 minutes at a hotel desk if that is what is available, but never zero                                    | **Never suspended, never shortened below 45 minutes, never delegated to the CA.** The CA prepares; you read the bank balance yourself                                                           |
| **The two field days plus the 5-a-week floor** | Tue + Thu 11:00–16:00 (**K** / **D**) | It loses to a production incident every single time, and losing to a production incident always feels correct in the moment. Four of those in a row is a month with no customer contact and no one noticed                                                                                                            | The dual mechanism from §6.3: the **block** holds the slot, the **number on the scorecard** catches it when the block loses. The block will lose sometimes; the number will not | **The number never drops.** If both field days are eaten, the five conversations still happen — by phone, at 19:00, from the office. Report the honest figure on Monday even when it is `2 / 5` |

The reason these two and not the obvious third is worth stating, because a reader who has absorbed §6 will expect the strategy block here. **The Friday strategy block already has a mechanism** — §6.2's rule that it may be _moved in the same act_ but never cancelled, and §9.4's split of question-car-writing that keeps it alive on the road. It is defended. Money and customer contact at a pre-revenue company are not, and they are the two jobs where a missed month is invisible until a quarter later.

And the honest corollary, said plainly: **when the week breaks, the build block is what gives.** Not the money hour, not the field day. A technical founder-CEO's instinct is exactly backwards here — build feels like the load-bearing work because it is the work with a visible artefact at the end. At pre-revenue, with a product already live in both stores, the marginal hour of code is worth less than the marginal conversation with a dealer who has not yet paid you. Write that sentence down, because you will need to read it back to yourself in about eleven days.

### 10.3 The first meeting to drop

One, this month, dropped completely rather than shortened — and it is the vendor demo.

> **Drop the payment-gateway / cloud / SaaS "solution walkthrough". Reply asking for pricing and documentation in writing.**

It goes first for four reasons that are specific to VSYST and not to founders in general. It is **live right now** — the Easebuzz onboarding guarantees at least one such invitation this month. It is the **most convincingly disguised** waste on the calendar, because attending feels like diligence and declining feels like sloppiness, when in fact the opposite is true: [[C26-partnership-evaluation-and-mou-checklist|C26]]'s evaluation runs on documents and a walkthrough gives you none of them. It costs **more than its length** — a 60-minute afternoon call breaks a maker's afternoon into two useless halves, so the true price is closer to three hours ([Paul Graham, "Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule", 2009](https://paulgraham.com/makersschedule.html)). And it is **completely safe to decline** — a vendor who will not send pricing in writing has told you something useful for free.

The next three, in order, so the list does not have to be rebuilt each month:

| Rank | Drop                                                               | Replace it with                                                                                                                                     |
| ---- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 2    | **Association general body meetings with no speaking slot** (§3.4) | Attend only with a slot, or with five pre-booked pump visits around it. Three hours of X-T and X-N is the most expensive networking in Chhattisgarh |
| 3    | **"Let's explore synergies" calls**                                | [[C26-partnership-evaluation-and-mou-checklist\|C26]]'s first three questions, by email. What comes back tells you whether a call exists            |
| 4    | **Any internal meeting held to share status**                      | The COO's daily async check-in ([[08-the-operating-cadence\|COO 08]]). A status meeting at three people is a document read aloud                    |

And the structural change that belongs alongside the drop, because it returns more hours than all four rows combined: **you stop facilitating the weekly ops meeting.** That is not a meeting dropped — you still attend, still prepared, still reporting your own to-dos. It is a pen handed back to the seat that owns it. At three people this is the single most common way a CEO accidentally absorbs the operating system, and every hour of it is an hour the four jobs did not get.

### 10.4 The rest of the first month, and what not to do

- **Run the calendar audit on last month before you plan next month.** Thirty minutes, §1.3's six steps, four percentages next to the kernel. Do it before the new grid has run, so you have a genuine before-and-after rather than a story.
- **Write and send the first monthly stakeholder update within two weeks**, even with no investors — to the CA, the domain-expert director's own network, and one person outside the family whose opinion you fear slightly ([[C23-stakeholder-and-investor-update|C23]]). Ninety minutes of enforced honesty is the cheapest governance the company will ever buy (§5.3).
- **Hold the monthly business review with the numbers you have**, not the ones you wish you had. One reallocation decision, written into [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] with the reasoning (§5.2).
- **Slot the district week into week 2, permanently.** Same week every month, so the other three weeks can be planned around it (§9.4, Device 1). Apply §9.5's three-part gate before booking anything — density, an anchor who said yes on the phone, and a local reference.
- **Book one week off this financial year, announced in advance as a system test** (§8.5). Write the "what falls over" list first; it will be short, specific and uncomfortable, and it is the real deliverable.
- **Do not install a productivity system.** No new app, no new tracker, no fifteen-minute time-logging. Half-hour blocks reconstructed monthly get 90% of the signal ([First Round Review on Sam Corcos's 7,922 tracked hours](https://review.firstround.com/an-exact-breakdown-of-how-one-ceo-spent-his-first-two-years-of-company-building/)) and are the version you will still be running in March.
- **Do not add an all-hands yet.** Three directors who eat together do not need one. The trigger is the first non-director hire, and then it starts immediately (§3.1).
- **Do not introduce Grid B early.** It assumes a COO with real scope, direct reports and someone else in the codebase. Running a fifteen-person week at three people produces meetings with nobody in them.
- **Do not negotiate with yourself about the 06:30 block for the first four weeks.** It is the only slot in a family firm with a lock on it (§4.2), and the argument for skipping it is always available and always plausible.

## 11. Exercises

**11.1 — The calendar audit of last month, coded against the four jobs (45 min, alone).** Reconstruct last month from the calendar, WhatsApp, the call log, git history and the notes folder, in half-hour blocks. Code every block with exactly one letter from §1.2 — **D**, **K**, **P**, **S**, or one of the X buckets — obeying both rules: code by purpose rather than content, and no half-hours. Total by code, convert to percentages, and write the four numbers that matter next to a fifth for everything else. Then do the part that makes it a strategy diagnostic rather than a time sheet: open [[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager|C04]], read the guiding policy out loud, read the percentages out loud, and write **one paragraph** on the gap and **one change** — not five. Enter the totals in the `CEO Dashboard` tab of [vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx](toolkit/vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx) so this becomes a twelve-month trend rather than twelve separate embarrassments. _Artefact: a coded audit of last month in [[C27-ceo-weekly-template-and-calendar-audit|C27]], five percentages in the workbook, one paragraph, one change, dated._

**11.2 — Fill C27 and block out next week for real (40 min, this weekend).** Open [[C27-ceo-weekly-template-and-calendar-audit|C27]] and fill the weekly template with **next week**, not a typical week. Every one of §4.2's six named blocks goes in as a calendar event with its job-code letter in the title and its deliverable in the description — the line that says what the block must produce or it did not happen. Then do the three things that separate a filled template from a plan: send the two field-day invites to **named dealers** and get one confirmation before Sunday night; write Friday's strategy question on paper tonight; and announce the 16:00 unblock window with its four-item escalation list to both co-founders in writing. At the end, compare the blocked week against 11.1's percentages and write one line predicting what next month's audit will show. _Artefact: a filled C27 weekly template, next week's calendar populated with six coded blocks and a real deliverable on each, one confirmed dealer meeting, and a written prediction to be marked next month._

**11.3 — The stop-attending list (30 min, then 10 min of sending).** Build your own version of §3.1's inventory: every recurring meeting you currently attend, whose meeting it is, why you are in the room, and — the column nobody writes — **the exit criterion**, the observable condition under which you would stop attending. Now write the list this exercise is actually for: **the meetings you will stop attending, starting this month**, each with one sentence of reasoning and the date you stop. It must include the vendor walkthrough from §10.3 and at least two more, and it must state explicitly that you are handing the Monday ops meeting's facilitation back to the COO seat while keeping your seat in the room. Write the decline sentence once — the English and the WhatsApp versions from §3.3, neither of which is a maybe — and keep it where you can paste it. Then send the two messages that make it real: the handover sentence to the COO seat, and the "send me pricing and docs in writing" reply to the vendor. _Artefact: a meeting inventory with an exit criterion on every row, a dated stop-attending list of at least three meetings, a written decline sentence, and two messages already sent._

**11.4 — Put the customer minimum on the dashboard and defend it out loud (30 min, with both co-founders).** Add `CEO customer conversations — n / 5` to the COO's weekly scorecard, next to cash and pipeline, and copy §6.3's two-column definition of what counts and what does not underneath it so the definition cannot soften later. Then defend the number in the room, against the three objections that will actually be raised: _five is arbitrary_ (it is — it is chosen to be survivable in a bad week and to produce sixty-five conversations a quarter, which is enough to see a pattern); _it should drop when we are shipping_ (it does not — §2.3's never-zero rule, and a missed month is invisible until a quarter later); and _support tickets should count_ (they do not — that is service, not discovery, and the row that lets them in is the row that kills the instrument). Agree the reporting rule explicitly: **the CEO reads the figure out in the Monday ops meeting, including when it is red, and a red month requires a decision recorded in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] rather than an apology.** _Artefact: a live scorecard row with a written definition, a three-objection defence you have actually said aloud, and an agreed reporting rule — plus the first week's honest number._

**11.5 — Give the month a shape and book the district week (35 min).** Take §9.4's Device 1 and write your own four-week month: which week is the district week, which week the monthly business review lands in, which week carries the trip's follow-through, and which week the stakeholder update goes out. Fix the district week to the **same week every month** and put the next three in the calendar today. For the first trip, run §9.5's three-part gate in writing — density (at least six named pumps or distributors with owner names, from [[C06-market-map-sizing-and-segments|C06]]), an anchor who has said yes on the phone, and a local reference or escort — and record a go or a no-go with the reason. If it is a go, book the loop by §9.2's rules: geographic cluster, anchor first, overbooked by a third, no night driving, a hired driver, no release that week, and the **re-entry morning blocked before you leave**. If it is a no-go, write what would have to become true, and what you will do with those thirty-six hours in Raipur district instead. _Artefact: a written month shape with three district weeks in the calendar, a dated go/no-go gate with its three answers, and either a booked routed loop with a blocked re-entry morning or a written trigger for the trip you did not take._
