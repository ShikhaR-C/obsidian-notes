# 18 — Risk, Crisis and the Hard Things

_Phase 4 · Running the Company and Yourself · Months 6–24. After this lesson you can tell a problem (which has a method) from a hard thing (which has only trade-offs); run a three-founder pre-mortem that produces a scored, owned, dated list of the ways VSYST dies; draw the line between the existential risk you own and the operational risk the COO owns; work a written playbook for each of the ten hardest conversations a founder-CEO faces, including firing a relative and telling a dealer his ledger was wrong; run the first hour, first day and first week of a real crisis without going silent and without speculating; pre-commit triggers in the calm so the decision is already made when the alarm sounds; take the psychological load seriously enough to build a support system before you need one; and — if it comes to it — know what stopping honourably looks like in an Indian Pvt Ltd._

## Explain-it-like-I'm-5

There is a potato cold storage outside Rajnandgaon. Thirty thousand quintals in six chambers, farmers' stock held from March to November, and the owner earns a rent per bag. His whole business is one promise: **the temperature stays at 2–4°C**. Everything else is detail.

He has learned to sort his worries into two piles. The first pile has **methods**. The compressor needs servicing every 2,000 hours — there is a schedule. The staff wages fall due on the 7th — there is a calendar. The insurance renews in June — there is a reminder. None of these are pleasant, but none of them require him to be brave. Someone competent, following a list, handles them. That is the pile his manager runs.

The second pile has no method. In August one compressor half-fails during a four-day power cut and the diesel in the tank will run one chamber properly or all six badly. Chamber 3 belongs to eleven small farmers with two hundred bags each. Chamber 5 belongs to one trader who is also the man who lends him working capital every February. There is no correct answer in a book. Whatever he chooses, somebody's potatoes rot and somebody stops trusting him. He cannot delegate it, he cannot postpone it past tonight, and he will carry it either way. **That is a hard thing.** The only question is whether he decides at 11 PM with a clear head or at 4 AM when the diesel is already gone.

There are three things he does that this lesson is about. He sat down last winter — in the calm — and wrote the sentence _"if the diesel drops below 400 litres, we save chambers 3 and 4 and tell 5 and 6 by phone within the hour"_, so tonight's decision was made by a calmer version of himself. He tells the eleven farmers **before** the potatoes are ruined rather than after, because in this business people forgive damage and never forgive silence. And once a year he sits with his brother and his son and asks the strangest question in the business: _it is two years from now and the cold storage has shut down — what happened?_ They write their answers separately, on paper, before anyone speaks. The list they compare is the most honest document the family produces all year.

Your company is the cold storage. The ledger is the temperature.

## 1. Problems and hard things

### 1.1 The difference, and a test for it

Ben Horowitz opens _The Hard Thing About Hard Things_ on exactly this distinction ([a16z — book page](https://a16z.com/books/the-hard-thing-about-hard-things/)): setting a big goal is not hard; **laying people off when you miss it** is hard. Hiring great people is not hard; **firing your loyal friend who is no longer great** is hard. His conclusion is deliberately unsatisfying — "there's no recipe for really complicated, dynamic situations… no recipe for building a high-tech company" — and the value of the book is that it refuses to pretend otherwise.

A working test, in three questions. If the answer to all three is yes, you are holding a hard thing, and you should stop looking for the framework that dissolves it.

| Question                                                                        | A problem                                                                                     | A hard thing                                                     |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Is there a method that a competent person could follow to a correct answer?** | Yes — an SOP, a checklist, a formula, a lawyer's opinion                                      | No. Two reasonable people with the same facts choose differently |
| **Can it be resolved without harming anyone you owe something to?**             | Usually. The cost is money or time                                                            | No. Somebody who trusted you is worse off whichever way you go   |
| **Can you delegate it?**                                                        | Yes — that is what [[16-decisions-delegation-and-not-being-the-bottleneck\|lesson 16]] is for | No. Delegating it _is_ the wrong answer, and everyone will know  |

Most of what feels agonising at 2 AM is actually a **problem wearing a hard thing's clothes** — it feels heavy because you have not gathered the facts, not because the facts are unbearable. A GST notice is a problem: the CA has a method. A production outage is a problem: [[T17-incident-postmortem|T17]] has a method. A dealer disputing an invoice is a problem: the ledger has a method. Run the three questions before you spend a night on something. The relief of discovering that a "hard thing" was only an ungathered fact is one of the underrated returns of this lesson.

The residue that passes all three tests is small — perhaps six to ten moments in a company's life. Those are what §5 catalogues.

### 1.2 Why there is no recipe, and what to do instead

There is no recipe because a hard thing is by construction a **conflict between two obligations you actually hold**. To the loyal employee and to the twelve other people whose salaries depend on the company's survival. To the co-founder who is your brother and to the shareholders (also your family). To the dealer whose ledger you got wrong and to the company's ability to keep selling in that district. A framework can order your thinking, but no framework can pay both debts, which is why they hurt.

What replaces the recipe is not courage in the abstract. It is four unglamorous things:

1. **Decide the principle in the calm.** You cannot decide _fairly_ at 2 AM, but you can decide _consistently_ if the principle was written in daylight. This is what §7's pre-committed triggers are for, and it is the highest-leverage practice in this lesson.
2. **Gather the facts to the point of diminishing returns, then stop.** Hard things attract more analysis than they can absorb. The analysis becomes a way of not deciding — the classic form of [escalation of commitment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escalation_of_commitment), where each further week of "let's see how it goes" is easier than the sentence you already know you must say.
3. **Choose the version of the pain you can defend out loud.** Not the smallest pain — the most defensible one. You will be explaining this decision for years, including to yourself.
4. **Say it directly, once, to the person's face, and then do not re-open it.** Almost all the additional damage in a hard thing comes from softening, hinting, staging it in instalments, or reversing under emotional pressure a week later.

### 1.3 What the CEO's job actually is here

Fred Wilson's three-item reduction — set direction, get the best people, keep cash in the bank — is the spine of this course, but there is a fourth thing that never appears on the list and is the reason the seat is paid, feared and lonely: **the CEO is the person who does not flinch from the decision nobody else can be asked to make.** Every other director can say "that is the CEO's call" and be right. You cannot say it to anyone.

Two failure modes bracket this and both are common in family-founded companies:

- **The flincher.** Postpones, hopes the situation resolves itself, gives the underperformer "one more quarter", waits for the co-founder to volunteer to leave, keeps a project alive because killing it is awkward. Nothing is decided; the cost is paid anyway, later, larger, and with the company's credibility attached.
- **The brute.** Mistakes hardness for harshness. Fires by WhatsApp, announces a salary cut in an all-hands without a prior 1:1, treats "wartime CEO" as licence to be unpleasant. The decision may be right and the execution destroys the trust that made you able to make it.

The target is a third posture: **decide early, execute cleanly, carry the cost visibly.** The cold-storage owner does not enjoy telling chamber 5. He tells chamber 5 himself, at 11 PM, by phone, with the number of bags in front of him.

## 2. Wartime and peacetime

### 2.1 The two modes

Horowitz's most useful operating idea is that a CEO runs one of two fundamentally different companies, and the same person must be able to switch between them. "Peacetime in business means those times when a company has a large advantage vs. the competition in its core market, and its market is growing… In wartime, a company is fending off an imminent existential threat" ([a16z — Peacetime CEO/Wartime CEO, 14 April 2011](https://a16z.com/peacetime-ceo-wartime-ceo/)). His compressed version: _"Peacetime CEO aims to expand the market. Wartime CEO aims to win the market."_

| Dimension               | Peacetime CEO                                                        | Wartime CEO                                                                 | The failure when you mismatch                                                                      |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Objective**           | Expand the market; broaden the product; invest in the long game      | Win the one battle that decides survival                                    | A wartime posture in peacetime creates permanent crisis fatigue and burns people out for no reason |
| **Planning horizon**    | Quarters and years                                                   | Weeks                                                                       | Quarterly OKRs during a cash crisis are an elaborate way to avoid the one thing that matters       |
| **Decision style**      | Consensus-building, protocol, developing people through the decision | Direct, fast, sometimes unilateral; protocol suspended where it costs speed | Consensus-seeking while the runway burns is not humility, it is abdication                         |
| **Deviation from plan** | Tolerated — creativity is upside                                     | Not tolerated — a single misfire can be fatal                               | Punishing deviation in peacetime kills the experiments that find the next business                 |
| **Communication**       | Sets a broad vision, lets teams interpret                            | Says the specific thing, repeatedly, and checks it landed                   | Vague inspirational messaging in a crisis is heard as "he doesn't know either"                     |
| **Hiring bar**          | Hire ahead of need; build bench                                      | Hire only what wins the battle; freeze everything else                      | Hiring "for the plan" while default-dead is the single most common bootstrapped mistake            |
| **Cost posture**        | Invest into growth                                                   | Cut to the bone that keeps the loop alive                                   | Cutting in peacetime starves the company; investing in wartime ends it                             |

Sequoia's 2008 _R.I.P. Good Times_ deck is the canonical wartime memo to founders — cash, costs, survival, in that order ([Sequoia — R.I.P. Good Times, 2008](https://articles.sequoiacap.com/rip-good-times)). Read it not for its market call, which was of its moment, but for the tone: unsentimental, specific, and aimed entirely at the length of the runway.

### 2.2 The specific failure: peacetime plays during a cash crisis

This is worth naming precisely, because it is the version that kills bootstrapped Indian SaaS companies and it always feels responsible while it is happening.

| Peacetime play                                               | What it looks like during a cash crisis                                                                                   | What wartime requires instead                                                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| A quarterly OKR cycle                                        | Three founders spend two days writing objectives for a quarter the company may not finish                                 | One number, one owner, weekly: cash in, and the date it runs out                                               |
| "Let's build the thing that unlocks the next segment"        | Six weeks of engineering aimed at a market you have never sold to                                                         | Ship only what closes a deal already in the pipeline ([[C10-founder-led-sales-pipeline-and-script\|C10]])      |
| Consensus on every decision                                  | Three relatives negotiating each call; nothing moves for a week                                                           | Decide, log it in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log\|C29]], move; review after                       |
| "We'll fix the cost base after the raise"                    | Burn stays flat while the runway shortens                                                                                 | Cut first, raise second — investors fund a company that has already made its hard cuts                         |
| Protecting morale by not saying the number                   | Nobody outside the CEO knows the runway; people plan for a future that does not exist                                     | Tell the team the real number and the real date ([[12-communication-the-ceo-as-chief-storyteller\|lesson 12]]) |
| Keeping a loyal underperformer "through the difficult phase" | The strongest people watch and conclude that performance does not matter here                                             | Decide with the keeper test; act within a week                                                                 |
| Hiring a "growth" person to fix sales                        | A salary added to a company that has not proved the motion ([[07-customers-markets-and-founder-led-sales\|lesson 07]] §9) | The founder sells until the motion is teachable and affordable                                                 |

Two cautions, because the wartime frame is over-used. First, **wartime is a description of the company's situation, not a personality you adopt.** Horowitz's own later commentary is that the mode is temporary and that a CEO stuck permanently in wartime destroys the organisation. Second, **"wartime" is not a licence for the CEO to bypass governance.** Board resolutions, statutory filings, related-party disclosure and directors' duties under [Section 166 of the Companies Act 2013](https://ca2013.com/166-duties-of-directors/) do not suspend because things are hard — and, as §10.4 explains, the run-up to a failure is precisely when those duties bind hardest.

### 2.3 Where VSYST is today, honestly

VSYST is **not at war, and not at peace.** It is in a third condition the Horowitz frame does not name, which most bootstrapped companies actually live in: **pre-revenue with a controlled burn and an undecided market.** There is no incumbent attacking, no customer defecting, no cash cliff arriving next month — and also no revenue, no proven motion and no external clock. Naming this correctly matters, because both mis-labels are damaging:

- **Calling it peacetime** licenses the company to build features, plan quarters and hold consensus meetings while the only question that matters — will a Raipur dealer pay for this — goes another six months unanswered.
- **Calling it wartime** manufactures urgency without a target, which is how three people who are also a family exhaust themselves and each other over nothing an outsider could point to.

The honest description is: **VSYST is in the phase where the war has not started and its outcome is already being decided.** The specific proof is in §3.4's pre-mortem, where the two highest-scoring causes of death are not competitors or breaches — they are _dealers signing up and never reaching a first invoice_ and _staying pre-revenue because the company keeps building instead of selling_. Neither is dramatic. Both are lethal, slowly, and both are being decided right now by how the next eight weeks are spent.

The trigger that flips VSYST into genuine wartime is written down in §7.4. Until one of those triggers fires, the correct posture is peacetime discipline with wartime honesty about the clock.

## 3. The pre-mortem

### 3.1 Klein's method, and why it works

Gary Klein's premortem is the single best risk technique available to a company that has no risk department. The method, as published in _Harvard Business Review_: the team assumes "that the project they are planning has just failed—as so many do—and then generate plausible reasons" for the failure ([Klein — Performing a Project Premortem, HBR, September 2007](https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem)). Klein developed it as a risk-assessment method whose purpose is simply "to increase plan success rate" ([gary-klein.com — PreMortem](https://www.gary-klein.com/premortem)).

Two mechanisms make it work, and both matter for a three-person family company:

- **Prospective hindsight.** Asking _why did it fail_ rather than _what could go wrong_ recruits a different mental process — you are explaining a fact, not speculating about a possibility, and explanation produces far more specific, concrete causes than speculation does. Klein's framing addresses the planning fallacy directly: people "overestimate benefits and underestimate costs" ([Kahneman, _Thinking, Fast and Slow_, FSG, 2011](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thinking,_Fast_and_Slow)), and a pre-mortem is one of the few interventions that reliably interrupts it.
- **It legitimises doubt.** Klein's stated rationale is that "too many people are reluctant to speak up about their reservations during the all-important planning phase" ([HBR, 2007](https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem)). The pre-mortem does not ask anyone to be disloyal; it asks everyone to be imaginative about a failure that is stipulated. That reframe is exactly [psychological safety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_safety) engineered into a single meeting — Amy Edmondson's construct, "the belief that one will not be punished or humiliated for speaking up with ideas, questions, concerns, or mistakes" (_Administrative Science Quarterly_ 44(2), 1999).

In a family founding team the second mechanism is the whole point. The domain-expert director may not say _"I think your pricing is wrong"_ to the CEO who is also his relative. He will happily write _"we failed because dealers never agreed to pay per GSTIN"_ on a piece of paper when the premise of the exercise is that everyone already failed.

### 3.2 Running it with three founders — the silent-first rule

The most common way to waste a pre-mortem is to run it as a conversation. Whoever speaks first sets the frame; whoever is most senior sets the list; the other two spend the session reacting. This is the exact defect the [Delphi method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delphi_method) was designed around — anonymous independent responses first, precisely to stop "the authority, personality, or reputation of some participants from dominating others in the process." At VSYST the authority problem is compounded, because seniority in the family and seniority in the company are not the same ranking and everyone in the room knows it.

The protocol, 75 minutes, once a quarter:

1. **Set the scene in one sentence, in writing, with a date.** _"It is 1 September 2028. VSYST has shut down DZZLO OMS. The company failed."_ Not "struggled". Failed. The stipulation is what does the work.
2. **Ten minutes of silence. Everyone writes their own list, on paper, alone.** No laptops, no discussion, no reading each other's. Target eight to twelve causes each. Bad handwriting is fine; a shared doc is not — a shared doc is a conversation with a delay.
3. **Read out in rotation, one cause each, round-robin, until everyone is empty.** No commentary, no defending, no "but we've already handled that" during the read-out. Someone types them into the `Risk & Pre-Mortem` tab as they come.
4. **Merge only what is genuinely the same cause.** Resist merging "we ran out of cash" with "we never got dealers to pay" — those have different mitigations and different owners, and merging them is how the actionable one disappears.
5. **Score independently, then compare.** Each founder writes likelihood 1–5 and impact 1–5 for every row **without seeing the others' scores**, then all three are entered side by side. The rows where the three of you disagree by two or more points are the most valuable output of the meeting — larger than the average score itself. A gap means one of you knows something the others do not, or is defending something.
6. **Argue only the gaps.** Twenty minutes, timeboxed. The domain-expert director's likelihood scores on dealer behaviour and OMC policy are better data than the CEO's; the CEO's scores on technical and cash rows are better than his. Say so explicitly, so deference lands where it belongs and nowhere else.
7. **Take the top three by agreed score. Each gets a dated mitigation and one named owner, entered before anyone leaves the room.** This is the rule that converts the exercise from therapy into management. A pre-mortem that ends with a list and no dates has produced a mood, not a plan.

Two supporting rules. **The list is written down and kept**, so next quarter's pre-mortem starts by reading last quarter's — you will find that some causes have quietly become true and nobody noticed. And **new hires run their own pre-mortem in their first month before reading yours**; a person who has been in the company six weeks can see things three founders have stopped seeing.

### 3.3 Scoring: likelihood × impact, and the top-three rule

Use the same crude 1–5 × 1–5 scale as the COO's register ([[T15-risk-register|T15]] carries the scoring guide) so the two documents can be read together. Crudeness is deliberate: the score exists to force a ranking argument, not to simulate a probability. Three operating rules:

- **Score out of 25; act on the top three, monitor the rest.** Three is not arbitrary — it is the number of mitigations a three-person company can actually run alongside its day job. A "top ten priorities" list is a list of zero priorities.
- **Impact is measured against the company's survival, not against a rupee band.** The COO register bands impact in rupees (₹25k, ₹1–5 lakh, ₹5–25 lakh) because operational risk is mostly financial. On the CEO's sheet a 5 means _the company does not recover from this_, and a 3 means _we lose a year_. Different instrument, different scale.
- **A mitigation is a dated action with an owner, or it is an intention.** "Improve onboarding" is an intention. "COO publishes the onboarding SOP and instruments time-to-first-invoice on the dashboard by 30 Nov 2026" is a mitigation. The workbook tab has an owner column and a by-when column for exactly this reason, and a blank in either is treated as a red row.

### 3.5 What makes an early-warning signal real

An early-warning signal that requires someone to remember to worry is not a signal. Test each one against four criteria before it goes in the tab:

| Criterion                                                           | Fails                         | Passes                                                                                                                           |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Observable** — it is a number or an event, not a feeling          | "Dealers seem less engaged"   | "Median days-to-first-invoice > 14"                                                                                              |
| **Already instrumented** — it appears somewhere you look weekly     | "We'd notice if churn rose"   | It is a row on the CEO dashboard ([[17-the-numbers-a-ceo-watches\|lesson 17]]) or the COO scorecard ([[T05-kpi-scorecard\|T05]]) |
| **Leading, not trailing** — it moves before the damage, not with it | "Revenue fell"                | "Two consecutive weeks with fewer than three dealer conversations"                                                               |
| **Has a named watcher and a stated response**                       | "We should keep an eye on it" | "COO checks it every Monday; if it fires, this row goes to the weekly agenda and the mitigation date moves forward"              |

The signals that pass are almost always things you are already measuring for another reason. That is the point: the pre-mortem's job is not to create new telemetry, it is to attach meaning to telemetry you already have, so a number you glance at every Monday starts carrying a warning.

## 4. Existential risk vs operational risk

### 4.1 The boundary, and why it must be explicit

The COO course already owns a complete, twelve-row risk register, an insurance programme and a full-year compliance calendar ([[13-compliance-calendar-risk-and-insurance|COO lesson 13]], [[T15-risk-register|T15]], [[T16-compliance-calendar|T16]]). **This lesson does not repeat any of it and must not contradict it.** If the two documents ever disagree, the COO's is right about mechanism and this one is right about which risks the CEO personally carries.

The dividing test is one question: **can this risk be mitigated by a mechanism, or does it require a judgment nobody can pre-write?**

- If a checklist, a drill, a second signatory, an alert, a policy or an SOP substantially reduces it — it is **operational**, and it belongs to the COO's register. The CEO funds the mitigation and then stops thinking about it.
- If reducing it requires a decision about direction, capital, people or story that only the CEO can make — it is **existential**, and it belongs to the pre-mortem. The COO may execute parts of the mitigation, but the risk itself cannot be delegated.

A useful corollary: **operational risks recur; existential risks arrive once.** You will have many outages and you must survive each of them. You will have one moment when the strategy is wrong, and how you handle it decides the company.

| **A ledger error misstates a dealer's money**            | **Both — different halves**            | COO runs the incident ([[T17-incident-postmortem\|T17]]); **CEO makes the call to the dealer and decides restitution**   | Detection and rollback are mechanisms; whether to refund, disclose and to whom is judgment                             |
| **Data breach**                                          | **Both — different halves**            | COO runs containment and the regulatory clocks; **CEO owns notification, the statement and the commercial consequences** | The 6-hour CERT-In clock is a mechanism; what you tell 40 dealers is not                                               |
| **A co-founder must leave**                              | **CEO** (pre-mortem #5)                | **CEO**, with outside counsel                                                                                            | No mechanism exists. §5.2                                                                                              |
| **A competitor with far more money**                     | **CEO** (pre-mortem #6)                | **CEO**                                                                                                                  | Strategy response, not an operational control ([[06-strategy-ii-moats-positioning-and-the-business-model\|lesson 06]]) |
| **Regulatory/OMC shock invalidates part of the product** | **CEO** (pre-mortem #8 adjacent)       | **CEO**, with the CA/CS watching notifications for the COO's calendar                                                    | Detection is operational; the product and strategy response is not                                                     |
| **CEO burnout / founder unavailable**                    | **Both**                               | COO owns continuity (second person per critical task); **CEO owns actually taking the holiday**                          | §8                                                                                                                     |
| **The strategy is wrong**                                | **CEO**                                | **CEO**                                                                                                                  | Nobody else can say it                                                                                                 |

### 4.3 Two handoff rules

**Rule one — every existential risk with an operational component gets split explicitly, in writing, in both registers.** A breach appears on the COO's register as _"data breach — controls, drills, clocks"_ and on the CEO's pre-mortem as _"a breach destroys dealer trust and ends the company."_ Same event, two different jobs, two owners, and neither of them assuming the other has it. The failure mode this prevents is the one every small company hits: the CEO thinks the COO has "the breach thing" because it is on a register, and the COO thinks the CEO has "the trust thing" because it is not.

**Rule two — incidents flow one way, risks flow the other.** Every [[T17-incident-postmortem|T17]] postmortem asks _"could this recur bigger, and would the bigger version be existential?"_ If yes, a row appears on the CEO's pre-mortem sheet. Conversely, every CEO pre-mortem row asks _"is any part of this reducible by a mechanism?"_ If yes, that part is handed to the COO's register with a date. The two registers are reviewed in the same quarter, in adjacent sessions, so the handoffs happen while both people are in the room.

## 5. The hard things catalogue

Ten situations. Each gets the same four-part treatment: **what it feels like**, **what you must actually do and in what order**, **the mistake almost everyone makes**, and where the mechanics are handed off. These are written for a three-person, pre-revenue, family-founded company in Raipur — not for a hundred-person company with an HR department, because that advice does not survive contact with your reality.

### 5.1 (a) Running out of cash

**What it feels like.** Not a cliff. A slow narrowing, in which every week you find a reason the next month will be better, and every week the reason is slightly weaker than the last. The specific feeling is that you can see the number and are somehow still surprised by it, because you have been reading it as a forecast rather than as a fact.

**What to do, in order.**

1. **Compute default alive or default dead, today.** Paul Graham's question: assuming expenses stay constant and revenue growth is what it has been over recent months, do you reach profitability on the money you have left ([Paul Graham — Default Alive or Default Dead?, October 2015](https://www.paulgraham.com/aord.html))? For VSYST, pre-revenue, the answer is mechanically _default dead_ and the useful version of the question becomes: **at the current burn, what date does the bank balance hit the reserve floor, and what has to be true by then?** [[08-capital-runway-and-fundraising|Lesson 08]] and [[C13-runway-burn-and-scenario-planner|C13]] hold the arithmetic.
2. **Move to a weekly cash number and tell the other two directors the date.** Not the burn rate — _the date_. A date is a fact people act on; a burn rate is a statistic people discuss.
3. **Cut before you raise, not after.** Every cost that is not the shortest path to a paying dealer goes, this week, including things you are proud of. Investors and lenders fund companies that have already made the hard cuts; they do not fund the intention to make them.
4. **Rank every remaining rupee against one question:** does this buy a paying dealer, keep an existing dealer, or keep the company legal? Everything else is a no.
5. **Turn on the price.** The fastest cash lever a pre-revenue company has is charging the people already using the product. Annual prepay at a founding-cohort discount converts a year of future revenue into cash today ([[06-strategy-ii-moats-positioning-and-the-business-model|lesson 06]] §7).
6. **Log the decision and the reasoning in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]].** Cash decisions made under pressure are the ones you will most want an honest record of.

**The mistake almost everyone makes.** Cutting in two rounds. The first cut is sized to what feels bearable, the market does not turn, and three months later you cut again — which costs twice the trust, twice the disruption, and tells everyone left that more cuts are coming. Horowitz's rule is blunt: once you decide, "the time elapsed between making that decision and executing that decision should be as short as possible" ([a16z — The Right Way to Lay People Off, 21 September 2010](https://a16z.com/the-right-way-to-lay-people-off/)). Cut once, deeper than comfortable, and say it is the only one — then make that true.

**Handoff.** The 13-week forecast, the reserve floor and the payments calendar are the COO's ([[T20-budget-vs-actual-and-cash-forecast|T20]], [[06-money-rails-and-finance-operations|COO lesson 06]]). The trigger, the cut list and the conversation are yours.

### 5.2 (b) A co-founder who must leave — especially a family co-founder

**What it feels like.** Months of a quiet, corrosive knowledge you do not say out loud, punctuated by weeks where things seem fine and you decide you were wrong. At a family company there is a second layer: the conversation you are avoiding is with someone you will see at dinner, whose spouse will hear the version you did not intend, and whose father may be your father. The avoidance is not weakness; the cost of the conversation is genuinely enormous. It is still the wrong choice.

**What to do, in order.**

1. **Separate three questions that are usually mashed into one.** (i) Is the _role_ wrong — is this person in a seat that no longer exists or that they cannot fill? (ii) Is the _behaviour_ wrong — trust, reliability, honesty? (iii) Is the _ownership_ wrong? These have different answers, different remedies and different documents. A founder can leave the role and keep the shares. A founder can also be entirely honourable and still be in the wrong seat.
2. **Check what you have already agreed.** The founders' agreement, the shareholders' agreement, vesting and any leaver provisions are the frame ([[03-the-ceo-core-value-and-the-founder-contracts|lesson 03]] §6, [[C02-founders-agreement-and-vesting-checklist|C02]]). If VSYST does not yet have vesting in place, that is itself the most urgent item in this lesson — Noam Wasserman's work on founding teams is a catalogue of exactly this failure, and the reason vesting exists is to make this conversation survivable ([Wasserman — The Founder's Dilemma, HBR, February 2008](https://hbr.org/2008/02/the-founders-dilemma)).
3. **Talk to counsel before you talk to your co-founder** — a corporate lawyer and the CS, on the share and directorship mechanics (resignation of directorship via DIR-11/DIR-12, share transfer, whether a board or shareholder resolution is needed, and what changes on the MCA record). **VERIFY LIVE** with the CS; the forms and timelines change.
4. **Have the conversation once, in person, privately, with the decision already made.** Not a consultation. Say what is happening, why, what the terms are, and what you want the relationship to be afterwards. Then be quiet and let them react for as long as they need.
5. **Separate the shareholder conversation from the employment conversation, on different days.** This is the single most useful mechanical rule in this section, and §9 explains why.
6. **Agree the story you will both tell, in one sentence, before either of you tells anyone.** Then the CEO tells the team, the same day, and does not editorialise.
7. **Do the paperwork within two weeks.** An unresolved cap table is a live grenade in every future fundraise, partnership and audit.

**The mistake almost everyone makes.** Trying to solve it with a _role change_. "Let's move you to advisory" or "you focus on the OMC relationship" delays the conversation by six months, degrades the person's standing in front of the team, and ends in the same place with more damage. If the honest answer is that this person should not be in the company, a smaller job inside the company is not a kinder version of that answer.

**Handoff.** The offboarding mechanics — access revocation, credential rotation, handover of relationships, the exit checklist — are [[T13-offboarding-checklist|T13]]. The equity, the directorship and the family are yours.

### 5.3 (c) Firing someone you like who is loyal

**What it feels like.** Guilt that is out of proportion to your actual culpability, plus a specific and seductive rationalisation: _they are loyal, they have been here since the beginning, and loyalty deserves loyalty._ It does — but the loyalty you owe them is honesty and a decent landing, not a job they cannot do.

**What to do, in order.**

1. **Apply the keeper test honestly.** Netflix's formulation: _"if X wanted to leave, would I fight to keep them?"_ and _"knowing everything I know today, would I hire X again?"_ ([Netflix — Culture](https://jobs.netflix.com/culture)). Netflix's frame — "a professional sports team, not a family" — is a poor cultural fit for a Raipur company where the team may literally be family, but the _test_ is still the right diagnostic, because it strips out tenure and affection and asks only about the seat.
2. **Check you have done your job first.** Was the outcome ever written down? Did they get a clear, specific, unpleasant piece of feedback at least twice, with a date, and time to act on it? If not, the failure is partly yours and the first step is not termination — it is the honest conversation you never had ([[10-building-the-team-hiring-equity-and-firing|lesson 10]]).
3. **Decide, then act inside a week.** The gap between deciding and doing is where dignity leaks. People can always tell.
4. **Do it yourself, in person, in under fifteen minutes, on a day that is not a Friday afternoon.** Say the decision in the first thirty seconds. Do not build up to it; the build-up is for your comfort, not theirs.
5. **Be generous with the exit and specific with the help.** Notice pay or more, a written reference you have actually drafted, and three real introductions. In a Raipur market where everyone knows everyone, how you end things is a public statement about your company.
6. **Tell the team the same day, briefly, without a case for the prosecution.** They do not need the evidence. They need to know it happened, that it was decided fairly, and what changes tomorrow.

**The mistake almost everyone makes.** Kindness that is actually cowardice — softening the message until the person leaves the room not knowing they have been fired, or believing there is an appeal. The second-most-common mistake is explaining too much: every additional reason you give is a reason for them to argue with, and the decision is not up for argument.

**Handoff.** Full-and-final settlement, statutory dues, access revocation and the offboarding sequence sit with the COO ([[T13-offboarding-checklist|T13]], [[09-people-operations|COO lesson 09]]). The conversation is yours and cannot be delegated.

### 5.4 (d) A layoff or a salary cut at a tiny company

**What it feels like.** At five people, a layoff is not a restructuring — it is telling one specific person, whose family situation you know, that the thing you asked them to leave a stable job for has not worked. There is no HR abstraction to hide behind and no "affected population" to reference. That exposure is appropriate. It should be hard.

**What to do, in order.**

1. **Size it once, from the runway, not from the mood.** How many months does this buy, and does that number get you past a milestone that changes the company's situation? If the cut does not buy a milestone, it is a delay, not a plan, and you should be considering §10 instead.
2. **Get the law right before you speak to anyone. VERIFY LIVE with the labour-law advisor.** India's Industrial Relations Code, 2020 subsumed the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 and came into force on 21 November 2025 ([Industrial Relations Code, 2020](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_Relations_Code,_2020)); the Code raised the threshold requiring prior government permission for retrenchment from 100 to 300 workers, so a five-person company is far below it — but the _notice and compensation_ conditions that were Section 25F of the old Act ([Industrial Disputes Act, 1947](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/500379/)) have successors in the new Code, and the applicable state rules for Chhattisgarh may not have been notified in the form you expect. Nothing in this lesson is legal advice. Ask the advisor three specific questions in writing: which employees are "workers" under the Code, what notice and compensation are due, and what the Chhattisgarh Shops & Establishments position is.
3. **A salary cut is a change to a contract and generally requires the employee's consent.** You cannot simply announce a reduction. Where founders take a cut, that is their own decision as directors and should be minuted. **VERIFY LIVE.**
4. **Founders cut first, deepest, and say so.** If the three directors have not already taken the largest reduction in the room, do not have the meeting yet.
5. **Follow Horowitz's sequence.** Do not delay. Be clear that the _company_ failed, not the person — his exact framing: _"the company failed and in order to move forward, we will have to lose some excellent people."_ Managers deliver the news to their own people, never HR or a third party. Address the whole company afterwards, because — quoting Bill Campbell — "the message is for the people who are staying". Be present and visible for the rest of the day ([a16z, 2010](https://a16z.com/the-right-way-to-lay-people-off/)).
6. **Same day, everything.** Decision, individual conversations, all-hands, written communication, settlement dates. A layoff that dribbles across a week poisons the week.
7. **Then tell the survivors the plan.** Not reassurance — a plan, with a number and a date, and what changes about how the company is run. The people who stay are deciding, that afternoon, whether to start looking.

**The mistake almost everyone makes.** Cutting shallow to be kind, then cutting again. See §5.1. The second-most-common at a tiny company is announcing to the group before speaking to individuals — at five people, everyone is a special case and everyone deserves the news alone first.

**Handoff.** Settlement mechanics, statutory dues, PF/ESI where applicable and the paperwork are the COO's ([[09-people-operations|COO lesson 09]]).

### 5.5 (e) Losing your biggest customer

**What it feels like.** Personal, because at VSYST's stage the biggest customer is someone the CEO or the domain-expert director knows by name and probably introduced. There is an immediate temptation to save the account at any price, and a slower, more dangerous temptation to decide the loss says nothing about the product.

**What to do, in order.**

1. **In the first 48 hours, do nothing except find out why — from them, in person.** Not a survey, not an email. Go. Ask what changed, when they first thought about leaving, and what would have had to be different. This is the same switch-interview discipline as [[07-customers-markets-and-founder-led-sales|lesson 07]] §3, run in reverse, and it is the most valuable data the company will get all quarter.
2. **Separate a churn from a signal.** One dealer leaving because his son took over and prefers a different system is a churn. One dealer leaving because reconciliation never worked for his volume is a signal, and it is probably true for the next twenty.
3. **Decide whether to fight for it — once, with a number.** What would it cost to keep them, and would you offer that to the next dealer? If the answer is no, you are not saving a customer, you are creating a precedent and a discount you cannot honour at scale ([[06-strategy-ii-moats-positioning-and-the-business-model|lesson 06]] §7.4).
4. **Fix the concentration, not just the account.** The COO register already flags concentration above ~30% of revenue or pipeline ([[T15-risk-register|T15]] row 3). A pre-revenue company with three tenants is 100% concentrated by definition; the mitigation is pipeline, and it starts the same week.
5. **Tell the other two directors the same day, with the loss reason written down.** Loss reasons that live only in the CEO's head are how a company learns nothing from six identical losses.

**The mistake almost everyone makes.** Building the feature the departing customer asked for. The customer who is leaving has the least incentive to be precise about what would have kept them, and the feature arrives after they are gone, aimed at a market of one ([[C25-product-bets-and-the-no-list|C25]]).

**Handoff.** Renewal mechanics, health scores and the save-play are COO revenue operations ([[11-revenue-operations-and-partnerships|COO lesson 11]]). The relationship and the diagnosis are yours.

### 5.6 (f) Telling a dealer his ledger was wrong

**What it feels like.** A very specific kind of dread, because this is the one failure DZZLO cannot have. A dealer tolerates a slow screen, a missing report, an app that logs him out. He does not tolerate a number that says he is owed ₹4.2 lakh when he is owed ₹4.7 lakh — because that number is the only reason he stopped keeping the parallel register in the drawer. The temptation is not to lie. The temptation is to **fix it quietly and hope the size of it never becomes visible**, which is the same thing with better manners.

**What to do, in order.**

1. **Freeze the blast radius before you make the call.** How many tenants, which months, which direction (over-stated or under-stated), and is it still producing wrong numbers right now? You cannot make a credible call without those four facts, and you should not make it without them — see §6 on the cost of speculating.
2. **Decide the disclosure rule once, and apply it mechanically.** The rule this course recommends, and the one to write into [[C30-pre-mortem-crisis-playbook-and-succession|C30]] before you ever need it: **any error that changes a money figure a dealer could have acted on is disclosed to that dealer, by name, by the CEO, within 24 hours of confirmation — regardless of size, regardless of whether they noticed.** The moment you start sizing errors to decide whether to disclose them, you have created a threshold, and thresholds leak.
3. **Call, do not message.** Phone first, WhatsApp the written summary after so there is a record. In a Raipur district market the phone call is the apology and the message is the evidence; sending only the message reads as hiding behind text.
4. **Lead with the number and the correction, not the explanation.** "Your March closing was ₹4,72,300. Our system showed ₹4,21,900. The difference is ₹50,400 in your favour and it is corrected as of now. Here is what happened and here is what we have changed so it cannot happen again." Cause after correction, always — the dealer's first question is _what is true now_, not _whose fault was it_.
5. **Restitution is a CEO decision and it should be over-generous the first time.** If the error cost him money — an interest charge, a wrong payment to an OMC, a customer credit he extended on a wrong balance — make him whole and add something. This is not the [service recovery paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Service_recovery_paradox), which the evidence says is real but rare and unreliable as a strategy; it is simply cheaper than the alternative, and it is the only version of this you can describe out loud later.
6. **Publish the fix, narrowly.** Tell the affected dealers what changed in the system. Tell the whole tenant base only if the whole tenant base was exposed. Broadcasting an error that touched two tenants to forty is not transparency, it is anxiety distributed at your convenience.
7. **Run the postmortem, blameless, within a week, and read it yourself.** Google's SRE practice is the reference — the document is a record of contributing causes and actions, not of who typed the commit ([Google SRE Book — Postmortem Culture](https://sre.google/sre-book/postmortem-culture/)). At VSYST the CEO wrote much of the code, so "blameless" has an unusual edge: you must be visibly harder on the process than on any person, including yourself, or nobody will ever file an honest one.

**The mistake almost everyone makes.** Correcting the data without telling anyone, on the theory that the corrected number is now right so no harm was done. Two things go wrong. The dealer who _did_ notice — and one always does — now knows the system changes silently, which is worse than knowing it was wrong. And your own team learns that money bugs are handled quietly, which is exactly the culture in which the next one is not reported for three weeks.

**Handoff.** Detection, rollback, the month-rebuild tooling, the incident timeline and the postmortem document are engineering and COO mechanics ([[T17-incident-postmortem|T17]]). The phone call, the restitution and the decision about who else is told are yours and cannot be delegated to a support message.

### 5.7 (g) A breach, and the decision to notify

**What it feels like.** Vertigo. Every other hard thing in this catalogue has a boundary you can draw by lunchtime; a breach has an unknown boundary, a clock, and a lawyer's instinct pulling in the opposite direction from your customer's interest. There is also a very seductive first hour in which it looks like it might be nothing.

**What to do, in order.**

1. **Containment before analysis, always.** Rotate credentials, revoke tokens, close the hole. You will want to understand it first. Understanding it first is how a two-hour incident becomes a two-day one.
2. **Start the statutory clocks immediately, because they are shorter than your instinct.** CERT-In's directions under section 70B of the IT Act, 2000 require specified cyber incidents to be reported **within six hours** of noticing them, and explicitly allow you to report with the information you have and supplement later ([CERT-In Directions, 28 April 2022](https://www.cert-in.org.in/PDF/CERT-In_Directions_70B_28.04.2022.pdf)). Separately, section 8(6) of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 requires a Data Fiduciary, "in the event of a personal data breach… [to] give the Board and each affected Data Principal, intimation of such breach in such form and manner as may be prescribed" ([DPDP Act, 2023, s.8](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/186118625/); [bare Act PDF](https://www.indiacode.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/22037/1/a2023-22.pdf)). The prescribed form and timing sit in the Rules and the enforcement position keeps moving — **VERIFY LIVE** with the lawyer and the CS _before_ an incident, not during one, and write the answer into [[C30-pre-mortem-crisis-playbook-and-succession|C30]] as a filled-in form with phone numbers.
3. **Write one factual internal timeline from minute zero.** Times, actions, who did them. This becomes the CERT-In report, the dealer notification, the postmortem and, if it ever comes to it, the evidence. Reconstructing a timeline three days later from memory and Slack is how honest companies end up saying things that turn out to be wrong.
4. **Decide notification on the customer's interest, not on the legal minimum.** The question is: _could a dealer take a protective action if he knew?_ If yes — change a password, watch a bank account, warn his own customers — he is told, today, whatever the minimum requires. The legal floor is a floor.
5. **One statement, one spokesperson, no speculation about cause.** The kit is [[C24-crisis-comms-and-statement-kit|C24]]; the craft is [[12-communication-the-ceo-as-chief-storyteller|lesson 12]]; the discipline is §6 of this lesson.
6. **Assume it will be asked about for two years.** In the empanelment conversation with an OMC, in the ISO 27001 audit ([[certification-and-standards-roadmap|the roadmap]]), in the first serious enterprise deal. Handled well and documented, it becomes evidence that you are a company that handles things. Handled quietly, it becomes a thing that surfaces at the worst possible moment.

**The mistake almost everyone makes.** Using the word "no evidence" as though it were a finding. _"There is no evidence that any data was accessed"_ on day one usually means _"we do not have logs"_, and everyone technical who reads it knows that. Say what you know, say what you do not know yet, and say when you will next speak.

**Handoff.** Containment, the regulatory clock mechanics, the access review, the vendor register and the tenant-isolation audit are the COO's ([[13-compliance-calendar-risk-and-insurance|COO lesson 13]], [[T15-risk-register|T15]]). What forty dealers are told, and what it costs commercially, is yours.

### 5.8 (h) Killing a bet you personally championed

**What it feels like.** Embarrassment dressed as diligence. You will find yourself asking for one more data point, extending the pilot by a month, redefining the success metric slightly downward. Nobody is lying; the whole team can feel the direction of the drift and nobody will name it, because it is _your_ bet and they are three people who eat dinner with you.

**What to do, in order.**

1. **Read the kill criteria you wrote at the start.** If you did not write any, that is the actual lesson, and you write them for every remaining bet this week. Annie Duke's argument in _Quit_ is that quitting decisions are made badly in the moment and well in advance: you decide, before you begin, which signals would tell you to stop ([Annie Duke — _Quit: The Power of Knowing When to Walk Away_, Portfolio, 2022](https://www.annieduke.com/books/)). A kill criterion is a pre-committed trigger (§7) pointed at your own enthusiasm.
2. **Separate the sunk cost from the forward decision, out loud.** The eleven months and the ₹6 lakh are gone in every branch of the tree. The only question is what the _next_ three months of founder time buy in this option versus the best alternative. [Escalation of commitment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escalation_of_commitment) is not a personality flaw; it is the default, and naming it in the room is most of the defence.
3. **Ask the two counterfactual questions, in writing.** _"Knowing what we know today, would we start this now?"_ and _"If a competitor were doing this, would we be worried?"_ If both answers are no, the decision is already made and you are negotiating with yourself.
4. **Distinguish a pivot from an abandonment, and say which one this is.** Eric Ries's definition is worth being precise about: the moment to pivot is when the experiments stop validating the hypothesis, and a pivot is "a structural course correction to test a new fundamental hypothesis about the product, strategy and engine of growth" ([Eric Ries — The Lean Startup: Principles](http://theleanstartup.com/principles)) — it keeps one leg planted. Abandonment keeps nothing. A pivot announced as a pivot is a strategy; a pivot announced as a continuation is a lie the team will decode in a fortnight.
5. **Kill it in one sentence, publicly, with your name on the original decision.** "I championed the ATG integration. It has not produced a paying dealer in eleven months, we said in March that two by August was the bar, and we are stopping it on Friday." The full stop is the point. Kills that trail off into "we may revisit this" are not kills; they are the same project on a lower burner, still consuming the scarcest thing you own, which is attention.
6. **Redeploy the people the same week, visibly, to something that matters.** The signal a team reads from a kill is not "the CEO was wrong"; it is "does effort here get wasted?" Answer that within seven days.
7. **Log it in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] with the original reasoning attached.** The comparison of what you predicted to what happened is the single cheapest way a founder gets better at judgment, and it only works if the prediction was written down before the outcome.

**The mistake almost everyone makes.** Killing the project and quietly keeping the person on it "to wrap up", which is a six-month tail with no owner and no metric. The second-most-common: killing it at a founders' meeting and never telling the customers who were in the pilot. Two dealers piloting an abandoned module deserve a phone call, not a discovery.

**Handoff.** The roadmap mechanics, the no-list and the bet-sizing frame are [[C25-product-bets-and-the-no-list|C25]] and [[13-product-and-technology-leadership-for-a-technical-ceo|lesson 13]]. The public admission is yours.

### 5.9 (i) The unethical ask

**What it feels like.** Never like a bribe. It arrives as a favour between reasonable people, at the end of a good meeting, in a friendly voice, framed as how things are done: a "facilitation" for a clearance, a backdated invoice so a dealer's quarter looks different, an invoice split so GST is lower, a "consultancy fee" to the relative of somebody who can unlock a large account, a request to change a rate in a past month "just this once, it is only a display issue". The pressure is entirely social, and the cost of refusing is immediate and specific while the cost of agreeing is deferred and vague. That asymmetry is the whole trap.

**What to do, in order.**

1. **Know the law before the meeting, because it is not what most Indian founders assume.** The Prevention of Corruption (Amendment) Act, 2018 made **giving** a bribe a distinct offence under section 8 — the older law focused on the receiver — with a narrow proviso for a person compelled to give, who must report it to a law-enforcement authority within seven days. Section 9 goes further and makes a **commercial organisation** liable where a person associated with it bribes a public servant to obtain or retain business, with a defence of having adequate procedures in place ([LiveLaw — overview of the Prevention of Corruption (Amendment) Act, 2018](https://www.livelaw.in/an-overview-on-the-prevention-of-corruption-amendment-act-16-of-2018-part-ii); [Ropes & Gray — client alert, August 2018](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2018/08/key-amendment-to-indias-anti-corruption-legislation-has-significant-implications-for-multinationals)). **VERIFY LIVE** with the lawyer for anything you would actually rely on. The practical translation for a three-person company: there is no version of this where only the other side is exposed, and "adequate procedures" is a document you either wrote before the ask or did not.
2. **Never decide in the room, and never say yes-shaped words.** "Let me check with my co-founders and come back" is complete, polite and true. Ambiguity in the room is heard as a yes and is very hard to withdraw a week later.
3. **Have a scripted refusal that blames the system, not the person.** _"We are a Pvt Ltd with a statutory auditor and every invoice goes through the CA — I can't raise a document that doesn't match a real supply. What I can do is X."_ This lets a person who half-asked retreat without losing face, which is the outcome you want in a district you intend to sell in for a decade.
4. **Always offer the legitimate version.** Faster onboarding, a founder visit, a longer trial, a discount that appears on the invoice, a written support commitment. The ask is usually a proxy for a real need, and answering the need instead of the ask keeps the relationship.
5. **Write it down the same day.** Date, person, what was asked, what you said. Two lines in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]]. This costs nothing, and if the request ever escalates the contemporaneous note is the difference between a position and a claim.
6. **Tell the other two directors, every time, without exception.** The moment one founder is carrying an ask alone, the company has a second set of books in one person's head. In a family firm this rule protects the person who was asked more than it protects the company.
7. **Accept that you will lose deals, and price that in advance.** Decide, in the calm, what the company will not do to win an account — that is a values decision ([[11-culture-and-values-as-a-ceo-instrument|lesson 11]] §4) — and expect it to cost a real, nameable customer at some point. A value that has never cost you anything has not been tested.

**The mistake almost everyone makes.** Treating each ask as a one-off judgment call. It is not: it is a policy question that you are answering by precedent whether you notice or not. The second-most-common mistake is delegating the refusal to a junior person, which puts the least-protected member of the company in front of the most social pressure.

**Handoff.** The anti-bribery clause in the vendor and partner MoUs, the code of conduct and the gift policy are documents the COO owns ([[T27-policy-handbook-toc|T27]], [[C26-partnership-evaluation-and-mou-checklist|C26]]). Saying no to a person who can make or break a large account is yours.

### 5.10 (j) Turning down money that would break the company

**What it feels like.** Worst of all, because it does not look like a hard thing — it looks like good news, and everyone around you will treat it as good news. A ₹40 lakh contract from a large fleet operator that wants a private deployment and its own roadmap. An OMC-adjacent partner who will introduce two hundred dealers in exchange for exclusivity in three states. An investor with a cheque and a term sheet that quietly gives him a veto. Refusing feels like arrogance from a pre-revenue company in Raipur, and it will be described that way at your own dinner table.

**What to do, in order.**

1. **Convert the offer into its true cost in founder-quarters, not rupees.** One custom deployment is not ₹40 lakh of revenue; it is a fork, a second release train, a support obligation and roughly two of the three founders' attention for a year. Price it that way and most of these decisions answer themselves.
2. **Apply one test: does this make the next hundred customers easier or harder?** Work that is reusable across tenants is revenue. Work that exists for one tenant is a services business wearing a product's clothes — and a services business is a legitimate choice, but it is a _different company_ with different economics, and it must be chosen deliberately, not drifted into ([[06-strategy-ii-moats-positioning-and-the-business-model|lesson 06]]).
3. **Read exclusivity and control terms as strategy, not legalese.** Exclusivity in three states is a decision about where to play made by someone who is not you ([[05-strategy-i-diagnosis-and-the-strategy-kernel|lesson 05]]). An investor veto over hiring, pricing or a future raise is a decision right transferred out of the founders' hands ([[08-capital-runway-and-fundraising|lesson 08]], [[C16-term-sheet-decoder-and-cap-table|C16]]). Both are one-way doors and belong in the one-way-door log before, not after.
4. **Ask what the cash is genuinely for.** If it buys twelve months of runway to prove the district motion, it may be worth an ugly term. If it buys twelve months of building someone else's product, the runway was not the constraint.
5. **Say no cleanly and keep the relationship.** "We are not able to do a private deployment this year — here is what we can do, and here is the point at which we could revisit it." A specific, dated no is respected; a vague maybe wastes a year of both companies' time and ends the relationship worse.
6. **Take it to the other two directors as a decision, not as news.** In a family company, an offer that has already been discussed enthusiastically at home is very hard to decline in a board meeting. Bring it to the table before it has been celebrated.

**The mistake almost everyone makes.** Saying yes "just this once, because we need the cash", and discovering that the once-off is now in the codebase, in the support queue, and in the customer's expectations forever. Pre-revenue companies do not accidentally become services businesses; they do it one reasonable exception at a time.

**Handoff.** Partnership scoring, MoU terms and the commercial diligence sit in [[C26-partnership-evaluation-and-mou-checklist|C26]] and [[14-partnerships-ecosystem-and-founder-relationships|lesson 14]]. Term-sheet mechanics are [[C16-term-sheet-decoder-and-cap-table|C16]]. The judgment about what kind of company this is, is yours alone.

### 5.11 The shape all ten share

Read the ten together and the same skeleton shows through. It is worth memorising, because in the moment you will not remember which subsection you are in.

| Step                                                      | What it means                                                                                                    | The failure it prevents                                                |
| --------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **1. Get the facts to a boundary**                        | Size it, scope it, date it — enough to speak accurately, not enough to delay                                     | Speaking before you know; analysing instead of deciding                |
| **2. Check what was already agreed**                      | Contracts, vesting, kill criteria, disclosure rule, values, the trigger you pre-committed                        | Re-deciding a principle under emotional pressure                       |
| **3. Decide, and write down why**                         | One decision, logged in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log\|C29]] with the reasoning and the prediction | A decision that cannot be explained in a year, or learned from         |
| **4. Tell the affected person first, directly, yourself** | In person or by phone; the CEO, not a message, not a deputy                                                      | The person hearing it second-hand; the message being read as cowardice |
| **5. Tell everyone else the same day**                    | One version, one sentence, no case for the prosecution                                                           | Three different stories circulating by the weekend                     |
| **6. Execute fully and fast**                             | Paperwork inside two weeks; no instalments; no quiet tail                                                        | The second cut; the zombie project; the unresolved cap table           |
| **7. Carry the cost visibly and do not re-open it**       | You bear the discomfort in public; the decision stays decided                                                    | Reversal under pressure, which costs more than the original decision   |

Two closing observations about the whole catalogue. First, **in nine of the ten, the damage is done by delay rather than by the decision.** The CEOs who are remembered badly are almost never remembered for a wrong call made cleanly; they are remembered for the six months in which everyone knew and nobody said. Second, **every one of these is easier if the principle was written in the calm.** Which is what §7 is about, and why it is the most important section in this lesson.

## 6. Crisis mechanics: the first hour, the first day, the first week

A crisis is not the same animal as a hard thing. A hard thing has no correct answer and all the time in the world; a crisis usually has a knowable answer and no time at all. That is good news, because it means a crisis **can** be pre-written — and a company of three that has written it down will outperform a company of thirty that has not.

### 6.1 What counts as a crisis

Not everything urgent is a crisis, and treating every urgent thing as one is how a small team learns to ignore alarms. The test has three parts, and a crisis needs at least two:

| Test                                            | Crisis                                                                                              | Merely urgent                                       |
| ----------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| **Is trust at stake, not just uptime?**         | A dealer's money is wrong; data was exposed; a dispatch OTP let the wrong person lift stock         | The reports page is slow; a screen has a layout bug |
| **Is a clock running that you do not control?** | The 22:00–06:00 rate window; a statutory reporting deadline; a WhatsApp group already discussing it | It can be fixed in the next release                 |
| **Will people outside the company find out?**   | Dealers, their customers, an OMC contact, an app-store reviewer                                     | Only the team knows and only the team is affected   |

Write the list of the four or five events that qualify at VSYST into [[C30-pre-mortem-crisis-playbook-and-succession|C30]]. Naming them in advance does two things: it stops a Tuesday-afternoon bug from consuming a founder's week, and — far more important — it stops the real one being under-called for six hours because nobody wanted to be dramatic.

### 6.2 Who runs it: the CEO decides, the COO commands

The most useful thing the incident-response literature offers a three-person company is a separation of roles that most small teams collapse into one exhausted person. Google's SRE practice, which is descended from the fire-service Incident Command System, splits an incident into **Incident Command** (steers the response, holds situational awareness, delegates), **Operational work** (the only people touching the systems), **Communication** (the public face and the keeper of the incident document) and **Planning** (the longer tail — bugs, handoffs, deviations). Its stated rationale is precisely the one that matters here: "a clear separation of responsibilities allows individuals more autonomy than they might otherwise have, since they need not second-guess their colleagues," and interested parties "need to understand where they can interact with the incident commander" ([Google SRE Book — Managing Incidents](https://sre.google/sre-book/managing-incidents/)).

At VSYST, with three founders and no staff, the mapping is:

| Role                   | Who                                          | What they own                                                                               | What they must not do                                                                               |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Incident Commander** | **COO** (or the founder not fixing the code) | The clock, the timeline document, who is doing what, the decision to escalate or stand down | Touch the code. A commander with their hands in the system stops commanding within ten minutes      |
| **Operations**         | **CEO/CTO** and any engineer                 | Diagnosis and the fix. Nothing else                                                         | Talk to customers mid-fix. Every context switch costs the fix twenty minutes                        |
| **Communications**     | **CEO**                                      | What is said, to whom, when, in what words; the call to the affected dealer; the statement  | Improvise. The words come from [[C24-crisis-comms-and-statement-kit\|C24]] with the facts filled in |
| **Customer contact**   | **Domain-expert director**                   | Being reachable on the phone for the dealers who call; capturing what they say              | Explain cause. He relays status, he does not diagnose                                               |

Two rules make this work, and both are counter-intuitive for a technical founder-CEO:

- **The CEO does not run the incident.** The CEO decides and communicates. The moment the CEO is both debugging and deciding, the company has no one watching the clock, the customers or the second-order consequences — and the CEO is the worst-placed person to notice this happening.
- **If the CEO is the only one who can fix it — which at VSYST today is often true — then the COO is the commander and the CEO is _only_ Operations for the duration.** Someone else holds the phone. Write this down now, while it is theoretical, because at 11 PM on a rate-window failure nobody will negotiate it.

### 6.3 The single source of truth

One document. Not a WhatsApp thread, not three people's memories, not the ticket. A single, timestamped, append-only incident document that the commander owns and everyone reads before asking a question. Google's version is a "living incident document" that captures status and key decisions as they happen and becomes the raw material for the postmortem ([Google SRE Book](https://sre.google/sre-book/managing-incidents/)); the same idea in the fire service is the incident action plan.

The minimum shape, which lives as a template in [[C30-pre-mortem-crisis-playbook-and-succession|C30]]:

```
INCIDENT <date>-<n>   Severity: <1/2/3>   Commander: <name>   Status: ACTIVE / MITIGATED / CLOSED
One-line description (facts only, no cause)
Blast radius: tenants affected / months affected / money affected / still-live? Y/N
Timeline (append only, IST, never edit a past line)
  22:41  Alert: rate confirmation failing for 3 tenants
  22:44  <name> assumes command
  22:52  Cause hypothesis A discarded — see note
  23:10  Mitigation applied; monitoring
Decisions taken (and by whom)
External comms sent (time, channel, audience, exact text)
Open questions
Next update due at: <time>       <- this line is never blank while status is ACTIVE
```

Three rules that carry all the weight. **Append, never edit** — a corrected timeline is worthless as evidence and as memory. **The "next update due" line is never blank while the incident is open** — it is the mechanism that prevents §6.6's first classic error. And **facts and hypotheses live in different sections**, visibly labelled, because the single most common way a company ends up publicly wrong is that someone read a hypothesis off the shared document as though it were a finding.

### 6.4 The first hour

Sixty minutes, in this order. The order matters more than the speed.

| Minute    | Action                                                                                                                                                               | Why it is here and not later                                                                                |
| --------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **0–5**   | Declare. Say the word "incident" out loud, name the commander, open the document                                                                                     | Under-calling is the default failure. Declaring is cheap and stand-down is free                             |
| **5–15**  | **Stop the bleeding.** Disable the failing path, roll back, revoke, put the feature in read-only. Do not diagnose yet                                                | A shorter incident is worth more than a better-understood one. Diagnosis has no deadline; damage does       |
| **15–25** | **Establish the blast radius.** How many tenants, which data, is it still happening, is money affected                                                               | Everything you will say publicly depends on these four numbers. Without them you can only speculate         |
| **25–40** | **First external message — even with nothing solved.** Acknowledge, state impact in the customer's terms, state what you are doing, give the time of the next update | This is the whole game. The message that costs you nothing to send is the one that buys the next four hours |
| **40–55** | Diagnosis proper, by Operations only, while the commander holds everything else off them                                                                             | Protected focus is the commander's main product                                                             |
| **55–60** | Commander updates the document, confirms the next-update time, checks whether any statutory clock has started (CERT-In six hours; DPDP intimation)                   | The six-hour clock starts when you _notice_, not when you finish investigating                              |

The first external message is worth writing out now, in [[C24-crisis-comms-and-statement-kit|C24]], with blanks. It has four sentences and no fifth:

> "We are aware of an issue affecting <what the customer sees> since approximately <time>. <What is and is not affected, in their terms>. Our team is working on it now. We will update you by <specific time> whether or not it is resolved."

No apology beyond one plain "we are sorry about this", no cause, no estimate of when it will be fixed, no reassurance you cannot back. **A named next-update time is the single highest-value element in the message** — it converts an anxious customer into a waiting one.

### 6.5 The first day and the first week

| Window            | The CEO's job                                                                                                                                                      | The COO's job                                                                                | The trap                                                                       |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Hours 1–4**     | Comms only: the update cadence, the calls to the dealers who matter most, the decision on how far to disclose                                                      | Command: timeline, roles, escalation, statutory clocks                                       | The CEO drifts back into the code and comms stop                               |
| **Hours 4–12**    | Decide restitution in principle, even before the number is known. Brief the third director on one page                                                             | Rotate people; nobody works an incident more than ~8 hours. Confirm mitigation actually held | Heroics. A tired engineer at hour 11 causes the second incident                |
| **Day 1 close**   | One written summary to all affected dealers: what happened, current status, what happens next, when you will write again                                           | Close the document for the day with a next-update time even if status is MITIGATED           | Declaring victory. "Resolved" said once and wrongly costs more than the outage |
| **Days 2–3**      | The individual calls — the biggest dealer, the one who complained loudest, the one who said nothing. Especially the one who said nothing                           | Root-cause work; the fix that makes recurrence impossible, not merely unlikely               | Confusing "we fixed the symptom" with "we fixed the cause"                     |
| **Days 3–7**      | The blameless postmortem, in the room, with your own contribution named first ([Google SRE — Postmortem Culture](https://sre.google/sre-book/postmortem-culture/)) | The postmortem document, action items with owners and dates, the register update             | A postmortem with actions and no dates, which is a diary entry                 |
| **End of week 1** | Ask the §4.3 question: _could this recur bigger, and would the bigger version be existential?_ If yes, a row is added to the pre-mortem sheet                      | Add the mechanism half to [[T15-risk-register\|T15]]                                         | The incident closes and the company learns nothing structural                  |
| **Week 2**        | Tell the dealers what changed. One paragraph. This is the message that converts an incident into evidence of competence                                            | Verify the actions actually shipped                                                          | Silence after the fix, which erases the credit you earned                      |

The rhythm underneath all of it is a **cadence you announce and then meet**. Hourly while active, at end of day while mitigated, then once when closed and once again when the structural fix ships. A cadence met three times is worth more to a dealer's trust than a fast fix, because a fast fix proves your systems are good and a met cadence proves _you_ are.

### 6.6 The two classic errors

**Error one: going silent.** It is the more common of the two and it feels responsible from the inside — _we will tell them when we know something_. The customer does not experience silence as diligence; they experience it as either incompetence or concealment, and they fill the gap themselves, usually in a WhatsApp group with four other dealers. By the time you speak, you are no longer describing the incident, you are contradicting a version of it that has already set.

The mechanism against it is not resolve, it is the "next update due" line in §6.3. If the time arrives and nothing has changed, you send: _"No change yet. Still working. Next update at <time>."_ That message feels worthless to write and is the most valuable one you will send all night.

**Error two: speculating about cause.** This one is more expensive and it is the specific temptation of a technical CEO, because you genuinely do have a hypothesis at minute twelve and it is genuinely usually right. Usually is the problem. Say "a database issue" and be wrong, and you have handed everyone a reason to disbelieve the true explanation when it arrives. Say "a third-party outage" and be wrong, and you have publicly blamed a vendor you will need next quarter. Say "no data was accessed" before the log review, and you may have to withdraw the only sentence that mattered.

The rule is one line, and it belongs on the wall of the incident document: **describe impact publicly; describe cause only after the postmortem.** Impact you can observe. Cause you can only currently believe. W. Timothy Coombs's Situational Crisis Communication Theory — the empirical basis of most modern crisis practice — turns on the _attributions of responsibility_ stakeholders form early, and its base recommendation is that "informing and adjusting information" comes first, before any argument about blame ([Situational Crisis Communication Theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Situational_crisis_communication_theory); [Coombs & Holladay, _Management Communication Quarterly_ 16(2), 2002](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/089331802237233)). An early wrong cause poisons exactly the thing that is hardest to repair.

Two smaller errors worth naming: **over-apologising in the first message** (it invites the reader to size the damage before you have), and **letting a second person speak** (a well-meaning WhatsApp reply from another founder becomes the company's position). One voice, from [[C24-crisis-comms-and-statement-kit|C24]], with the craft from [[12-communication-the-ceo-as-chief-storyteller|lesson 12]].

## 7. Scenario planning and the pre-committed trigger

This is the most important section in the lesson, and it is the cheapest thing in it. A three-person company cannot buy resilience — no redundancy, no reserves, no risk function. What it can buy, for the price of one afternoon a quarter, is **decisions made in advance by a calmer version of itself.**

### 7.1 Why the calm decides better

Three well-evidenced reasons, none of them about willpower.

- **The hot–cold empathy gap.** People in a calm ("cold") state systematically fail to predict how their preferences will change once they are stressed, frightened or under time pressure — and people in a hot state cannot easily recover the reasoning of their calm selves ([hot–cold empathy gap; Loewenstein, _Health Psychology_, 2005](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empathy_gap)). The practical consequence: the _decision quality_ you get at 11 PM during an incident is not merely slower than your daytime judgment, it is systematically different. You will over-weight what is vivid and under-weight what is absent.
- **Implementation intentions beat intentions.** Peter Gollwitzer's finding is that a goal stated as "if situation X arises, I will do Y" produces far more reliable action than the same goal stated as a resolution, because the cue does the remembering for you ([Gollwitzer, "Implementation intentions: Strong effects of simple plans", _American Psychologist_ 54, 1999](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implementation_intention); meta-analysed with Sheeran, _Advances in Experimental Social Psychology_ 38, 2006). "We will cut costs if things get tight" is an intention. "If bank balance < ₹X on the 1st of any month, we execute cut list B that week" is an implementation intention, and it is roughly a different species.
- **It is a Ulysses pact.** Odysseus did not resist the Sirens; he arranged, in advance, to be unable to act on what he would want later — "a freely made decision that is designed and intended to bind oneself in the future" ([Ulysses pact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulysses_pact)). Every trigger in this section is that. You are not trying to be braver in the moment. You are removing the moment's vote.

There is a fourth reason specific to a family founding team, and it may be the strongest. **A pre-committed trigger depersonalises the hard conversation.** "We agreed in March that if the OMC pursuit had no written next step by September we would stop" is a rule speaking. "I think you should stop chasing IOCL" is a nephew speaking to an uncle. The first is survivable at dinner; the second is not.

### 7.2 The anatomy of a trigger

A trigger is not a worry with a number attached. It has six parts, and one missing part makes it decorative:

| Part                                                     | Bad               | Good                                                                                                 |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Signal** — a thing you already measure                 | "Sales feel slow" | Paying GSTINs added in the last 60 days                                                              |
| **Threshold** — a number and a direction                 | "Too few"         | Fewer than 2                                                                                         |
| **Observation point** — where and when it is read        | "We'd notice"     | CEO dashboard, first Monday of the month                                                             |
| **Pre-decided action** — the exact thing that happens    | "Reassess"        | Founder-led sales becomes the CEO's only priority; all feature work except billing stops for 6 weeks |
| **Owner** — one name, not a committee                    | "The founders"    | CEO                                                                                                  |
| **Review date** — when the trigger itself is re-examined | never             | Next quarterly pre-mortem                                                                            |

Two design rules. **A trigger must be embarrassing to ignore** — visible on a shared dashboard, read aloud at a meeting, with a named owner — because the only real failure mode of this technique is a trigger that fires and is quietly re-interpreted. And **the action must be specific enough to start on the day it fires.** "Cut costs" is a discussion; "cut list B" is a list you already wrote, with line items and rupee amounts, sitting in [[C13-runway-burn-and-scenario-planner|C13]].

### 7.3 Extending the runway ladder to non-cash risks

[[08-capital-runway-and-fundraising|Lesson 08]] establishes the cash ladder: a set of runway thresholds, each with a pre-committed action, so that the company never discovers its position and decides its response on the same day. The move this lesson makes is to notice that **the ladder is a general instrument, not a cash instrument.** Anything with a leading signal can carry one.

| The cash ladder (lesson 08)                | The same shape applied elsewhere                                                                |
| ------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Runway thresholds in months                | Activation, conversation count, pursuit age, holiday count, disagreement count                  |
| Each rung has a pre-written action         | Each rung has a pre-written action                                                              |
| The action escalates as the number worsens | Same                                                                                            |
| Reviewed monthly against the bank balance  | Reviewed monthly on the CEO dashboard ([[17-the-numbers-a-ceo-watches\|lesson 17]])             |
| Prevents the _"one more month"_ drift      | Prevents the _"he'll come good"_, _"the deal is close"_, _"I'll rest after the release"_ drifts |

### 7.5 Scenarios, briefly, and what a three-person company should actually do

Scenario planning proper was built at Royal Dutch/Shell, and Pierre Wack's account of it remains the clearest ([Wack — "Scenarios: Uncharted Waters Ahead", _Harvard Business Review_, September 1985](https://hbr.org/1985/09/scenarios-uncharted-waters-ahead)). The core distinction is worth importing even if the apparatus is not: **a forecast says what will happen; a scenario describes a world in which a different thing is true, so that you can notice which world you are in.** Shell's value from it was not prediction — it was that its managers had already thought inside a world of high oil prices, so when that world arrived they recognised it weeks before their competitors did.

For a company of three, the honest translation is three scenarios, one page, once a year, at the annual plan ([[19-planning-okrs-and-capital-allocation|lesson 19]], [[C28-annual-plan-and-ceo-okr-sheet|C28]]):

| Scenario at +12 months  | What is true in this world                                                                | The signal that tells you you are in it                                           | What you do differently, starting now                                                                                                                                        |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **A. It works**         | 25–40 paying GSTINs, activation under 10 days, one district referenceable                 | Rows 4 and 5 of the ladder green for two quarters                                 | Hire the first support/ops person; write the district playbook so a non-founder can run it                                                                                   |
| **B. It grinds**        | 8–12 paying GSTINs, every deal still needs the CEO, cash tightening                       | Deals close but CAC does not fall; the CEO is in every one                        | Narrow the ICP hard ([[C09-icp-and-customer-discovery-guide\|C09]]); raise price on the segment that does convert; extend runway before you need to                          |
| **C. The ground moves** | An OMC or incumbent bundles an adequate tool; or a regulation changes the invoicing model | An OMC circular; a dealer mentioning a bundled tool; a GST/e-invoice notification | Move to the layer they will not build — reconciliation, the dealer's own customers, the audit trail ([[06-strategy-ii-moats-positioning-and-the-business-model\|lesson 06]]) |

Three rules keep this from becoming an away-day artefact. **Do not assign probabilities** — the point is recognition, not weighting. **Name the signal that distinguishes the worlds**, or you have written fiction. And **each scenario must change something you would do this month**, or it is not a scenario, it is a mood.

### 7.6 The review discipline

Triggers decay. Numbers that were right in October are wrong in March, and a trigger nobody has re-read is a trigger nobody will honour. Three habits:

- **Read the ladder aloud at every quarterly pre-mortem**, before anything else. Which rows fired? What did we actually do? Which thresholds are now wrong?
- **A fired-and-ignored trigger is a red row and gets a written explanation.** Not a punishment — a record. The pattern of ignored triggers is the most honest description of what the company actually believes.
- **Every trigger has an expiry.** If it has not fired in a year and would not fire in any plausible world, delete it. A ladder with twenty rungs is a ladder nobody climbs.

## 8. The psychological load

This section is here because leaving it out would be dishonest, and because the failure mode it describes — a founder-CEO who breaks down slowly and does not notice — is row 9 on VSYST's pre-mortem with a score of 15. Treat it as an operating risk with a mitigation, not as a soft topic at the end of a lesson.

### 8.1 What the research actually says, and what it does not

Founder mental-health statistics are among the most distorted numbers in circulation. The honest version is narrower and still serious.

The best-known study is Michael Freeman and colleagues' survey published as "The prevalence and co-occurrence of psychiatric conditions among entrepreneurs and their families" ([_Small Business Economics_ 53(2), 2019, pp. 323–342](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11187-018-0059-8); [record at Icahn School of Medicine](https://scholars.mssm.edu/en/publications/the-prevalence-and-co-occurrence-of-psychiatric-conditions-among--2/)). What it reports: among **242 entrepreneurs and 93 comparison participants**, 49% of the entrepreneurs reported a personal mental-health history; taken together with family history among the asymptomatic, mental-health differences "directly or indirectly affected" 72% of the entrepreneur sample. Entrepreneurs reported more depression (30%), ADHD (29%), substance use (12%) and bipolar disorder (11%) than the comparison group; 32% reported two or more conditions.

Now the caveats, which matter as much as the numbers:

| Caveat                                                                       | Why it changes how you should use the figure                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Self-report, not clinical diagnosis**                                      | These are people describing themselves on a survey, not people assessed by a clinician                                                                                                                                       |
| **Small, self-selected samples**                                             | 242 and 93 are small, and people with an interest in the topic are likelier to respond                                                                                                                                       |
| **Cross-sectional**                                                          | It cannot tell you whether founding a company causes distress or whether people prone to certain traits are drawn to founding. Freeman's own frame leans toward the second                                                   |
| **Not a general-population comparison**                                      | The comparison group was a convenience sample, not a representative population, so "entrepreneurs are N× more likely than the public" claims do not follow from this study                                                   |
| **The widely-quoted multipliers come from an earlier unpublished pre-print** | "Founders are 2× more likely to be depressed, 10× more likely to be bipolar" circulates constantly and is not a finding you should repeat as established. Use the prevalence figures above, with the caveats, or use nothing |

A second, more recent and more directly relevant data point is Startup Snapshot's 2023 founder survey, which is a commissioned industry survey rather than peer-reviewed research and should be read as such: **72% of founders reported the entrepreneurial journey had affected their mental health; 37% reported anxiety and 36% burnout; 81% said they do not openly share their stress, fears and challenges; only 10% talk to their investors about it; only 23% had seen a psychologist or coach; and 50% reported a negative stigma around seeking professional support** ([Brad Feld — The Impact of Stress on the Well-being of Startup Founders and CEOs, May 2023](https://feld.com/archives/2023/05/the-impact-of-stress-on-the-well-being-of-startup-founders-and-ceos/), reporting the [Startup Snapshot](https://www.startupsnapshot.com/) study).

The honest summary is not "founders are mentally ill". It is this: **a large majority of founders report that the work affects them, a large majority do not tell anyone, and only about a quarter get professional help.** The gap between the first number and the third is the actionable part, and it is entirely within your control.

### 8.2 The four specific loads

**The loneliness of the seat.** It is structural, not emotional. There are things you cannot say to the team (we may not make payroll in March), things you cannot say to customers (the module you rely on has one maintainer), things you cannot say to investors if you have them, and — at VSYST — things you cannot say to your co-founders because they are also your family and the words cannot be unsaid at dinner. Thomas Saporito's point in _Harvard Business Review_ is simply that this is a real occupational feature of the job and that pretending otherwise is what makes it dangerous ([Saporito — "It's Time to Acknowledge CEO Loneliness", HBR, 15 February 2012](https://hbr.org/2012/02/its-time-to-acknowledge-ceo-lo)). Notice the mechanism: it is not that nobody cares. It is that **every relationship you have has a reason you should not tell them everything** — and the sum of many partial audiences is no audience.

**The Struggle.** Ben Horowitz's essay is the most accurate description of the interior of a bad quarter that anyone has written, and it is worth reading in full rather than quoted: "The Struggle is when you wonder why you started the company in the first place… The Struggle is where self-doubt becomes self-hatred… The Struggle is not failure, but it causes failure. Especially if you are weak" ([a16z — The Struggle, 15 June 2012](https://a16z.com/the-struggle/)). His advice is deliberately unheroic — do not carry it all yourself, do not take it personally, focus on the road and not the wall, play long enough to get lucky. The single most useful line for a founder in it is the first one in his list: _don't put it all on your shoulders._ It is advice about **distribution**, not about endurance.

**Imposter feelings after a loss.** After a lost deal, a bad month or a public mistake, a specific thought arrives: _they are going to find out I don't know what I'm doing._ Clance and Imes named this in 1978 as "an internal experience of intellectual phoniness" ([Clance & Imes, _Psychotherapy: Theory, Research & Practice_ 15(3), 1978](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impostor_syndrome)) — and it is worth knowing that it is **not a clinical disorder**, it is an experience, extremely common among competent people, and it spikes after visible failure rather than tracking actual competence. The practical defence is evidentiary: the decision journal ([[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]]) exists partly for this. A record of what you predicted and what happened is the only thing that reliably argues with the feeling, because the feeling is immune to reassurance.

**Identity fusion.** The specific founder condition, and the least discussed. Identity fusion describes "a visceral sense of oneness" with a group such that personal and collective identity stop being separable — the construct developed by William Swann and colleagues, which predicts extraordinary willingness to sacrifice for the group ([identity fusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_fusion)). For a founder whose company carries the family name in a city where everyone knows the family, fusion is close to total: a bad quarter is not a bad quarter, it is _evidence about you_, and a wind-down is not a business outcome, it is a public verdict on your worth. Fusion is also why founders are capable of the effort the job requires. It is not a defect to be eliminated. It is a load-bearing wall that needs a second wall next to it.

The counter-mechanism is not detachment; it is **a second identity that has its own evidence.** Something you are demonstrably good at that has nothing to do with DZZLO, some role in which you are not the CEO, and at least one relationship in which the company is not the topic. Founders who have one of these survive a bad year differently from founders who do not, and the time to build it is now, when it feels unnecessary.

Ranked by evidence and by how cheap it is at VSYST's stage. None of these is a nice-to-have; each is a mitigation on row 9 of the pre-mortem.

**Say this part plainly: professional help is a normal tool, not a last resort.** You would not run DZZLO's production database without a DBA's opinion when something looked wrong, and you would not regard calling one as an admission of failure. A therapist is the same category of decision. The Startup Snapshot numbers above say that half of founders perceive stigma around it and only about a quarter go — which means the stigma, not the need, is what is doing the filtering.

### 8.4 The India-specific part

Three things are different here and they are worth naming rather than importing an American paragraph.

- **The treatment gap is enormous, and it is a supply problem as much as a stigma problem.** India's National Mental Health Survey 2015–16 found a current prevalence of mental disorders of roughly 10.6% among adults and an overall treatment gap around 85% — that is, fewer than one in six people with a diagnosable condition received care, with the gap exceeding 85% for common conditions like depression and anxiety ([Gautham et al., _International Journal of Social Psychiatry_, 2020, reporting the NMHS 2015–16](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0020764020907941); [NMHS summary, NIMHANS](https://indianmhs.nimhans.ac.in/phase1/Docs/Summary.pdf)). If you go looking for help and find it hard to find, that is the system, not you.
- **There is a free, government-run, confidential option and most founders do not know it exists.** Tele-MANAS — the National Tele Mental Health Programme, launched 10 October 2022 by the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare — is a 24×7 toll-free helpline on **14416** (or 1-800-891-4416), staffed by trained counsellors with escalation to psychiatrists, available in around 20 Indian languages ([Tele MANAS, MoHFW](https://telemanas.mohfw.gov.in/home); [PIB — Tele-MANAS milestone release](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2022057)). **VERIFY LIVE** — numbers and coverage change. Put it in the phone of all three founders today. It costs nothing and it removes the "I wouldn't know where to start" excuse permanently.
- **In a city like Raipur, confidentiality is a real constraint, not a paranoid one.** The therapist two streets away may share a social circle with a dealer. This is a genuine argument for tele-consultation with a practitioner in another city, which is now ordinary and inexpensive. It is not an argument for not going.

### 8.5 The warning signs, written down while you are well

The purpose of writing these now is that in the state they describe, you will not recognise them. Give this list to the person named in §8.3 row 1 and to your co-founders, and give them explicit permission — in writing, in the family ground rules of §9 — to raise it.

| Signal                   | Threshold that means act, not observe                                                                                                                     |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Sleep                    | Under 6 hours for two weeks, or waking at 3–4 AM regularly with the same thought                                                                          |
| Withdrawal               | Avoiding calls you would normally take; three cancelled personal commitments in a month                                                                   |
| Decision paralysis       | Decisions you would have made in a day sitting for three weeks; queueing at the CEO ([[16-decisions-delegation-and-not-being-the-bottleneck\|lesson 16]]) |
| Irritability             | Two sharp exchanges with a co-founder in a fortnight that you would not have had a year ago                                                               |
| Anhedonia                | A genuinely good week — a signed dealer, a shipped release — producing nothing                                                                            |
| Alcohol or substance use | Any increase you have privately noticed and not mentioned                                                                                                 |
| Intrusive catastrophe    | Rehearsing the shutdown conversation involuntarily, in bed, more than occasionally                                                                        |
| Any thought of self-harm | **Immediate.** Call 14416, or a doctor, today. There is no threshold on this row                                                                          |

None of these is a character verdict. They are the same class of object as a rising error rate on a dashboard: a number that means _investigate now_, and that gets less expensive to act on the earlier you do.

## 9. The family dimension of a crisis

Every hard thing in §5 is harder at VSYST than the literature assumes, for one reason that no American startup book will tell you: **the person on the other side of the conversation is at dinner tonight.** Not metaphorically. Tonight, at the same table, possibly with the person whose opinion of you both matters more than either of yours.

This section is the missing chapter. It is not about being nice to your family. It is about the specific mechanics that let a family-founded company have the conversations it must have without destroying either the company or the family — and about being honest that sometimes you cannot fully save both.

### 9.1 Why a family firm's crisis has a different shape

Four structural differences, none of them emotional.

| Difference                   | In a company of strangers                                           | At VSYST                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **The exit option**          | A soured co-founder relationship ends. You may never see them again | You will see them at every wedding, funeral and festival for the next forty years. The relationship has no exit, so every conversation is priced against a lifetime, not a company         |
| **The audience**             | The conversation stays between the parties                          | A third party — a parent, a spouse, an aunt — will hear a version of it within a week, and that version will be the one that sets                                                          |
| **The escalation route**     | HR, the board, an investor, a lawyer                                | All of these exist on paper and none of them feels usable, because using them against a relative reads as an act of war                                                                    |
| **The downside correlation** | Your job and your family's finances are separate risks              | They are the _same_ risk. If the company runs out of cash, three households are affected simultaneously, and the person you are negotiating with is also the person who cannot cushion you |

The fourth is the one that quietly poisons decision-making. In a normal company, a founder arguing for the cautious option and a founder arguing for the aggressive one are two people with different risk appetites. In a family company, they may be two people with different _household exposures_ — one with school fees due, one without — and neither will say so out loud. Half the disagreements that present as strategy disagreements at a family firm are undisclosed personal-cashflow disagreements. **Name that possibility once, in the calm, so it is nameable later.**

Nor is this a VSYST peculiarity. Indian family businesses are measurably weaker than global peers on exactly the governance instruments that make these conversations survivable: PwC's family-business research finds a substantially larger share of Indian firms without a documented succession plan, family-only boards without cross-industry representation, and "old guard" resistance to leadership transition running at roughly twice the global rate ([Forbes India on PwC's 12th Family Business Survey](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/news/indian-family-businesses-high-on-ambition-low-on-governance/2991239/1); [PwC India](https://www.pwc.in/services/entrepreneurial-and-private-business/pwcs-12th-family-business-survey.html)) — **VERIFY LIVE**, these figures move each survey edition. The weakness is normal. It is not therefore safe.

### 9.2 Three claims in one person

The most useful single idea in the family-business literature is Renato Tagiuri and John Davis's **three-circle model**, drawn at Harvard Business School in 1978: a family enterprise is three overlapping systems — **family**, **ownership** and **business** — and any individual sits in one of seven regions formed by the overlaps ([Davis — Three-Circle Model of the Family Business System](https://johndavis.com/three-circle-model-family-business-system/); [Elgar Encyclopedia of Family Business — Three Circle Model](https://www.elgaronline.com/display/book/9781800888722/ch110.xml)). It remains the dominant frame for a reason: it explains, in one picture, why a single sentence lands three different ways.

At VSYST all three founders sit in the **centre** — family member, shareholder, and employee/director simultaneously. That is the hardest position in the model, because every statement carries three claims and the speaker gets to choose which one they answer.

| The sentence you say                                   | Heard as a **business** claim     | Heard as an **ownership** claim                               | Heard as a **family** claim                            |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| "The OMC pursuit isn't converting; we should stop"     | A resource-allocation call        | "My contribution is being written down"                       | "My nephew thinks my judgment is poor"                 |
| "We need to cut founder salaries to statutory minimum" | A runway decision (§5.4)          | "My return on this investment is being reduced"               | "I have to explain this at home tonight"               |
| "You're not the right person to run sales any more"    | A role decision                   | Nothing — shares are untouched, but it will not feel that way | "I am being demoted by someone younger than me"        |
| "We should wind the company down" (§10)                | An honest reading of the evidence | "Our capital is gone"                                         | "The family's name is attached to a failure in Raipur" |

**The mechanical remedy is to say which circle you are speaking from, out loud, before you speak.** It sounds artificial the first two times and then it becomes the most valuable sentence in the company: _"I want to talk about the role, not the shareholding, and not about us."_ It gives the other person permission to hear one claim instead of defending against three. It is also the only reliable way to stop a conversation about a job becoming a conversation about worth.

### 9.3 Ground rules, set in advance

[[03-the-ceo-core-value-and-the-founder-contracts|Lesson 03]] §7 establishes the family clause and the "no business at the table" rule. This section adds what a _crisis_ needs, and the point of writing it now is that a ground rule invented during a crisis is indistinguishable from a tactic.

Six rules. Write them, sign them, keep them one page long, and — critically — **have all three founders agree them while nothing is wrong.**

| #   | Rule                                                                                                                                                                                                      | Why it exists                                                                                                                                                            |
| --- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1   | **No business at the family table.** Any business question raised socially gets one sentence: _"put it on Monday's agenda."_ Paired with a real minuted agenda, so the rule redirects rather than refuses | The single most common failure mode of family firms. Business decided socially is decided without the absent founder, without minutes, and without the ability to say no |
| 2   | **No ambush.** Nobody raises a serious matter about a person without that person knowing in advance what the meeting is about                                                                             | An ambush guarantees a defensive response, and in a family the defensiveness outlives the decision by years                                                              |
| 3   | **Bad news travels up first, and to the person concerned before anyone else.** Never let a founder hear it from a spouse, a cousin, or an employee                                                        | This is the rule that most often gets broken, and it is the one that does the most damage                                                                                |
| 4   | **One agreed sentence leaves the room.** After any hard decision, all three founders agree the _exact_ sentence they will each use with the wider family — before anyone leaves                           | Three well-meaning versions become three different stories, and the family reconciles them into a fourth that nobody said                                                |
| 5   | **The relationship gets a protected slot that the company cannot enter.** One recurring family occasion a month at which the company is not discussed by anyone, for any reason                           | Without a protected slot, the relationship becomes 100% company, and then a bad quarter _is_ a bad relationship                                                          |
| 6   | **The §8.5 warning signs may be raised by any founder, at any time, without it being an attack**                                                                                                          | Written permission, given in advance, is what makes it possible to say "I think you are not okay" to someone senior to you                                               |

Rule 4 is the underrated one. Do not underestimate how much of the damage at a family firm comes not from the decision but from the **reconciliation of versions** in the days afterwards. One sentence, agreed in the room, kills it.

### 9.4 The outside tiebreaker, actually used

[[03-the-ceo-core-value-and-the-founder-contracts|Lesson 03]] §7 names the instrument: one specific human being, named in the founders' agreement while everyone is calm, empowered to decide a defined list of matters, whose decision all three founders agree in advance to accept. Not a mediator. A **decider**. Trigger 10 in §7.4 fires it automatically: the same disagreement reopened a third time goes to the advisor, and the recommendation is minuted.

Four things determine whether it works, and they are all decided before it is used:

- **Chosen for absorbability, not impressiveness.** The right person is the one whose "no" all three founders can absorb without loss of face — often a respected industry figure, a former employer, the company's CA, or a senior person from outside the family whose standing nobody disputes. Standing matters more than expertise, because the job is to end an argument, not to win it.
- **Named by name, in writing, in advance.** A tiebreaker chosen after the deadlock is not a tiebreaker; they are one side's candidate, and the other side knows it.
- **Given the same brief, in writing, from all three.** One shared document of facts, agreed before it is sent. If the founders cannot agree on the facts, that is itself the finding, and the advisor should be told so.
- **Their recommendation is minuted and executed, or the refusal is minuted.** A tiebreaker whose call is quietly ignored is worse than none, because the next deadlock has no instrument left. If the founders decide not to follow it, that decision is recorded with reasons in the minutes ([[C17-board-pack-agenda-and-minutes|C17]]).

Two guardrails. **The tiebreaker is not a therapist and not a family elder.** A family elder has authority in the family circle and none in the ownership or business circles, and using one collapses exactly the separation §9.2 is trying to preserve — the classic Indian pattern where a father's word settles a shareholding question and nobody can appeal it. And **the tiebreaker's remit is written and bounded**: reserved matters, deadlock on strategy, a founder-role question. Not everything.

### 9.5 Separating the shareholder conversation from the employment conversation

This is the single most useful mechanical rule in the lesson (§5.2 step 5), and here is why it works.

A founder who must leave their job is losing three things at once: an income, an identity, and a claim on the future value of the company. Held as one conversation, the three fuse — the person cannot concede any point, because conceding the role feels like conceding the shares, and conceding the shares feels like conceding their place in the family. Held as two conversations on two days, each becomes negotiable, because each has a different instrument, a different document, and a different standard of fairness.

|                                        | **The employment conversation**                                                                            | **The shareholder conversation**                                                                                                                                                        |
| -------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Question on the table**              | Is this person in the right seat, doing the right work, at the right compensation?                         | What happens to the shares, the vesting, the directorship and the future economics?                                                                                                     |
| **Standard of fairness**               | Merit and the needs of the role                                                                            | The agreement signed when everyone was friendly                                                                                                                                         |
| **Governing document**                 | Role description, the CEO–COO contract, [[C03-decision-rights-matrix\|C03]]                                | Founders' / shareholders' agreement, vesting and leaver provisions ([[C02-founders-agreement-and-vesting-checklist\|C02]])                                                              |
| **Who is in the room**                 | The CEO and the person. Nobody else                                                                        | Both, plus — for anything binding — the company secretary and counsel                                                                                                                   |
| **Statutory footprint**                | Internal; offboarding is [[T13-offboarding-checklist\|COO T13]]                                            | Directorship resignation and share transfer touch the MCA record. **VERIFY LIVE with the CS** on the forms and timelines — DIR-11 / DIR-12 and the transfer mechanics are amended often |
| **Timing**                             | First. Day one                                                                                             | Second. A different day, with a gap that is long enough for the first to be absorbed and short enough that uncertainty does not do its own damage — a week is usually right             |
| **The sentence that keeps them apart** | "This is only about the role. Your shareholding is not on the table today and I will not discuss it today" | "We are here about the shares only. The role decision is made and we are not reopening it"                                                                                              |

Three additional rules:

1. **A founder can leave the role and keep the shares** — and, at a family firm, this is often the _right_ outcome, not a compromise. It separates ownership from management, which is the reform Indian family firms most conspicuously lack. Vesting is what makes it fair: earned shares stay earned, unearned shares do not.
2. **Never trade one against the other in the room.** "Take the smaller role and we won't touch the equity" is a threat wearing a suit. It will be remembered as a threat, repeated as a threat, and it converts a governance question into a grievance with a forty-year memory.
3. **The employment conversation is the CEO's alone. The shareholder conversation is the company's** — board-minuted, documented, and with the related-party dimension handled properly, since a transaction between the company and a director's relative engages [Section 188](https://ca2013.com/188-related-party-transactions/) and the directors' duty to act in the company's interest under [Section 166](https://ca2013.com/166-duties-of-directors/) ([[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties|lesson 09]], [[C18-director-duties-and-governance-checklist|C18]]).

### 9.6 Fair process — when the outcome cannot be fair, the process can

The finding that matters most here is decades old and still under-used. W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne's work on **fair process** shows that people care about _how_ a decision was made almost as much as what it was, and that the three elements — **engagement** (they were genuinely consulted), **explanation** (they were told the reasoning), and **expectation clarity** (the new rules and standards were made explicit) — produce voluntary cooperation even from people whose preferred outcome lost ([Kim & Mauborgne — "Fair Process: Managing in the Knowledge Economy", _Harvard Business Review_, January 2003](https://hbr.org/2003/01/fair-process-managing-in-the-knowledge-economy)).

Van der Heyden, Blondel and Carlock carried this specifically into family firms and found that it is _more_ important there, not less, because a family business applies three different and conflicting standards of fairness at once — **need** (family), **merit** (business) and **equality** (ownership) — and procedural fairness is the only thing that can hold a decision together when the distributive standards disagree ([Van der Heyden, Blondel & Carlock — "Fair Process: Striving for Justice in Family Business", _Family Business Review_ 18(1), March 2005](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1741-6248.2005.00027.x); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1498252)).

That is the entire dilemma of §9.2 stated academically. Your uncle applies _need_, the company applies _merit_, and the cap table applies _equality_. No outcome satisfies all three. **A process can.** Concretely, at VSYST, that reduces to five things you can actually do:

1. **Consult before deciding, and let it show.** Ask the question genuinely open, early enough that the answer could change the decision. People can tell the difference between being consulted and being briefed.
2. **Give the reasoning, not just the conclusion** — including what you weighed and rejected. "We considered keeping the pursuit alive at low intensity and rejected it because a half-pursued OMC deal costs a founder-quarter and produces nothing" is a different object from "we're stopping."
3. **Say what the new rules are.** After the decision: what changes, what does not, who decides what now, and what the person is measured on from Monday.
4. **Decide it in the forum where such things are decided, every time** — the minuted founders' meeting, not the car, not the phone at 11 PM, not the wedding.
5. **Be consistent.** Fair process compounds across decisions and collapses the first time you make an exception for the founder you like best. In a family firm, everyone is keeping score whether or not they admit it.

### 9.7 The wider family, spouses, and the version that reaches the wedding

Three people run VSYST. Considerably more than three people have opinions about it, and in a crisis they will all form one.

- **Spouses are stakeholders whether or not you treat them as such.** They carry the household exposure, they absorb the mood, and they will hear something. The choice is not whether they are informed but whether they are informed accurately. The workable rule: the founders agree what spouses are told _as a group_, and each founder tells their own — same content, same week. Ground rule 4's single agreed sentence is the mechanism.
- **Elders are a category, not a person.** In many Chhattisgarhi families the senior generation retains real authority even without shares or a directorship. Decide in advance — in writing, in the founders' agreement — whether an elder is _informed_, _consulted_, or _deciding_. All three are legitimate choices. What is not legitimate is leaving it undefined, because then whoever gets to the elder first sets the outcome.
- **Do not brief the family before you brief the person.** Ground rule 3 exists because this is the most common way a survivable decision becomes an unsurvivable one.
- **Expect the story to arrive before you do.** In a city where the family is known, a dealer's WhatsApp group and a family WhatsApp group are two hops apart. Ship your version first, in your own words, once — the same discipline as §6's incident comms, applied to a different audience.
- **Protect one relationship explicitly.** Ground rule 5. If you can only defend one thing while the company is on fire, defend a standing occasion at which nobody is a founder.

### 9.8 When the person you must confront is senior to you in the family

The hardest case, and the one Indian founder-CEOs actually face: the co-founder whose role has to change is your uncle, your father-in-law, or an elder cousin, and the corporate hierarchy and the family hierarchy point in opposite directions.

There is no technique that makes this comfortable. There are four things that make it possible:

1. **Borrow authority from a rule, not from your position.** "We agreed in March that a pursuit with no written next step for two quarters gets parked" is a rule speaking (§7.1). "I have decided you should stop" is a junior relative speaking to a senior one, and it will be heard as a status claim before it is heard as a business argument. This is the deepest reason the pre-committed trigger is the highest-leverage practice in this lesson.
2. **Use the outside tiebreaker early rather than late** (§9.4). A recommendation from someone with independent standing lets a senior relative change position without losing face — which is often the actual obstacle, and is a solvable one.
3. **Give the role change a form that is honourable and true.** Not a face-saving fiction — §5.2 is explicit that a smaller job inside the company is not a kinder version of "leave". But where the honest answer genuinely is a narrower role that plays to what the person is best at, say so plainly and write down what the role does and does not decide ([[20-the-ceo-own-operating-system-and-succession|lesson 20]] §8.3 has the same rule for founders after a succession).
4. **Take the private cost yourself.** Say it in person, alone, first, before any meeting, without a witness and without a document — and then say the same thing in the meeting, unchanged. The private version is not a softer version. It is the same sentence, given the dignity of a closed door.

**And the honest ending.** Sometimes there is no version in which both the company and the relationship come through undamaged. This lesson will not pretend otherwise. What you control is whether the damage came from the decision or from the way you made it — because families forgive hard decisions far more often than they forgive being ambushed, misled, talked about, or discovered. Every mechanism in this section is aimed at that single distinction.

### 9.9 The fortnight around the conversation

Everything above is machinery. This is the machinery running, on a calendar, for the case the whole section is built around: **a co-founder's role has to change, and he is at dinner tonight.**

**Day 0 evening is the hardest single instruction in this lesson.** You will want to soften it at dinner — to explain again, to check he is all right, to reopen a point that landed badly. If you need to say something at dinner, say the one sentence that has nothing to do with the company: _"I'm glad you're here."_

**And the fortnight leaks.** Assume it. Between Day −3 and Day 0 somebody's spouse will know, and by Day +1 a cousin will have a version. That is not a failure of the protocol — it is the reason the protocol front-loads rule 3 (the person hears it first) and rule 4 (one agreed sentence). You cannot stop the story travelling in a city where the family is known. You can make sure that the version travelling is the one all three of you agreed, and that the person it is about heard it from you first, in a room, with the door closed.

## 10. Deciding to stop

Most courses for founders end at resilience. They will teach you to survive the crisis, cut the burn, hold the team, tell the story — and then stop, because the next page is the one nobody wants to write. This lesson writes it, for three reasons.

**Because it is a real outcome and pretending otherwise makes it worse.** The base rates are not a secret: CB Insights' post-mortem analysis of 431 failed startups found that 70% ran out of capital, with poor product-market fit (43%), bad timing (29%) and unsustainable unit economics (19%) underneath ([CB Insights — The Top Reasons Startups Fail](https://www.cbinsights.com/research/report/startup-failure-reasons-top/)). A founder who has never once thought about what stopping looks like will meet the question for the first time at the worst possible moment — with four weeks of cash, three angry vendors and no plan.

**Because for a director of an Indian Pvt Ltd it is not only an emotional question, it is a legal one.** From the point where the company is heading towards insolvency, the director's duty starts to shift: continuing to trade while knowing there is no reasonable prospect of avoiding insolvency, without taking every step to minimise the loss to creditors, is exactly what [s.66(2) of the IBC](https://ibclaw.in/section-66-fraudulent-trading-or-wrongful-trading/) makes personally actionable ([AZB & Partners — Liability of directors for 'wrongful trading' under the IBC](https://www.azbpartners.com/bank/liability-of-directors-for-wrongful-trading-under-the-insolvency-bankruptcy-code/); [Law.asia — insolvency directors' duties shift to creditors](https://law.asia/ibc-directors-liability/)). **Deciding late is not neutral. It transfers your company's losses onto other people, and Indian law has a name for that.**

**Because a company that stops well and a company that stops badly produce completely different futures for the three people in it.** Raipur is not large. The dealers, the CA, the bank, the association and the family are all still there afterwards. In a district market, how you ended is your reference for the next thing you do.

This section is not advice to stop. VSYST is early, cheap to run and has not yet run its bet. It is advice to know — in advance, in writing, in the calm — what evidence would mean _stop_, so that if the day comes you are executing a decision rather than discovering one.

### 10.1 What "this is not working" looks like in evidence

The failure mode here is not that founders miss the evidence. It is that founders have a feeling for eighteen months and never once convert it into a testable claim — so the feeling can neither be confirmed nor dismissed, and it just sits there taxing every decision.

Convert it. Each row below is a feeling on the left and the only thing on the right that could settle it.

| The feeling                            | The evidence that would confirm it                                                                                                                                     | The evidence that would refute it                                                                                         | Where it is read                                                                             |
| -------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| _"Dealers don't really want this"_     | Of the last 20 qualified conversations, fewer than 2 reached a paid subscription, and the losses cluster on **"I don't need this"** rather than price, trust or timing | The objections cluster on price, feature gaps or timing — all of which are addressable, none of which is a demand verdict | [[C10-founder-led-sales-pipeline-and-script\|C10]] objection bank; the pipeline tab          |
| _"They sign up but never use it"_      | Activation under 40% and median days-to-first-invoice above 21, **after** the onboarding SOP has been run as written for two full quarters                             | Activation moves when onboarding changes — which makes it an execution problem with a known remedy                        | CEO dashboard ([[17-the-numbers-a-ceo-watches\|lesson 17]])                                  |
| _"We can't get paid enough to matter"_ | Realised price per GSTIN, after discounting, leaves gross margin per tenant that cannot carry support at any plausible scale                                           | Margin works at the price a minority already pay — a segmentation problem, not a model problem                            | [[C12-unit-economics-and-business-model-calculator\|C12]]                                    |
| _"Every sale needs me"_                | After 30+ closes, no deal has been closed by anyone but the CEO, **and** a written script plus a trained person has been tried and failed                              | Nobody has actually tried. There is no script, or there is a script and no second seller                                  | [[C10-founder-led-sales-pipeline-and-script\|C10]]; dashboard row on % not personally closed |
| _"We are always out of money"_         | Three consecutive quarters where burn was cut as planned and revenue still did not cover the reduced burn's trajectory                                                 | Burn was never actually cut, or the cuts were made and revenue is now visibly tracking                                    | [[C13-runway-burn-and-scenario-planner\|C13]]                                                |
| _"The founders have checked out"_      | Dealer conversations booked by the founding team fell below the trigger-4 threshold for two months and **nobody noticed or minded**                                    | The number fell and it was raised, argued about and fixed                                                                 | §7.4 ladder; the weekly note                                                                 |

Four rules make the evidence honest rather than decorative.

1. **The mitigation must have been run as written before its failure counts.** A plan that was half-executed has told you nothing about the plan. This is the single most common way a good bet gets killed early and a bad bet survives late — both from the same defect. The pre-mortem tab's _dated action, named owner_ discipline (§3.3) exists so that "we tried that" is a checkable claim.
2. **Three consecutive periods, not one.** One bad quarter is noise; three quarters of a flat top-of-tree input after a real intervention is a finding.
3. **Name which of the three failures it is, because only one of them is a stop.** _No demand_ — the market does not want the thing at any price you can charge. _No distribution_ — they want it and you cannot reach them affordably. _No product_ — they want it, you can reach them, and what you built does not do the job. Distribution and product failures are pivots, sometimes brutal ones. Only a demand failure, confirmed, is a wind-down argument — and it is the rarest of the three, which is why "no demand" should be the last conclusion you reach, not the first.
4. **Beware the false stop signals.** A lost marquee deal. A funded competitor's press release. A tired founder in February. A month with no closes over a festival period. None of these is evidence about the business; all of them feel like it. Sunk-cost reasoning gets the blame for keeping companies alive too long ([sunk cost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunk_cost), [escalation of commitment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escalation_of_commitment)), and it deserves it — but the mirror error, quitting on a mood in a bad month, has no fashionable name and costs founders just as many good companies.

**The sentence test, for the 2 AM version of this question.** Write down: _"For this to work, X has to be true."_ Then ask two things — is X something you can test in the next 90 days for less than a stated rupee amount, and have you already tested it twice and failed? If X is untested, you have a plan, not a verdict. If X has failed twice on honest attempts, you have a verdict, and the useful conversation is §10.2's.

### 10.2 Pivot, park, hand over, or wind down

"Stopping" is four different decisions that founders routinely collapse into one, and then choose the worst of them by default. Eric Ries's definition is the useful anchor: a pivot is "a structured course correction designed to test a new fundamental hypothesis about the product, strategy, and engine of growth" ([The Lean Startup — principles](https://theleanstartup.com/principles)) — a _change of bet with the same team, the same company and the accumulated learning intact_. A wind-down is the opposite motion: the orderly return of everything the company holds to the people it belongs to.

|                                       | **Persevere**                                                                       | **Pivot**                                                                                                   | **Park (dormant)**                                                                                            | **Hand over / sell**                                                                                 | **Wind down**                                                                                                              |
| ------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **What changes**                      | Nothing structural; execution intensity                                             | The hypothesis — segment, product, model or motion. One of them, not all                                    | Nothing. Operations stop; the shell and the IP survive                                                        | Ownership of the product and the customers                                                           | Everything ends                                                                                                            |
| **What is kept**                      | Everything                                                                          | Team, cash, learning, company, brand                                                                        | The company, the name, the IP, the ability to restart                                                         | The product's life for its users; possibly the team                                                  | The learning, the relationships, your name                                                                                 |
| **Precondition that makes it honest** | The bet has not actually been run yet                                               | You can state the _new_ hypothesis in one sentence and the test that settles it in 90 days                  | No statutory dues outstanding, no disputes, no prosecutions — the s.455 conditions are strict                 | Someone genuinely wants it: a competitor, a dealer group, an OMC vendor, or the team itself          | The demand verdict is confirmed, **and you still have the money to end properly** (§10.6)                                  |
| **What it costs**                     | The runway it consumes                                                              | One to two quarters and a great deal of credibility if repeated                                             | Annual filings and a CS retainer — small, but not zero                                                        | Time, legal fees, and a difficult conversation about price                                           | The wind-down reserve, and a year of your reputation's attention                                                           |
| **The trap**                          | "One more month", indefinitely                                                      | Pivot #3. A team that has pivoted three times has no hypothesis, it has a habit                             | The zombie: a parked company that nobody restarts and nobody closes, quietly accruing filing defaults         | Selling for a rupee to avoid a decision, and leaving your dealers with a new owner who does not care | Deciding it when the reserve is already gone, which converts a dignified exit into an expensive one                        |
| **Fit at VSYST today**                | **This is the position.** The bet has not been run — §3.4's top three are all unrun | Live option if the demand is real and the _segment_ is wrong (lubricant distributors instead of pumps, say) | A genuine option for a bootstrapped family company that wants to stop the work without destroying the vehicle | Plausible in 24 months if the product works and the motion does not                                  | Not on the table, and the reason to write §10.7 anyway is that it is cheap to write now and impossible to write well later |

Two hard notes on this table.

**The zombie is the real enemy, not the wind-down.** A company that is neither pivoting nor stopping — three founders working evenings, no growth, no decision, nobody willing to say the sentence — destroys more of a founder's life than a clean shutdown does, and it destroys it slowly enough that no single month ever justifies acting. Every mechanism in §7 exists to prevent exactly that state.

**And "park" is genuinely underrated for a company like VSYST.** A bootstrapped family Pvt Ltd with no debt, no outside investors, no employees and a product that works has an option a funded startup does not: stop the work, keep the vehicle. [Section 455](https://ca2013.com/455-dormant-company/) lets an "inactive" company — broadly, one with no significant accounting transaction in the last two financial years — apply for **dormant** status by special resolution and Form MSC-1, receive a certificate in MSC-2, and then live on a much lighter compliance diet, filing an annual return of financial position in MSC-3, with revival via MSC-4/MSC-5 when you want it back ([ClearTax — dormant company under s.455](https://cleartax.in/s/dormant-company-act); [TaxGuru — s.455 dormant company status](https://taxguru.in/company-law/section-455-companies-act-2013-dormant-company-status.html)). The conditions are strict — no pending inspection, inquiry, investigation or prosecution, no outstanding statutory dues, no management dispute — and the forms, fees and timelines move; **VERIFY LIVE with the CS** before you rely on a single sentence of this. But if the honest answer one day is _"not now, not never"_, this is the instrument that says it, and it costs a fraction of what a strike-off-and-reincorporate cycle costs.

### 10.3 Who decides, and how the decision is made

A wind-down is the largest one-way door a company has ([[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]]), and it is a **reserved matter** — it does not belong to the CEO alone, and no amount of technical-founder conviction substitutes for a board decision properly taken ([[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties|lesson 09]], [[C03-decision-rights-matrix|C03]]).

Run it as a decision, not as an announcement:

1. **The CEO writes the case, in prose, and circulates it seven days ahead.** The same discipline as a board pack ([[C17-board-pack-agenda-and-minutes|C17]]): the evidence from §10.1 with numbers and dates, the four options from §10.2 each argued honestly, the recommended route, the money required to execute it, and what the recommendation would look like if it were wrong. A wind-down argued from a slide deck at the meeting is a wind-down decided by whoever speaks best.
2. **Somebody is assigned to argue against it.** The contra-memo ([[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties|lesson 09]] §8.4), one page, written by a named director. If nobody can write a credible case for continuing, that is itself a finding worth recording.
3. **The outside advisor is in the room** (§9.4). This is the decision their standing exists for.
4. **The decision names a route and a date**, not a direction. "We stop" is not a decision; "we file for strike-off, the last day of service to dealers is 31 March, the CS starts on 1 April" is.
5. **It is minuted, with any dissent recorded by name.** Not for ceremony — because the minutes are the record that shows _when_ the directors concluded what they concluded, and §10.4 is about why that date matters.
6. **Nothing is announced until the money to do what you are announcing is confirmed** (§10.6). The single most damaging sequence available to you is announcing a shutdown you then cannot fund.

**Then tell people in this order, and never let anyone learn it out of order.**

| #   | Who                                                 | When                                                               | By whom                      | What they get                                                                                                   |
| --- | --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | The three founders                                  | The decision meeting                                               | —                            | The decision, the route, the date, the agreed sentence (rule 4, §9.3)                                           |
| 2   | CS, CA, counsel                                     | Same week                                                          | CEO                          | The route and the timetable; they tell you what has to happen before anything is said publicly                  |
| 3   | Employees, if any                                   | Before customers. Always                                           | CEO, in person, individually | The date, the final settlement terms, notice, the reference commitment, the help you will actually give (§10.7) |
| 4   | Dealers (customers)                                 | On a stated date, all at once, in writing and by call              | CEO                          | Service end date, data export plan, refunds, who to contact — see §10.7                                         |
| 5   | Vendors and partners                                | Same week as dealers                                               | CEO                          | The end date, the settlement plan, and the return or termination of anything of theirs you hold                 |
| 6   | The wider family                                    | Same week, from each founder to their own household, same sentence | Each founder                 | §9.7                                                                                                            |
| 7   | The association / the district / the public account | Last, when the above is done                                       | CEO                          | §10.8's written account                                                                                         |

Two prohibitions. **Never let a dealer work it out from an unanswered support message** — the silent fade is the version people never forgive, and it is the one that happens by default when founders are exhausted. And **one voice throughout** (§6.2): every question, from every audience, is answered by the CEO or by a person reading the CEO's words.

### 10.4 The director's duties in the run-up — the part with legal teeth

This subsection is the reason a founder should read §10 while things are fine.

An Indian director's baseline duty is to act in good faith to promote the objects of the company for the benefit of its members as a whole, and in the best interests of the company, its employees, shareholders, the community and the environment, exercising "due and reasonable care, skill and diligence" with independent judgment ([s.166, Companies Act 2013](https://ca2013.com/166-duties-of-directors/)). What changes as insolvency approaches is **who the duty is owed to.** Once the company enters what practitioners call the twilight zone, the duty tilts from shareholders towards creditors, and the IBC gives that tilt teeth: [s.66(1)](https://ibclaw.in/section-66-fraudulent-trading-or-wrongful-trading/) covers carrying on business with intent to defraud creditors, and **s.66(2) is the one that catches ordinary, well-meaning founders** — a director can be made personally liable to contribute to the company's assets where they knew or ought to have known that there was no reasonable prospect of avoiding insolvency and did not exercise due diligence in minimising the potential loss to creditors ([AZB & Partners](https://www.azbpartners.com/bank/liability-of-directors-for-wrongful-trading-under-the-insolvency-bankruptcy-code/); [TaxGuru — remedy against fraudulent or wrongful trading](https://taxguru.in/corporate-law/remedy-fraudulent-wrongful-trading-ibc.html); [Law.asia](https://law.asia/ibc-directors-liability/)). Note the words: not _intent_, not _dishonesty_ — **knew or ought to have known**, and _did not take the steps_.

Eight temptations, all of which feel reasonable at 11 PM, and what each one actually is. **Every row here is a "verify live with the CS and the CA before you act" row — this is a map of where to ask, not legal advice.**

| The temptation                               | What it looks like at a three-person company                                                                                             | The exposure                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         | What to do instead                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| -------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

The question in any later proceeding is _what did the directors know, and when_ — and the answer comes from your records                                                                                                                         | Keep minuting. From the day the question becomes live, **every** discussion of solvency gets a minute, a date and the professional advice taken                                                    |

**The paper trail is the defence, and it is free.** From the first meeting at which "should we stop" is a real question, keep: the dated minutes of every solvency discussion, the evidence pack, the date and content of each professional's advice, the date you stopped taking new credit or prepayments, and the record of what you did to minimise loss to creditors. Directors who are pursued after a shutdown are rarely pursued for the decision. They are pursued for the four months of silence before it.

The fourth option — **hand the product over**: a business-transfer or asset-sale agreement moving the code, the tenants and the contracts to a buyer, with the dealers' consent and a migration plan. It is worth real effort anyway, because it is the only ending in which your dealers do not lose the thing they came to depend on.

**Read the whole table for one pattern:** every route begins with _liabilities extinguished_, _dues cleared_, _filings current_. **The cheapest, fastest, most dignified exit is only available to a company that stayed compliant on the way down** — which is the practical reason §10.4's fourth row matters more than it looks, and the reason the COO's compliance calendar ([[T16-compliance-calendar|T16]]) keeps running until the last day.

### 10.6 The exit reserve — decide while you can still afford to stop well

Here is the asymmetry nobody warns founders about. **Stopping costs money.** Every obligation in §10.7 — the refunds, the final settlements, the CA's closing accounts, the CS's filings, the engineering weeks that produce clean data exports — has a price, and it falls due at precisely the moment the company has the least cash it has ever had. Founders discover this in the wrong order: they run to zero, _then_ decide to stop, and find that the dignified ending is the one option they can no longer buy.

So split the reserve floor in [[C13-runway-burn-and-scenario-planner|C13]] into two named numbers: the **operating reserve** (what you keep so a bad month is not an emergency) and the **exit reserve** (what it would cost to end the company properly, today). Put the second on the CEO dashboard as a line that is never spent, and read trigger 3 in §7.4 against runway **net of** it.

What goes into the number, at VSYST's actual shape today. **All figures illustrative and to be priced with the CA and CS — VERIFY LIVE.**

| Line                                      | What it covers                                                                                               | Scale today (3 founders, no employees, no lease, no debt)                                                       | What changes it                                                                                      |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Statutory dues, cleared in full**       | Any TDS, GST or PF deducted or collected and not yet remitted                                                | Should be ₹0 at all times, by policy (§10.4)                                                                    | Never let it be anything else                                                                        |
| **Customer refunds**                      | Unexpired portion of any annual prepayment                                                                   | ₹0 pre-revenue; rises directly with every annual plan sold                                                      | Sell monthly during any period when your stop-criteria are close to firing                           |
| **Vendor and infrastructure settlements** | AWS, app-store developer accounts, gateway, domains, ERPNext hosting, any tool on annual terms               | Small, and knowable — the COO's vendor register ([[T14-vendor-and-tool-register\|T14]]) already lists every one | Annual contracts and anything with an early-termination clause                                       |
| **Professional fees**                     | CA's final accounts and closing audit, CS's route (STK-2 or otherwise), counsel on any contract terminations | The largest single line for a company of this shape                                                             | The route in §10.5. Voluntary liquidation is an order of magnitude above strike-off                  |
| **People**                                | Notice, full-and-final settlement, any statutory entitlement                                                 | ₹0 today. **This line is why the reserve must be re-priced at every hire**                                      | Each employee adds a real, computable number. Ask the CA for it _before_ the offer letter, not after |
| **Data return and shutdown engineering**  | Building the exports, running them per tenant, keeping a read-only window open, verified deletion            | Founder weeks rather than rupees — but the read-only window has a real infrastructure cost                      | Whether you built export tooling early (you should — §10.7)                                          |
| **Buffer**                                | The things this table does not know about                                                                    | 25% of the above, minimum                                                                                       | Experience                                                                                           |

Three rules for the number.

- **Compute it once a year at the annual plan and after every hire, lease or annual contract**, and write it in the `Runway & Scenarios` tab with the date. It is a five-minute calculation that changes what "runway" means.
- **Ring-fence it in language, not in a separate account.** A three-person company does not need a second bank account for this; it needs three founders who have agreed, in writing, that the number is not spendable, and a dashboard line that shows it.
- **The exit reserve also buys the good endings, not just the tidy ones.** It is what lets you hand the product to a buyer instead of switching it off, keep the export portal open for a quarter, and pay a contractor to finish the migration. A company that stops with zero has only one ending available, and it is the worst one.

**The sentence to remember: decide to stop while you can still afford to stop well.** That is the entire argument of §10 compressed, and it is the reason the decision is made against evidence and a date rather than against an empty bank account.

### 10.7 How to end well — the five obligations, in order

The order matters, and it is not the order exhaustion suggests.

**1. Return the customers' data. Before anything is switched off.**

This is first because it is the obligation with a deadline you cannot extend, and because a dealer's ledger, invoices and rate history are not your records — they are his, sitting on your servers, and Indian tax and GST record-retention rules mean he is legally required to keep them for years after you are gone. Concretely:

- **Announce a service-end date with real notice** — 60 to 90 days is decent, and honour whatever your contract says if it says more. Say it once, in writing and by phone, to every dealer on the same day.
- **Ship a complete export, per GSTIN, in a format a dealer's accountant can actually open**: the full ledger with running balances, every invoice as a PDF _and_ as data, vouchers and payments, rate history with timestamps, the customer master, and dispatch records. CSV or Excel plus PDFs. Not a Mongo dump.
- **Add a closing statement of balances per dealer, and offer to have it reconciled** — the same instrument §5.6 uses for a ledger error, applied to the whole book. This is what turns "the software shut down" into "the software shut down and my books tie."
- **Keep a read-only export window open** for a defined period after service end if the exit reserve allows it. This is the single kindest thing on the list and it costs the least.
- **Then delete, and confirm it in writing.** The [DPDP Act 2023, s.8](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/186118625/) requires a data fiduciary to erase personal data once consent is withdrawn or the purpose for which it was collected is no longer being served, unless retention is required by law ([DPDP Act, 2023 — MeitY](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2024/06/2bf1f0e9f04e6fb4f8fef35e82c42aa5.pdf)) — **VERIFY LIVE with counsel**, since the DPDP Rules 2025 phase obligations in over time. Send each dealer a dated confirmation naming what was deleted and when, and state the backup expiry date rather than pretending backups vanish instantly.
- **And never, under any circumstances, hold data hostage against an unpaid invoice.** Chase the dues separately. A company that withholds a man's ledger to collect ₹40,000 has told an entire district something about itself that no later explanation corrects.

**2. Clear the dues, in the right order.** Statutory money first — TDS, GST and PF deducted or collected on someone else's behalf were never yours to allocate (§10.4). Then employees. Then vendors, by the order the CS confirms is lawful, not by who is loudest or closest to the family. **Founders come last**: unpaid founder salary and founder loans to the company are the final claim, and if they go unpaid, that is the correct outcome, not an injustice. A director who repays himself ahead of a supplier has created a related-party problem and a reputational one in the same transaction.

**3. Help people land — properly, not gesturally.** Notice paid rather than worked where you can afford it; final settlement paid within a stated number of days and actually met; a written, honest, specific reference letter for every person; and — the part that matters more than any of it — **three named introductions per person, made by you, by name, with a sentence about what they are good at.** Then tell them you will take a reference call for them forever, and mean it. Where there is any doubt about notice periods, final-settlement timelines or statutory entitlements, **VERIFY LIVE with the CA**. The offboarding mechanics themselves are the COO's ([[T13-offboarding-checklist|T13]]); the introductions are yours and cannot be delegated.

**4. Tell the counterparties, and return what is theirs.** The gateway, the cloud account, the app-store listings (unpublish deliberately, with a final in-app notice — do not simply abandon them), ERPNext, the domain, any partner or pilot counterparty, and any relationship carried in a founder's name ([[14-partnerships-ecosystem-and-founder-relationships|lesson 14]]). Terminate per the contract's notice terms rather than by going quiet. Anything of theirs that you hold — data, credentials, hardware, brand assets — goes back, with a written confirmation.

**5. Write the account of what was learned.** §10.8.

| Obligation           | The concrete act                                                                           | The deadline                                                  | Owner             |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------- |
| Data returned        | Per-GSTIN export + closing balance statement + read-only window                            | Delivered **before** the service-end date, not on it          | CEO (technical)   |
| Data deleted         | Verified erasure + dated written confirmation + backup expiry stated                       | Within the window counsel confirms                            | CEO               |
| Statutory dues       | Every rupee deducted or collected, remitted; final GST, TDS, PF and ROC filings; final ITR | Per each statute's own calendar — **VERIFY LIVE with the CA** | CA, chased by CEO |
| Employees            | Notice, full-and-final, reference letters, three introductions each                        | F&F within the stated days; introductions within two weeks    | CEO               |
| Vendors and partners | Contractual termination notices, settlements in the lawful order, assets returned          | The same week dealers are told                                | CEO               |
| The account          | The written post-mortem (§10.8)                                                            | Within 30 days of the last day of service                     | CEO               |

**What ending well buys.** Not absolution — the company still ended. It buys the three things a founder actually needs next: people who will work with you again, dealers and partners who will take your call about the next thing, and a family name in Raipur that survived a failure intact. Those are not consolation prizes. In a district market they are the entire starting capital of whatever you do next, and they are bought or destroyed in the eight weeks after the decision.

### 10.8 The written account of what was learned

Write it within thirty days of the last day, while the detail is still exact. It is the same instrument as the incident post-mortem the COO runs ([[T17-incident-postmortem|T17]]) pointed at the company instead of at an outage, and it has the same two rules: **specific about decisions, blameless about people** — with the one exception that you, the CEO, are named for your own calls.

Seven sections, three pages, no adjectives:

1. **What we set out to do** — the original strategy kernel ([[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager|C04]]), quoted verbatim from the date it was written, not as you now remember it.
2. **What we believed** — the assumptions we were betting on, from the assumption register, each with the date it was recorded.
3. **What we tested, and what it cost** — the experiments actually run, with dates and rupees.
4. **What we found** — the numbers. Conversations held, dealers signed, activation, days-to-first-invoice, paying GSTINs, churn, burn. Fractions, not percentages (§17's rule).
5. **What we got wrong** — decisions, in order of cost, each with what we knew at the time. This is where [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] earns everything it cost to maintain: you can compare what you predicted with what happened, which is impossible from memory and trivial from the log.
6. **What we would do differently** — and, more usefully, _what evidence would have changed our minds sooner, and why we did not go looking for it._
7. **What we would tell someone starting the same thing** — the section other people will actually read.

Then do the exercise that only a company with a pre-mortem can do: **put §3.4's ten candidate causes of death beside what actually happened, and grade them.** Which cause did you rank first? Which one killed you? Was the early-warning signal present and ignored, present and unnoticed, or genuinely absent? Klein's premortem is designed to surface causes in advance ([HBR, 2007](https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem)); the shutdown account is the only place its accuracy can ever be scored. Whatever you do next, you will start it with a calibrated sense of your own risk judgment, which is a rare and expensive thing to own.

File it in the vault. Publishing a version is a choice, not a duty — but the honest ones travel, and in a small market a founder who wrote clearly about why something did not work is treated very differently from one who went quiet.

### 10.9 The "what would make us stop" note — written now, sealed, dated

This is the artefact §10 exists to produce, and the right time to write it is the time when it feels least necessary.

It is not a prediction of failure and it is not a resignation letter. It is the same Ulysses pact as every trigger in §7, aimed at the one decision that a founding team will otherwise never make on time — because the cost of raising it socially is so high that it gets postponed past the point where the good endings are still available. Writing it in the calm means that if the day comes, nobody has to be the person who says the sentence first. **The document says it. You only have to read it out.**

```
WHAT WOULD MAKE US STOP — VSYST Technologies Pvt. Ltd.
Written: <date>      Signed: <all three founder-directors>
Reviewed: every quarterly pre-mortem. Never edited in a month when any condition is live.

1. THE CONDITIONS  (all must be falsifiable, dated, and readable from an existing source)
   C1  <e.g. after two full quarters of the onboarding SOP run as written, activation
        is still under 40% and median days-to-first-invoice still above 21>
   C2  <e.g. of the last 30 qualified dealer conversations, fewer than 3 became paying
        GSTINs, and the dominant recorded objection is "I don't need this">
   C3  <e.g. runway net of the exit reserve is under 4 months and no funded plan
        exists that both founders outside the CEO believe>

2. WHAT WE DO IF THEY FIRE
   Not "we stop". We convene the §10.3 decision within 14 days, with the evidence pack,
   the contra-memo, and the outside advisor present. The options on the table are the
   five in §10.2, and one of them is chosen with a route and a date.

3. THE ROUTE WE EXPECT TO TAKE      <strike-off / voluntary liquidation / dormant / hand over>
   Chosen in advance so the choice is not made while exhausted. Re-checked with the CS annually.

4. THE EXIT RESERVE                  ₹ <amount>, as computed on <date>, never spendable.
   Re-priced at every hire, lease and annual contract.

5. WHO OPENS THIS                    <named founder>, and any founder may call for it to be read
   aloud at any founders' meeting, without that being an act of disloyalty.

6. WHAT WE OWE, WHATEVER HAPPENS     The five obligations of §10.7, in that order, funded by 4.

7. WHAT THIS NOTE IS NOT             It is not a forecast, not a lack of belief, and not a
   secret. All three founders hold a copy. Nobody may cite it as evidence of anyone's
   commitment.
```

Three rules make it work, and they are the same three that make any pre-commitment work.

- **All three sign it, and each keeps a copy.** A stop-note held by one founder is that founder's opinion.
- **It is re-read at every quarterly pre-mortem, before the ladder** (§7.6) — and edited only in a quarter when no condition is anywhere near live. Rewriting your stop-criteria during a bad month is the exact failure the note exists to prevent.
- **It is row 14 of the trigger ladder**, not a separate document floating in the vault. Everything else in §7.4 triggers an action inside the company; this one triggers the conversation about the company itself, and it belongs at the bottom of the same ladder so that it is read every time the ladder is read.

**And the honest last word on it.** Writing this note does not make stopping more likely. Every piece of evidence in this lesson points the other way: the companies that die badly are the ones that never named the conditions, drifted into the zombie state of §10.2, and lost the ability to choose their own ending. The note's real function, in the overwhelming majority of cases, is that it never fires — and that in the meantime the three of you stop carrying an unspoken question that would otherwise sit under every meeting you have.

## 11. At VSYST — applying this now

- **Put the pre-mortem in the calendar before you close this file.** Seventy-five minutes, all three founder-directors, quarterly, the first one this month. Run it silent-first (§3.2) — the ten minutes of writing alone is the whole mechanism, and it is the part that gets dropped. At a family company where seniority in the room and seniority in the family are different rankings, this is the cheapest instrument you own for getting the domain-expert director's real opinion on the record.
- **Act on the ranking, not on the vividness.** §3.4's top three are activation (#2), selling instead of building (#10) and the CEO being the only salesperson (#4). Not the breach, not the incumbent, not the OMC. If this quarter's work does not visibly attack those three, the risk exercise has been decorative.
- **Agree, sign and print the trigger ladder (§7.4).** One page, on the wall, read aloud at every pre-mortem. Unagreed triggers are worthless because the argument you avoided in the calm returns in the storm — and rung 2 is the one that decides, in advance, the day VSYST becomes a wartime company, so nobody has to win that argument while it is happening.
- **Write the six family ground rules and name the outside tiebreaker — by name — this month** (§9.3, §9.4). A ground rule invented during a crisis is indistinguishable from a tactic, and a tiebreaker chosen after the deadlock is one side's candidate. Both cost an evening now and are unbuyable later.
- **Pre-write exactly one crisis playbook, and make it the money-affecting ledger error.** It is the crisis with the highest product of likelihood and irreversibility for this specific product, the one where the first hour decides the outcome, and the one where you already have most of the tooling. [[C24-crisis-comms-and-statement-kit|C24]] holds the words; [[C30-pre-mortem-crisis-playbook-and-succession|C30]] holds the playbook.
- **Compute the exit reserve this quarter, even though it is small** (§10.6) — and put it on the CEO dashboard as a line that is never spent. Right now, with no employees, no lease and no debt, VSYST's number is as small as it will ever be. That is a fact about today's shape, not a permanent feature, and it should be re-priced the day the first offer letter goes out.
- **Write the stop note and seal it, while nothing is wrong** (§10.9). Pre-revenue, bootstrapped, three founders who like each other — this is the easiest month VSYST will ever have to write those three conditions down honestly.
- **Decide the prepayment policy before billing ships, not after.** Annual prepay is the fastest cash lever a pre-revenue company has (§5.1) and it is simultaneously a refundable liability sitting on someone else's balance sheet (§10.4, §10.6). Write the refund terms into the subscription agreement now, and treat prepayments as a liability in the workbook rather than as cash. This is a one-line decision today and an ugly renegotiation later.
- **Keep every filing current, permanently.** Not for tidiness: the cheap exit, the dormant option, the fundraise, the GSP application and the ISO path are all only available to a company that stayed compliant ([[T16-compliance-calendar|COO T16]], [[certification-and-standards-roadmap|Certification and Standards Roadmap]]). Three continuous years of unfiled returns disqualifies all three of you for five years, across every company you might ever hold — which is the largest disproportion between cost and consequence anywhere in this course.
- **Write §8.5's warning-signs card and hand a copy to the other two founders.** Written permission, given in advance, is what makes it possible for a junior relative to say "I think you are not okay" to a senior one.
- **What not to do:** do not build a second risk register — operational risk lives in the COO's [[T15-risk-register|T15]] and only existential risk comes to the CEO's tab (§4). Do not improvise crisis comms; the whole point of [[C24-crisis-comms-and-statement-kit|C24]] is that the words exist before the incident. Do not discuss a wind-down with anyone outside the three founders and the CS before a decision has been taken — a half-considered shutdown rumour in a district market is very expensive and completely unrecoverable. Do not renegotiate the ladder or the stop note in a month when a condition is live. And never brief the family before you brief the person (§9.3, rule 3).

## 12. Exercises

Five exercises. Do them in this order — 12.1 produces the material 12.2 and 12.4 depend on, and 12.5 is what makes the other four survivable at a family company.

**12.1 — Run the pre-mortem, properly, with all three founders (75 min, one room, paper, no laptops).** Set the scene in one written sentence with a real date: _"It is [today + 24 months]. VSYST has shut down DZZLO OMS. The company failed."_ Ten minutes of silence, each founder writing eight to twelve causes **alone**. Round-robin read-out with no commentary and no defending. Merge only genuinely identical causes. Then each founder scores every row for likelihood and impact 1–5 **without seeing the others' scores**, and the three sets go side by side into the `Risk & Pre-Mortem` tab of [vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx](toolkit/vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx). Spend twenty timeboxed minutes arguing **only the rows where the scores differ by two or more** — those disagreements, not the averages, are the output. Take the top three by agreed score and give each one a **dated action and one named owner, typed before anyone leaves the room**, plus the early-warning signal that must pass all four tests in §3.5. **Artefact:** a completed section A of the `Risk & Pre-Mortem` tab with three independent score columns, and three mitigation rows with names and dates, filed in [[C30-pre-mortem-crisis-playbook-and-succession|C30]]. **Success test:** at least one cause appears that the CEO did not write, and at least one row shows a two-point disagreement. If all three lists match, the silent-first rule was not enforced and the meeting was a conversation, not a pre-mortem.

**12.2 — Agree, sign and print the trigger ladder (45 min, all three, immediately after 12.1).** Take §7.4's thirteen rows as a draft and work each one until every part of §7.2's anatomy is filled: signal, threshold, observation point, pre-decided action, one owner, review date. Delete any row whose signal is not already measured somewhere you look weekly — a trigger on a number nobody reads is decoration. Argue the thresholds now, in the calm, especially rung 2, and write down what was agreed rather than what was said. Then **add row 14 pointing at the stop note from 12.4**, sign the sheet with all three names and today's date, print it, and put it on the wall. **Artefact:** a signed, dated one-page trigger ladder in the `Risk & Pre-Mortem` tab and on paper, with a calendar entry to re-read it at every quarterly pre-mortem. **Success test:** for every rung, you can name the exact file, tab or screen where the signal is read, and the exact first action that would start on the day it fires — without discussion.

**12.3 — Write one crisis playbook out in full, using [[C30-pre-mortem-crisis-playbook-and-succession|C30]] (60 min, CEO alone, then 20 min review with both founders).** Choose the money-affecting ledger error (§5.6, §6). Write the whole thing: what qualifies it as Sev-1 and who can declare it (§6.1); who commands and who decides (§6.2); where the single source of truth lives and who writes to it (§6.3); the first-hour checklist minute by minute (§6.4); the exact first message to an affected dealer, in the language he actually reads, with no adjectives and no speculation; the reconciliation and closing-balance statement the CA will sign; the escalation card with real phone numbers for the CS, the CA, counsel and the CERT-In reporting path if the incident turns out to have a security dimension; and the first-day and first-week sequence (§6.5). Then have both founders read it cold and mark every sentence that would need a decision at 4 PM on a Tuesday — each of those is a gap. **Artefact:** one complete playbook inside [[C30-pre-mortem-crisis-playbook-and-succession|C30]], cross-linked to the pre-written statements in [[C24-crisis-comms-and-statement-kit|C24]], printed and pinned. **Success test:** a person who is not the CEO could run the first hour from the page alone. That is the only test that matters, because the CEO may be on a highway between districts when it fires.

**12.4 — Write the "what would make us stop" note, seal it and date it (45 min alone, then 30 min with all three).** Alone first: draft §10.9's block. Three conditions, each falsifiable, each readable from a source that already exists, each with a stated observation period — not a feeling, not a rupee figure with no date attached. Then compute the **exit reserve** from §10.6's table with the CA's help and write the number into the note and into the `Runway & Scenarios` tab. Choose the expected route from §10.5 in advance and check it once with the CS. Bring it to the other two, argue the conditions until all three agree them, sign it, date it, and each founder keeps a copy. Then log it in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] as what it is: a one-way-door decision made in advance, in the calm, by people who were not under pressure. **Artefact:** a signed, dated `what-would-make-us-stop.md` in the vault, three copies, a computed exit-reserve figure on the dashboard, and a C29 entry. **Success test:** each condition can be answered _yes / no / not yet_ by one person in under ten minutes using only sources that exist today. If any condition needs a debate to evaluate, it is a feeling wearing a number and it goes back for rewriting.

**12.5 — The family instruments: ground rules, the tiebreaker, and the warning-signs card (40 min, all three, at the office, not at home).** Write §9.3's six crisis ground rules on one page in plain language — these sit alongside, not instead of, the eight everyday rules in [[11-culture-and-values-as-a-ceo-instrument|lesson 11]] §8.3. Then do the part everyone postpones: **name the outside tiebreaker.** One human being, by name, chosen for whose "no" all three of you could absorb without losing face; write their remit (which matters go to them), how they are briefed (one shared written brief agreed by all three), and the agreement in advance that their recommendation is either executed or refused **in the minutes with reasons**. Send them the ask this week. Finally, each founder writes their own §8.5 warning-signs card — the three or four specific, observable signals that mean _this person is not okay_ — and hands a copy to the other two, with the ground rule that any founder may raise any signal at any time without it being an attack. **Artefact:** `family-crisis-ground-rules.md` signed by all three, a named tiebreaker with a written remit and a sent message, and three warning-signs cards exchanged. **Success test:** the tiebreaker's name was agreed in under fifteen minutes. If it takes longer, the disagreement you just uncovered is the most important finding of the whole lesson, and it goes to the top of the next founders' agenda.
