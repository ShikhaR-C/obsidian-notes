# 12 — Communication: The CEO as Chief Storyteller

_Phase 3 · The Inward Job · Months 2–9. After this lesson you can treat communication as the mechanism that converts your decisions into other people's actions rather than as a soft skill bolted onto the job, state honestly what the evidence for the "repeat it seven times" rule actually is and where it comes from, map VSYST's five audiences onto one story told at five resolutions and say what inconsistency costs, write and defend a full company narrative plus its one-sentence, one-paragraph and elevator derivatives, run a CEO communication cadence that sits beside the COO's meeting rhythm without duplicating it, deliver bad news internally in the shape that costs least, write memos instead of decks and record the reasoning behind a decision so it survives six months, send a monthly stakeholder update to people who never asked for one, write a price-increase letter and an incident note in Indian business English that a dealer will actually read, decide what may live on WhatsApp and what must be a signed letter, publish in public without giving away the things that must never be published, and run each of the four crises this company can actually have from a first statement you have already written._

## Explain-it-like-I'm-5

Stand on platform one at Raipur Junction at six in the morning. A train is late. There is exactly one fact in the building — _Gondwana Express, two hours late, will arrive on platform four instead of platform one_ — and there are perhaps nine hundred people who need it.

Watch what the announcer does. He says it in Hindi. He says it again in English. He says it a third time in Chhattisgarhi to the group of families sitting on their bedrolls near the tea stall, because that is who is sitting there. The same fact goes onto the electronic board in short form, onto a chalk slate near the foot-overbridge in shorter form, and into the mouth of the man selling tea, who will be asked about it forty times in the next hour and will repeat whatever he heard. When the platform change is confirmed, the announcer says it every eight minutes until the train is in, and he never once changes the wording, because a passenger who hears "platform four" and then "platform four or five" will spend the next hour asking strangers instead of walking to platform four.

Now imagine that the announcer decides announcing is beneath him — that he made the decision about platform four, wrote it in the register, and it is now everyone else's job to find out. The fact still exists. The train still arrives on four. And nine hundred people are standing on platform one.

That is what a CEO's communication is. You are not decorating a decision. You are the only reason it exists anywhere outside your own head. The decision to move the train to platform four and the act of saying "platform four" nine times in three languages are not two jobs — they are one job, and the second half is the half that actually moves people.

## 1. Communication is the job, not a soft skill next to it

Write this on the wall next to the rule from [[05-strategy-i-diagnosis-and-the-strategy-kernel|lesson 05]]:

> **A decision that nobody can act on did not happen. The CEO's output is not decisions; it is other people's behaviour. Communication is the entire conversion mechanism between the two.**

Fred Wilson's reduction of the job — set the vision and strategy **and communicate it**, recruit and retain the very best talent, make sure there is always enough cash in the bank — hides the point inside the first item, and founders read past it every time ([AVC — What A CEO Does](https://avc.com/2010/08/what-a-ceo-does/), 2010). The word "and" is doing enormous work there. Wilson is not saying a CEO sets strategy and, as a secondary chore, tells people about it. He is saying the strategy is not set until it has been communicated, because an uncommunicated strategy has changed nobody's Tuesday.

This is easiest to see by asking what a CEO physically produces in a week. Not code — the technical CEO at VSYST does ship code, and [[13-product-and-technology-leadership-for-a-technical-ceo|lesson 13]] deals with what that costs. Not invoices. What a CEO produces is a set of sentences that cause other people to do specific things: a director to stop building a module, a dealer to sign, a partner to open a door, an advisor to make an introduction, a future employee to accept less money than a Bengaluru offer. If the sentences do not land, none of those things happen, and the quality of your judgment was irrelevant.

### 1.1 The repetition rule — what the evidence actually says

Every founder has been told that people need to hear something **seven times** before they believe it. The number is repeated with total confidence, usually without a source. Here is the honest position, because a course that repeats folk numbers uncritically is training you to be fooled by them.

**Where the seven comes from.** It comes from advertising, not from organisational research, and it is about 140 years old. The "Rule of Seven" descends from a much-quoted 19th-century sequence attributed to the London advertising man Thomas Smith, which walks through what a prospect thinks on each successive exposure to an advertisement — including "the seventh time, they start to get a little irritated with it" and "the seventeenth time, they make a note to buy the product" ([Wikipedia — Effective frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Effective_frequency)). That is a copywriter's aphorism about **purchase behaviour in response to paid media**, not a finding about whether your ops associate has internalised the quarter's priority. The academic literature that grew out of it does not converge on seven either: Herbert Krugman's widely adopted "Why Three Exposures May Be Enough" argues there are only three psychologically distinct exposures — curiosity, recognition, decision — and that the fourth and fifth are just repeats of the third, while the field as a whole is described in the same source as "quite controversial", with different studies producing different numbers (same source). So the folk rule imports a contested advertising number into a completely different domain and then states it as though it were physics.

**What the practitioner literature actually claims.** Patrick Lencioni's _The Advantage_ is the usual source people half-remember, and the publisher's own description of the book is instructive: once a leadership team has clarity, "they need to communicate the answers to employees **over and over again**" — repetition is asserted as necessary, and **no number is given** ([The Table Group — The Advantage](https://www.tablegroup.com/product/the-advantage/)). 37signals' internal communication guide is sharper and more useful, because it separates two cases: "If you have to repeat yourself, you weren't clear enough the first time. However, if you're talking about something brand new, you may have to repeat yourself for years before you're heard. Pick your repeats wisely" ([37signals — The 37signals Guide to Internal Communication](https://37signals.com/how-we-communicate)). That distinction is the whole craft. Repeating a badly-written message seven times produces seven bad messages.

**Why repetition works at all, mechanically.** Three reasons, and each suggests a different fix than "say it again":

| Mechanism                        | What is actually happening                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        | The fix that is not repetition                                                                       |
| -------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Curse of knowledge**           | You have held this idea for weeks; the listener has held it for nine seconds. Newton's 1990 Stanford tapping study is the canonical demonstration — tappers of well-known tunes wildly overestimated how many listeners would recognise them ([Wikipedia — Curse of knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curse_of_knowledge)) | Say the concrete thing, not the abstraction. "Only Raipur district until thirty pumps" beats "focus" |
| **Attention, not comprehension** | People understood you; they were reading a dealer's WhatsApp at the time. Web-reading research has shown for decades that people scan rather than read ([Nielsen Norman Group — How Users Read on the Web](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/))                                                          | Front-load. The decision in the first line, the reasoning after it                                   |
| **Channel reach**                | The people who read long documents and the people who absorb things in a room are different people, and neither set is complete                                                                                                                                                                                                   | Same message, different media, deliberately — not the same medium, more times                        |

**The house call for VSYST.** Do not quote "seven times" to anyone, because you would be quoting an advertising slogan as though it were research. Use this instead, and say it in the words below:

> **Say it until you are bored of it, then say it twice more — in at least two different media — and then check that it came back to you.** The instrument is not the count. The instrument is the _say-it-back test_: at the end of the month, ask two people, separately, what the company's one priority is this quarter. If the two answers differ, the message did not land, and the count is irrelevant.

The say-it-back test is the only honest measurement available at VSYST's size, and it costs two minutes. Gallup's long-running Q12 work puts "I know what is expected of me at work" as the very first item in its engagement instrument, ahead of everything about growth, recognition and purpose — the most basic clarity item is the foundation the rest sits on ([Gallup — Q12 Meta-Analysis](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/321032/employee-engagement-meta-analysis-brief.aspx)). At three people you do not need a survey to find out whether that item is satisfied. You need to ask.

## 2. The five audiences and the one story

A CEO talks to five audiences. Not more. Everyone you will ever communicate with as CEO of VSYST maps to one of these, and the mapping tells you the cadence, the channel and the risk.

| Audience                 | Who it is at VSYST                                                                                 | What they need from you                                                                         | What they fear                                                                             | Cadence                                                     | Channel                                            | Who else speaks to them                                              |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Team**                 | The two other directors today; the first Support/Ops Associate, field contractor and engineer next | To know the one priority, whether we are winning, and that nothing is being hidden              | That the company runs out of money and they find out last; that their work does not matter | Weekly note · monthly all-hands · quarterly strategy update | Written note, then a room                          | The COO, constantly. The family, at dinner                           |
| **Customers**            | Dealers (the paying tenant) and, indirectly, their B2B customers who ride free                     | That the software will be there tomorrow, that their money is correct, and that a human answers | Their ledger being wrong; their data reaching a competitor or the taxman; being locked in  | On change, on incident, monthly release note                | WhatsApp first, email for record, letter for money | Support (the COO's queue), the domain-expert director, other dealers |
| **Investors / advisors** | Today: the CA, a mentor, the CS, an industry advisor. | **Partners**             | IOCL and other OMCs; Easebuzz; ERPNext implementer; app stores                                     | Reliability, compliance posture, a named person accountable                                     | Reputational contamination; a small vendor failing loudly on their platform                | On milestone, on incident, plus a quarterly touch           | Formal email, signed letters, meetings             | Their own internal teams — who will form a view of you without you   |

### 2.1 The one-story rule

> **The same story goes to all five audiences. Only the resolution changes.**

Resolution, not content. A photograph shown at 4K and at thumbnail size is the same photograph; a photograph replaced by a different photograph is a lie. The five resolutions of VSYST's story look like this:

| Audience           | Resolution                                                 | What is added or dropped                                                                         |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Public             | One sentence                                               | Everything except the category and the who                                                       |
| Customer           | One paragraph + one number about _his_ business            | Market size, technology, funding — a dealer does not care and it makes you sound like a salesman |
| Team               | The full narrative + this quarter's crux                   | The reasoning, the risks, the things that could kill us                                          |
| Partner            | Full narrative + compliance, reliability and scale posture | Anything speculative; partners hear speculation as risk                                          |
| Investor / advisor | Full narrative + the numbers + what went wrong             | Nothing. This is the highest-resolution version and the only one with the misses in it           |

### 2.2 What inconsistency costs

Founders do not usually tell five different stories on purpose. They drift, because each audience rewards a slightly different emphasis and there is no written original to drift from. Then the drift is discovered by the one person who talks to two audiences.

| Inconsistency                                                                                              | How it is discovered                                                      | What it costs                                                                                           |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Team hears "we are pre-revenue and careful"; a dealer hears "we have hundreds of customers"                | A dealer's son looks you up, or an employee is in the room during a pitch | Your team stops believing anything you say to outsiders — and then stops believing what you say to them |
| Investor update says activation slipped; the all-hands says everything is on track                         | The first person who sees both                                            | You have just taught the team that the honest version is the one told to outsiders                      |
| IOCL is told the product is enterprise-ready; the roadmap says the reconciliation module is six months out | The first technical review meeting                                        | The partnership, and possibly the category. OMCs talk to each other                                     |
| A LinkedIn post claims a feature that is in the backlog                                                    | A dealer asks for it in a demo                                            | The demo, plus your ability to demo again                                                               |

The mechanism that prevents all four is boring and cheap: **one written narrative, dated, in the vault, that every other version is derived from.** Not a deck. A document. When you change the story, you change that document and note what changed and why — which is a [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] entry, because a narrative change is a strategy change wearing different clothes.

## 3. The company narrative

### 3.1 What a narrative is, and what it is not

A company narrative is not a mission statement, a tagline, an elevator pitch or an "about us" page. Those are outputs. The narrative is the **argument** that makes all of them derivable: a claim about how the world has changed, the problem that change creates for a specific person, why the moment is now, what you do about it, why you specifically, and where it ends up.

Three sources converge on roughly this shape. Sequoia's long-standing business-plan guidance opens with the _company purpose_ in a single declarative sentence, then the _problem_, then _why now_ as a first-class section rather than an afterthought ([Sequoia — Writing a Business Plan](https://www.sequoiacap.com/article/writing-a-business-plan/)). Nancy Duarte's analysis of great talks — including King's and Jobs's — finds a repeated oscillation between _what is_ and _what could be_, ending in what she calls the new bliss, with the call to action carried by that gap rather than by exhortation ([TEDxEast — Nancy Duarte: The secret structure of great talks](https://www.ted.com/talks/nancy_duarte_the_secret_structure_of_great_talks), 2011). And April Dunford's positioning work insists that the frame you choose — the category the buyer places you in — determines every subsequent judgment about you, which is why "what we do" cannot come first ([April Dunford](https://www.aprildunford.com/)).

Six beats, in this order, always:

1. **The world changed.** A fact about the outside, not about you.
2. **That change created a problem for a named person.** Named. Not "SMEs".
3. **Why now.** Why this is solvable in 2026 and was not in 2016.
4. **What we do.** One loop, in his vocabulary.
5. **Why us.** Something structural, not enthusiasm.
6. **Where this goes.** The thing that becomes true if we win.

### 3.2 The VSYST narrative — full draft

What follows is a **draft to be argued with**, not scripture. Read it aloud once; anything you cannot say aloud without embarrassment is wrong. Then take it to the domain-expert director and change at least three things, because the second beat has to be true in his experience or it is not true at all.

> **India's fuel retail has been digitised from the top down, and the dealer got the mandates without the tools.**
>
> Over the last decade the oil marketing companies have wired the forecourt. Automation on the dispensers, number-plate recognition at the gate, digital payment on every pump, e-invoicing and GST returns above it, and a Territory Manager who arrives with a checklist that grows every year. All of it was built so that the OMC and the tax system could see the pump clearly. Almost none of it was built so the **dealer** could see his own business clearly.
>
> **So the man who owns the pump still runs a fifteen-to-twenty-crore-a-year business on paper.** His money is not in the fuel he sells across the counter; it is in the fuel he sells on credit. Thirty to fifty per cent of his volume goes out on _udhaar_ to transport fleets, factories, hospitals and contractors, which means at any moment several tens of lakhs of his money is sitting inside somebody else's truck. That exposure is tracked in a register, a spreadsheet his nephew maintains, a Tally file the accountant opens once a month, and a WhatsApp thread where a fleet manager sends an order at 11 p.m. and a rate is agreed in a voice note. Rates move nightly. Invoices split three ways between product, reimbursement and GST. TCS starts applying past a turnover threshold he must not miss. And when a customer disputes a number four weeks later, the only evidence is a page in a register and two people's memory. Nobody in this chain is stupid, and every part of it works a little — which is precisely why nothing has replaced it.
>
> **Now is the first moment this is fixable.** The attendant has a smartphone and uses UPI. The dealer's son is thirty-four, has an engineering degree, and is already the de facto CTO of the pump. GST and e-invoicing have made structured data compulsory rather than optional, so the discipline is arriving anyway. Cloud infrastructure costs a rounding error compared to what a district's worth of pumps used to cost to serve. The pieces did not exist together in 2016. They do now.
>
> **DZZLO closes one loop, end to end, on a phone.** The dealer confirms tomorrow's rates in the overnight window. His customer places an order against those rates. The dispatch goes out and is released with an OTP the driver reads out at delivery, so a delivery is a fact rather than a claim. The invoice generates in the three tiers the business actually uses — product, cash reimbursement, GST — with TCS added automatically once the threshold applies. The voucher, the payment and the reconciliation close the ledger the same day. One loop. Not a general-purpose ERP, not an accounting package: the specific loop that Indian fuel distribution runs on, and nothing else.
>
> **The dealer pays; his customers ride free.** Every transport fleet, factory and contractor that buys from him gets an account on his tenant at no cost — because the ledger is only worth anything if both sides of it are looking at the same screen. That single decision is why the product spreads inside a district instead of sitting on one pump.
>
> **Why us.** Our office is inside a working petrol pump. One of our directors has spent three decades in fuel distribution and can tell you, without opening a file, why a particular fleet gets thirty days and the one next to it gets fifteen. Our CEO writes the code. There is no translation layer between a dealer saying _"hisaab nahi milta"_ on a Tuesday and that complaint being someone's Wednesday. A larger company can copy any screen we build. It cannot copy the fact that we are already sitting on the forecourt at six in the morning.
>
> **Where this goes.** If we are right, the ledger between a fuel dealer and his credit customers stops being paper and becomes a shared, verifiable record — first in one district, then across the state. That record is the thing everything else needs: the dealer's own working-capital decisions, the lender who has never been able to underwrite him properly, the OMC that has never seen past its own tank. We are not building a billing app. We are building the ledger that Indian fuel distribution does not have.

That is roughly 640 words. It is deliberately long, because it is the source document — the four short versions below are compressions of it, and you cannot compress something that does not exist.

### 3.3 The derived versions

**One sentence (public, LinkedIn bio, a stranger at a wedding):**

> DZZLO is the order-to-ledger system for Indian fuel distribution — the dealer pays, his credit customers use it free, and both sides finally see the same number.

**One paragraph (a dealer, a partner's email, an association introduction):**

> Petrol pump dealers, lubricant distributors and bulk-diesel operators run most of their business on credit, and almost all of that credit is tracked on paper. DZZLO puts the whole loop on a phone — rate confirmation overnight, orders against the confirmed rate, dispatch released with a driver OTP, GST-correct invoices in the three tiers the business actually uses, and the voucher and reconciliation closing the same day. Your customers get accounts free, so the ledger both of you are arguing about becomes one screen you both look at. We are based in Raipur, our office is inside a working pump, and we sell district by district.

**Elevator, thirty seconds (an OMC officer in a corridor, an investor at a demo day):**

> A petrol pump dealer sells thirty to fifty per cent of his volume on credit — tens of lakhs of his money sitting inside somebody else's truck, tracked on paper. DZZLO is a mobile order-management system for that: rates, orders, OTP-verified dispatch, three-tier GST invoicing, and the ledger closing the same day. The dealer pays per GSTIN; his customers get it free, which is how it spreads. We're three people in Raipur, live on both app stores, selling one district at a time.

**The WhatsApp-forward version (twenty words, because this is the version that actually travels):**

> DZZLO — pump ka pura hisaab mobile pe. Order, delivery OTP, GST bill, ledger — dono taraf same screen.

### 3.4 Testing a narrative

Four tests, in order of severity:

1. **The say-it-back test.** Tell it to the domain-expert director. Two days later, ask him to tell it to you. What survives is the narrative; what he forgot was decoration.
2. **The dealer test.** Read beat two aloud to an actual dealer and stop. If he does not interrupt within thirty seconds to correct you or add to it, beat two is wrong. Indifference is the failure signal, not disagreement.
3. **The subtraction test.** Remove "fuel distribution" and "petrol pump" and see whether the narrative still describes a hundred other companies. If it does, you have written a category, not a company.
4. **The enemy test.** Does the narrative name something you are _against_? "Digitised from the top down, and the dealer got the mandates without the tools" is an enemy. A narrative with no enemy has no tension, and tension is what makes it repeatable.

File the narrative in the vault, dated, and treat every change as a versioned event. The messaging derivatives — the words used on the website, in the app store listing, on a WhatsApp forward — belong in [[C08-positioning-and-messaging-canvas|C08]]. The investor-facing compression belongs in [[C15-investor-narrative-and-deck-outline|C15]]. This lesson owns the source; those templates own the outputs.

## 4. Internal communication — the CEO's cadence

### 4.1 The split from the COO's rhythm

[[08-the-operating-cadence|COO lesson 08]] owns the calendar: what meets weekly, what meets monthly, what meets quarterly, and the templates for each. Do not rebuild any of it. The CEO's internal communication is a **thin layer of meaning laid on top of that calendar**, and it consists of exactly four things:

| CEO artefact                      | Cadence                                                   | Length                     | Medium                                                             | Why it exists                                                                 |
| --------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **The weekly note**               | Every week, same day, same time                           | 300–600 words              | Written, one channel, archived                                     | So that "what matters this week and why" exists outside your head             |
| **The all-hands opening**         | Monthly, first 5 minutes of [[T24-all-hands-agenda\|T24]] | 5 minutes                  | Spoken, in a room                                                  | So direction is heard in a body, with faces, and can be questioned            |
| **The quarterly strategy update** | Quarterly, before the COO's planning half-day             | 1,000–1,500 words + 30 min | Written, then discussed                                            | So the plan that follows is downstream of a stated diagnosis, not of momentum |
| **Bad news**                      | Immediately, always                                       | As short as honesty allows | Room first if it affects one person; written same day for everyone | Because the alternative is that they hear it from someone else                |

Everything else — status, blockers, ticket queues, sprint scope — is the COO's, and a CEO who writes about those is doing the COO's job badly while not doing his own. Fill [[C22-internal-comms-calendar-and-all-hands|C22]] with these four and their dates, and then hold the dates.

### 4.2 The weekly written note

This is the single highest-leverage habit in this lesson, and it costs about thirty-five minutes on a Monday morning.

```
VSYST — week of <date>                           from <CEO>

ONE THING           the single most important thing this week, and why it is
                    that and not the other candidate

WHAT CHANGED        outside: a dealer conversation, an OMC signal, a competitor,
                    a rule change. inside: a decision made, a decision reversed

THE NUMBERS         3–5 lines, straight from the scorecard, no commentary
                    beyond one line on anything off-track

DECIDED             what was decided this week and — in one line each — why.
                    anything one-way-door gets a C29 link

NOT DOING           what we chose not to do, and what would make us reconsider

ASK                 what I need from each of you by name, with a date
```

Five rules about it, each of which someone will try to talk you out of:

1. **Same day, same time, without exception.** A weekly artefact without a fixed slot becomes fortnightly and then stops. If there is nothing to say in a week, write the four lines that say so — a quiet week stated is information.
2. **Written first, always.** "Speaking only helps who's in the room, writing helps everyone. This includes people who couldn't make it, or future employees who join years from now" ([37signals](https://37signals.com/how-we-communicate)). At three people the "who couldn't make it" audience is small; the "future employees" audience is the whole point.
3. **The `NOT DOING` block is not optional.** It is the block that carries strategy, because strategy is refusal ([[05-strategy-i-diagnosis-and-the-strategy-kernel|lesson 05]]). It is also the block that stops the other two directors from quietly building the thing you decided against.
4. **Numbers come from the scorecard, never from memory.** If a number matters enough to appear weekly, it is a row in the COO's [[T05-kpi-scorecard|T05]]. Two sources of numbers is worse than none.
5. **One channel, and it is not chat.** "Writing solidifies, chat dissolves. Substantial decisions start and end with an exchange of complete thoughts, not one-line-at-a-time jousts" ([37signals](https://37signals.com/how-we-communicate)). At VSYST that means the note goes into the vault and a link goes into WhatsApp — never the note itself into WhatsApp, where it will be scrolled past by 9 a.m. and unfindable by Thursday.

### 4.3 The monthly all-hands, and the CEO's five minutes

The COO runs it, and [[T24-all-hands-agenda|T24]] is the agenda — including its honest note that at VSYST's size the forty-five-minute version is theatre and the twenty-minute version is the real one. The CEO opens it, and the opening is not a warm-up. It is the highest-attention ninety seconds of the month, and it should contain exactly three things:

- **The one-sentence direction**, word for word the same as last month unless it changed.
- **What changed this month in the outside world** — one dealer story, one market fact, one thing about IOCL or the district.
- **What that means for the quarter's priority** — reaffirmed or revised, and if revised, why, out loud.

Do not use the five minutes for good news; the wins slot handles that. Do not use it for gratitude; the recognition slot handles that. Use it for direction, because you are the only person in the room who can supply it.

The first real all-hands at VSYST will be four or five people standing in the office inside the family's pump, and it will feel absurd. Start it anyway, the month the first person outside the three directors joins, because a transparency ritual retrofitted at fifteen people reads as a response to a problem, and one started at four reads as how the company works.

### 4.4 Written versus said in a room

| Put it in writing                                | Say it in a room (then write it)                                                                        |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Any decision, and its reasoning                  | Anything that affects one specific person's role, pay, or standing                                      |
| Numbers, targets, dates, thresholds              | Bad news with a personal dimension: a departure, a mistake with a name attached                         |
| Anything the fourth employee will need in a year | A disagreement between founders that has heat in it                                                     |
| Policies, limits, authorities                    | The first time you say something genuinely new — writing lands better after a room has already heard it |
| Anything you might be asked to prove later       | Anything you are still deciding and want argued with                                                    |
| Praise, so it is durable and specific            | Correction, so it is private and reversible                                                             |

The asymmetry is the rule: **praise in writing, correct in person; decide in a room, record in writing.** Nothing that affects a person's own work should ever reach them for the first time in a group setting — that is the no-surprises rule, and it runs in both directions.

### 4.5 The no-surprises rule, both ways

Downward: nobody learns news about their own work, their own team or their own company in a meeting for the first time. Upward, and this half gets forgotten: **you must make it survivable to bring you bad news early.** The way you react the first time someone tells you something broke sets the price of honesty for the life of the company. If the first reaction is heat, you have just bought yourself a company in which problems arrive late — and problems that arrive late arrive as crises, which is §9.

Two mechanisms, both cheap:

- **The standing question.** Every 1:1 and every weekly note ends with "what do I not know that I should?" Asked eleven times it gets a shrug; the twelfth time it gets the thing.
- **Blameless by default.** Google's SRE practice makes the point that a postmortem which assigns blame stops producing information — "you cannot 'fix' people, but you can fix systems and processes" ([Google SRE Book — Postmortem Culture](https://sre.google/sre-book/postmortem-culture/)). The COO owns the postmortem template ([[T17-incident-postmortem|T17]]); the CEO owns whether the room is safe enough for it to be filled in honestly.

### 4.6 Delivering bad news internally

Runway shortened. A partnership died. A dealer churned. A release broke money. A person is leaving. The shape is always the same and there are only three ways to get it wrong: **slow, third-person, or without a plan.**

```
1. FAST         Same day. Before the rumour, before the gossip, before the
                dinner table. Speed is the single largest variable in how it lands.

2. FIRST-PERSON "I decided", "I was wrong about", "I missed this."
                Never "it was decided", "mistakes were made", "the team".

3. THE FACTS    What happened, in numbers, without softening. If you do not
                know something yet, say that you do not know and when you will.

4. THE COST     What it means for us specifically. Runway, plan, this quarter's
                priority. Say the uncomfortable number.

5. THE PLAN     What we are doing, who owns each part, by when. Even a partial
                plan beats "we're figuring it out."

6. THE ASK      What you need from them, and what you are NOT asking for.

7. THE DOOR     "Ask me anything now, or come to me alone afterwards."
                Then actually be available for the next two hours.
```

Ben Horowitz's essay on the founder's struggle is the honest companion reading here — the point being that the struggle is normal, is not a sign the company is broken, and is survived by telling the truth rather than by managing perception ([a16z — The Struggle](https://a16z.com/the-struggle/)). His related piece on "lead bullets" is the other half: there is usually no clever manoeuvre, and the communication that works is the one that names the hard work and starts it ([a16z — Lead Bullets](https://a16z.com/lead-bullets/)).

One VSYST-specific hazard. This is a **family-founded** company, which means bad news has an informal channel that outruns every formal one. A director's spouse can know before the Support/Ops Associate does. There is no mechanism that closes that gap entirely; there is one that reduces it, and it is speed. Say it same day, to everyone, in the same words, and the informal channel carries a rumour that has already been confirmed rather than a rumour that has not.

## 5. Writing as the CEO's primary instrument

### 5.1 Memos over decks

Amazon's practice is the strongest available statement of the case, and it is worth quoting exactly rather than paraphrasing: "We don't do PowerPoint (or any other slide-oriented) presentations at Amazon. Instead, we write narratively structured six-page memos." The 2017 shareholder letter goes on to say what most people miss — that the memos are "written and re-written, shared with colleagues who are asked to improve the work, set aside for a couple of days, and then edited again with a fresh mind," and that they "simply can't be done in a day or two"; a great one might take a week or more ([Amazon — 2017 Letter to Shareholders](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2017-letter-to-shareholders)). The following year's letter continues the same theme of writing and standards as the mechanism of quality ([Amazon — 2018 Letter to Shareholders](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2018-letter-to-shareholders)). The fuller account of how the six-pager and the silent-reading meeting actually work in practice — including that the meeting opens with everyone reading in silence for twenty minutes before anyone speaks — is set out by two long-serving Amazon executives in _Working Backwards_ ([Working Backwards — Colin Bryar and Bill Carr](https://www.workingbackwards.com/)).

Why the memo beats the deck, mechanically:

| Deck                                                                                                             | Memo                                                                                     |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Bullets hide the logic. "Expand to Durg" is a bullet; whether it survives contact with the argument is invisible | Prose forces the connective tissue. You cannot write "therefore" without having a reason |
| Requires the author present to be understood                                                                     | Stands alone. Readable in six months by someone who was not there                        |
| The presenter controls pace; the room cannot re-read                                                             | Every reader controls their own pace and can go back                                     |
| Rewards performance                                                                                              | Rewards thinking                                                                         |
| Ambiguity survives                                                                                               | Ambiguity is visible on the page                                                         |

### 5.2 How a three-person company gets 80% of the benefit

You are not Amazon and you do not need six pages. The transferable core is three things, and none of them requires headcount:

1. **Write the thing before the meeting, not after.** One page — occasionally two — circulated the evening before. At VSYST there are three readers; the cost of authorship is the whole cost.
2. **Open the meeting with silence.** Ten minutes, phones down, everyone reads. This is the part founders skip and it is the part that does the work: it removes the "I didn't get a chance to read it" tax entirely, and it means the discussion starts at the level of the argument rather than at the level of the summary.
3. **Write the decision _and its reasoning_, at the bottom of the same document, the same day.** Not "approved". Something like: _"Decided: no second district before thirty active tenants in Raipur. Reasoning: density is the only scale economy we found (lesson 06 §3); two half-districts produce neither. Reconsider if: an OMC or association opens a door in another district that expires."_

That last item is the whole reason this lesson links to [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]]. A decision recorded without reasoning is an instruction; a decision recorded with reasoning is a **reusable judgment**. Six months later the question is never "what did we decide" — you will remember that. The question is "were the conditions we assumed still true," and only the reasoning answers it. Log the conditions and you have built the only defence against a company that changes direction because it forgot why it chose the last one.

### 5.3 The document types a CEO writes

| Type                                    | Length            | Trigger                                  | Lives in                                                   | Read by                    |
| --------------------------------------- | ----------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Weekly note                             | 300–600 words     | Every week                               | Vault, `notes/weekly/`                                     | Team                       |
| One-pager for a decision                | 400–900 words     | Anything reversible but expensive        | Vault + [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log\|C29]] | Directors                  |
| Strategy memo                           | 1,000–2,000 words | Quarterly, or when the diagnosis changes | Vault + [[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager\|C04]]             | Directors, later the board |
| Monthly stakeholder update              | 600–900 words     | Monthly, fixed date                      | Vault, `updates/YYYY-MM.md`                                | Advisors, CA, mentor       |
| Dealer letter (price, incident, policy) | 200–400 words     | On the event                             | Vault + sent as PDF/email                                  | Dealers                    |
| Partner note (IOCL, Easebuzz)           | 300–600 words     | On milestone or incident                 | Vault + formal email                                       | Partner                    |
| Public post                             | 200–800 words     | Deliberate, low frequency                | LinkedIn + vault copy                                      | Anyone                     |

### 5.4 The vault is the company's memory — treat it as an asset

This vault, published through Quartz, is already the company's memory: the strategy research, the pricing work, the COO's operating system, the correspondence with IOCL, this course. Two disciplines make it an asset rather than a pile:

- **Write the reasoning, not just the conclusion.** A page that says "we chose per-GSTIN billing" is a fact. A page that says why, what the alternatives were, what would make us change, and what we were uncertain about is a decision you can audit. The app-store-economics research in this vault does the second thing, which is why it survived a year.
- **Write plainly.** Government digital-service style guidance is unfashionably good on this: short sentences, front-loaded meaning, no jargon, and the recognition that plain language is not dumbing down but is what lets a busy reader act ([GOV.UK — Writing for GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/content-design/writing-for-gov-uk)). A dealer, a CA, an OMC officer and a future employee are all busy readers.

GitLab, which runs a very large company almost entirely on a public written handbook, states the principle as handbook-first: if it is not written down, it does not exist, and the handbook is the single source of truth rather than a summary of one ([GitLab Handbook — Communication](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/communication/)). At three people that sounds like overkill. At three people it is the cheapest it will ever be to start.

## 6. The investor and stakeholder update

VSYST has no investors. It has three founder-directors, a CA, a CS, and a small number of people whose opinion of you is worth something. The instruction in this section is therefore counter-intuitive and it is not negotiable:

> **Write a monthly stakeholder update starting this month, before anyone has asked for one, and send it to people who did not invest.**

### 6.1 Why a company with no investors writes one anyway

Four separate reasons, and the fourth is the one that actually pays.

| Reason                                           | What it does                                                                                                            | Why it matters at VSYST specifically                                                                                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **It is a forcing function on the numbers**      | You cannot write "active tenants: 14" on the 5th of every month unless somebody closed the books and counted by the 4th | Forces the COO's month-end close and [[T20-budget-vs-actual-and-cash-forecast\|T20]] to actually happen on a date, rather than when someone remembers |
| **It builds a track record before you need one** | Twelve dated documents in which you predicted something and then reported what happened                                 | This is the single most credible artefact a first-time founder can hand an investor, and it cannot be manufactured retroactively                      |
| **It converts weak ties into a bench**           | An advisor who reads you monthly is warm; an advisor you call once a year is cold and knows it                          | You have no board and no investors. Your only governance pressure is the people you voluntarily report to                                             |
| **It is a monthly conversation with yourself**   | Writing "what went wrong" forces you to name the thing you have been avoiding for three weeks                           | This is the reason. The audience is a device for making yourself honest. If the list were empty, you would still write it                             |

Mark Suster's argument for "continuous boards" — frequent, low-ceremony contact with investors between formal meetings so that nobody is surprised and everyone has had a chance to course-correct — is written for funded companies, but the mechanism it describes is availability of your thinking to people who can improve it, and that does not require a cap table ([Both Sides of the Table — How To Communicate with your Investors between Board Meetings](https://medium.com/both-sides-of-the-table/how-to-communicate-with-your-investors-between-board-meetings-4835e4b86614)). Elad Gil makes the complementary point from the CEO's side: managing a board is mostly the work you do _outside_ the board meeting, and the meeting itself should contain no surprises ([High Growth Handbook — The role of the CEO: managing your board of directors](https://growth.eladgil.com/book/cofounders/the-role-of-the-ceo-managing-your-board-of-directors/)). At VSYST there is no board beyond the three directors ([[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties|lesson 09]]), which means there is no external forcing function at all — so you build one out of paper and a mailing list.

### 6.2 Who is on the list today

Start with six to ten names. Not thirty. A list you are embarrassed to write honestly to is the wrong list.

| Recipient                                                                   | Why they are on it                                                                                                  | What they will actually do with it                                    | Risk of including them                                                                                                  |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **The two other founder-directors**                                         | They are the board. The update is the minute-book's honest cousin                                                   | Argue with the diagnosis. Good                                        | None. If you cannot send it to them, it is dishonest                                                                    |
| **The CA**                                                                  | Sees the money, signs the returns, will be the first to notice a number that cannot be true                         | Correct your definitions. Occasionally save you from a filing miss    | None                                                                                                                    |
| **The CS**                                                                  | Governance, ROC, cap-table hygiene                                                                                  | Flag anything that needs a resolution before it needs a rectification | None                                                                                                                    |
| **One industry mentor / senior dealer or distributor**                      | The only person on the list who can tell you the market is moving before the market moves                           | Make one introduction a quarter if you ask well                       | He may talk. Assume everything you write reaches a competitor eventually — and write nothing you would not survive that |
| **One or two operator-founders** (someone who has sold Indian SMB software) | Pattern-matching on GTM, pricing, churn                                                                             | Tell you which of your problems is normal                             | Low                                                                                                                     |
| **Prospective angels — a `bcc` you begin 6–12 months before any raise**     | The raise is a decision about a relationship that already exists ([[08-capital-runway-and-fundraising\|lesson 08]]) | Watch. That is the point                                              | **Real** — see §6.7 on Section 42                                                                                       |

Two disciplines about the list. **Send individually or via bcc, never a visible group** — a recipient who can see the other names starts performing for them, and so do you. And **never add someone silently**: "I'm going to start sending you a monthly note on the company; tell me if you'd rather not get it" takes one line and converts a surprise into a small commitment.

### 6.3 The anatomy of the update

Six hundred to nine hundred words. One screen on a phone plus a scroll. Plain text in the body of the email — **not a PDF attachment**, because a PDF is a decision to open something later, and later does not arrive.

```
VSYST — <Month YYYY>                                    sent <fixed date>, from <CEO>

1. THE ONE LINE     What happened this month, in one sentence, including the
                    bad half. "Two new paying GSTINs, and we lost the Bhilai
                    pilot on support response time."

2. THE NUMBERS      6–10 rows. Same rows every month, same definitions,
                    forever. This month · last month · target. No prose.

3. THE ASK          1–3 asks. Each named to a person if possible, each with a
                    date, each small enough to say yes to in one reply.

4. WHAT WENT WRONG  Compulsory. Never empty. What broke, what I got wrong,
                    what I still do not understand.

5. WHAT WENT RIGHT  Shorter than 4. Specific: a named dealer, a shipped thing.

6. WHAT I'M DOING   The next 30 days in 3–5 lines. What we are NOT doing.
   ABOUT IT

7. THE RECAP        The narrative paragraph. Context, market, why any of the
                    above matters. This is at the bottom on purpose.
```

### 6.4 Where the ask goes — the argument

This is the only genuinely contested question in the format, so decide it deliberately rather than by habit.

| Position                                   | The case for it                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  | The case against                                                                                          |
| ------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Ask at the top, right after metrics**    | Aaron Harris of Y Combinator's advice is exactly this — put metrics first, asks immediately after, and push the qualitative recap to the _bottom_, so that a busy reader who abandons the email halfway has still seen the two things that could produce action ([Visible — Tips from YC: Using Asks, Metrics, and a Recap to Power Your Investor Updates](https://visible.vc/blog/tips-from-yc-using-asks-metrics-and-a-recap-to-power-your-investor-updates/)) | Asking before you have reported feels transactional, especially to an unpaid advisor who owes you nothing |
| **Ask at the bottom, as a call to action** | It reads as earned. The classic sales structure: context, then request                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           | It is the block most reliably not reached. On a phone, block 7 is where reading dies                      |
| **Ask in the subject line**                | Guarantees it is seen                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            | Turns a relationship into a channel. Burns fast                                                           |

**The house call for VSYST: block 3, near the top, exactly as YC advises — with one modification.** Because your readers are advisors rather than investors, and advisors have no economic reason to help you, each ask must be _named and small_. Not "intros to fleet operators welcome." Instead: _"Ramesh — you know the Durg transporters' group secretary. Would you forward the one-paragraph description below to him before the 20th? I'll take it from there."_ That is a thirty-second yes. A generic ask is a zero-second no, and it teaches your list that your asks are noise.

One more rule, and it is the one that keeps the list alive: **report back on last month's ask.** If someone made an introduction in June, the July update says what came of it — including "nothing, and here is why." People help twice as often when they can see what happened the first time.

### 6.5 The metrics block at N = small

At VSYST's size, most SaaS metrics are arithmetic on numbers too small to carry meaning. [[17-the-numbers-a-ceo-watches|Lesson 17]] owns this problem in full; here is only the reporting rule. **Report counts, not rates, until the denominator is above about thirty.** "Churn 6.7%" on fifteen tenants means one dealer left, and one dealer leaving is a story with a name in it, not a percentage.

| Row                                        | Definition to fix now and never change                                                         | Why it is on a CEO's update rather than the COO's scorecard                                                                                    |
| ------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Paying GSTINs**                          | Distinct GSTINs with an active paid subscription on the last day of the month                  | This is the unit that money attaches to ([[06-strategy-ii-moats-positioning-and-the-business-model\|lesson 06]]); count it, do not estimate it |
| **Active tenants**                         | Dealer companies with ≥1 order created in the last 30 days                                     | Distinguishes sold from used. The gap between these two rows is the churn you have not booked yet                                              |
| **New / lost this month**                  | Names, not numbers, if fewer than five                                                         | A named loss forces the "why" sentence                                                                                                         |
| **Orders and dispatches**                  | Count, last 30 days                                                                            | The product loop's heartbeat                                                                                                                   |
| **Weekly active users on the tenant side** | Distinct users opening the app in a 7-day window, averaged                                     | The dealer's staff, not the dealer. Adoption lives here                                                                                        |
| **Cash in bank**                           | Bank balance on the last day, in ₹                                                             | Wilson's third job. The only row nobody may round                                                                                              |
| **Net burn**                               | Cash out minus cash in, for the month                                                          | Pair with the next row or it means nothing                                                                                                     |
| **Runway**                                 | Cash ÷ trailing 3-month average net burn, in months                                            | State the arithmetic in the row so nobody has to trust it ([[C13-runway-burn-and-scenario-planner\|C13]])                                      |
| **Pipeline**                               | Dealers in a named stage, count and stage ([[C10-founder-led-sales-pipeline-and-script\|C10]]) | Because at pre-revenue the pipeline _is_ the forecast                                                                                          |
| **One quality row**                        | Pick one: uptime in the 22:00–06:00 window, or ledger disputes opened                          | Says out loud that you measure whether the money is right, not only whether it moves                                                           |

Three rules on the block: **same rows, same order, same definitions every month** — a changed definition is a lie by default, so if you must change one, show both the old and the new number for three months. **Numbers come from one system**, the COO's [[T05-kpi-scorecard|T05]], never from memory. And **do not add a metric to make a month look better.** The moment you add "total registered users" in a month when paying GSTINs fell, every future reader discounts everything.

### 6.6 The "what went wrong" section is compulsory

If block 4 is ever empty, one of two things is true, and both are bad: nothing was attempted this month, or you are managing the reader. Write it as a standing block with a heading, so its absence is visible.

| Bad month, reported badly                                             | The same month, reported well                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| "We faced some challenges in onboarding and are working through them" | "We onboarded four pumps in July and two of them stopped using the app inside three weeks. Both cases were the same thing: the dealer's manager kept a parallel register because he did not trust the ledger until it matched Tally. I do not yet know whether that is a product gap or a training gap. I am spending two days at one of the two pumps this month to find out, and I will tell you which it was in the August note." |
| Softens, and teaches the reader to discount you                       | Names the loss, states the count, admits the unknown, gives a date, and promises the follow-up                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       |

There is a reason to be blunter about this than good manners suggests. Ben Horowitz's central claim about the struggle is that founders lose credibility not by having problems but by pretending they do not, and that telling the truth about a bad situation is the only move that keeps the people around you useful ([a16z — The Struggle](https://a16z.com/the-struggle/)). The asymmetry is brutal and worth memorising: **a bad number you reported is a data point; the same number discovered by someone else is a character assessment.**

The corollary, for the day this list contains real investors: **the update is where surprises go to die.** A number that will be bad at the next board meeting must appear in the update before the meeting, in your own words, with your own explanation. That is the entire mechanism behind the no-surprises rule in §4.5, moved outward.

### 6.7 One Indian legal caution before you press send

The moment your bcc list contains people who might invest, the update stops being pure narrative and starts having a legal shadow. Under **Section 42 of the Companies Act, 2013**, a private placement is an offer of securities or an invitation to subscribe made to a select group of identified persons, made through a private placement offer letter, with conditions attached — including that offers go only to persons whose names were recorded _before_ the invitation, that they be made to no more than the prescribed number of persons, and that the company must not "release any public advertisements or utilise any media, marketing or distribution channels or agents to inform the public at large about such an offer" ([Section 42, Companies Act 2013 — Indian Kanoon](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/91454896/)). **VERIFY LIVE** with your CS, because the numeric limits and the associated rules under the Companies (Prospectus and Allotment of Securities) Rules are amended regularly.

The practical translation for a monthly update is simple and you should follow it even though the risk today is small:

- **Report; do not offer.** "We are at eleven months of runway and expect to raise a seed round in Q3" is reporting. "We are opening a ₹2 crore round at a ₹15 crore cap, minimum ticket ₹10 lakh, closing 30 September" is an offer with terms, and it does not belong in an email to a bcc list.
- **Never post the update publicly** — not on LinkedIn, not on the Quartz site. A public financial update that includes fundraising language is precisely the "public at large" problem.
- **Keep the list recorded and dated.** You should be able to say who received which update and when. That record costs nothing now and is unanswerable later.
- **Fundraising conversations happen one-to-one**, off the update, with the CS in the loop and the paperwork in [[C14-fundraise-readiness-and-data-room-index|C14]] and [[C16-term-sheet-decoder-and-cap-table|C16]].

### 6.8 What twelve of them are worth

Nothing, for about four months. Then something changes that is hard to explain to anyone who has not done it: you acquire a **memory with dates on it**, and it starts arguing with you.

- **In a raise.** The deck ([[C15-investor-narrative-and-deck-outline|C15]]) says what you believe. Twelve dated updates say whether you were right last time you believed something. An investor's core underwriting question about a first-time founder is not "is the market big" — it is "does this person see reality accurately and say so out loud." A year of updates answers that question in a way no meeting can, and it is the cheapest diligence asset you will ever build. Index them in [[C14-fundraise-readiness-and-data-room-index|C14]] from month one.
- **In your own planning.** Read the last six updates before writing a quarterly plan ([[C28-annual-plan-and-ceo-okr-sheet|C28]]). You will find that you have written "onboarding friction" in four of them and done nothing structural about it. That discovery is worth more than the quarter's brainstorming.
- **In a bad quarter.** The update you least want to send is the one that keeps the list. People forgive a bad month. They do not forgive going quiet, because going quiet is what founders do just before something dies, and everyone on your list knows it.
- **When someone joins.** Update number nine is the best onboarding document in the company for a senior hire, because it shows a real month rather than a pitch.

**Where it lives.** [[C23-stakeholder-and-investor-update|C23]] is the template, the recipient list and the fixed send date. It is a **CEO** artefact and it goes out in your voice, unedited by anyone. Its operational sibling in the COO course, [[T23-board-and-investor-update|T23]], owns the _pack_ — which numbers, from which system, closed by which date, in which order. The rule to hold: **T23 supplies the numbers; C23 supplies the judgment about them.** If you find yourself writing T23's content into C23, you are doing the COO's job; if you find yourself letting T23's format dictate what you are willing to say, you have lost the update.

## 7. Customer-facing communication — the CEO's own voice with dealers

At a hundred tenants, the CEO does not write to customers. At fourteen, he does, and pretending otherwise is a mistake that costs more than the time it saves. A dealer who has heard from the founder once will forgive an outage; a dealer who has only ever heard from a support queue will treat you as a vendor and price you like one.

### 7.1 What the CEO writes and what the queue writes

The COO owns customer operations — the support SOP, the response targets, the macros, the escalation ladder ([[10-customer-operations-support-and-success|COO 10]], [[T25-customer-support-sop|T25]]). Do not touch any of it. The CEO writes four things, and only four.

| Communication                                                          | CEO or queue?                                                               | Why                                                                                                                                                                     | Frequency at VSYST today       |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Ticket replies, how-to answers, password resets, "where is my invoice" | **Queue**                                                                   | Volume work with a documented answer. A CEO doing this is a bottleneck pretending to be attentive ([[16-decisions-delegation-and-not-being-the-bottleneck\|lesson 16]]) | Daily. Not yours               |
| **The onboarding note to a new dealer**                                | **CEO**, personally, for the first ~50 tenants                              | The one moment a dealer is deciding whether this is a company or a website                                                                                              | 2–5 a month                    |
| **The monthly release note**                                           | **CEO** writes, queue distributes                                           | It is a statement about direction wearing a changelog's clothes                                                                                                         | Monthly                        |
| **The price-change letter**                                            | **CEO**, signed, always, forever                                            | Money. Never delegated, at any size                                                                                                                                     | Rare, and each one is an event |
| **The incident note**                                                  | **CEO** if money, data or the overnight window was touched; queue otherwise | See §9 for the escalation test                                                                                                                                          | Hopefully rare                 |
| Renewal chasing, dunning, payment reminders                            | **Queue**, on a schedule                                                    | If the CEO chases payment, every future negotiation starts from a weaker place                                                                                          | Monthly                        |

The pattern underneath: **the CEO writes when the message is about the relationship or about money, and stays out when the message is about the product's operation.** Everything in the middle belongs to the COO's queue, and the fastest way to break a support function you have just built is to reach past it because you happened to see the message first.

### 7.2 The four letters

**The onboarding note.** Two hundred words, sent the day the tenant goes live, from your own address, signed with your name and mobile number. It says three things: welcome and what we just switched on for you; the one number that will tell you within two weeks whether this is working (for a fuel dealer: _how many of your credit customers placed at least one order themselves_); and a direct line for anything that involves a rupee. Do not attach a manual. Do not describe features. The note's entire job is to make the next problem reachable, because the dealer who tells you his ledger looks wrong is the dealer you keep.

**The release note.** Monthly, short, in the dealer's language of outcomes rather than yours of modules. The rule is one line per change with the _consequence_ first: not "added bulk voucher entry", but "you can now settle ten customers' payments in one screen instead of ten — saves about twenty minutes at day-end." End with one line naming what is coming next month, and say the same thing you would say to an investor, because a dealer's son will read both. Never put an unshipped feature in a release note with a date attached — see §8.3.

**The price-change letter.** §7.5, worked.

**The incident note.** §7.6, worked, for the routine case. §9 handles the case where it is a crisis.

### 7.3 The WhatsApp reality of Indian B2B

Pretending that Indian fuel distribution runs on email is a category error. Orders arrive at 11 p.m. as voice notes. Rates are agreed in a thread. A dealer's manager will send you a photograph of a register page rather than open a laptop. WhatsApp Business exists precisely because of this pattern of behaviour, and it ships the small tools for it — a business profile, a catalogue, labels for organising conversations, quick replies and away messages ([WhatsApp Business — Business App](https://whatsappbusiness.com/products/business-app/)). Your product's own competitor is not another OMS. It is a WhatsApp thread that already works a little.

So the answer is not "move dealers to email." It is a boundary you hold without apologising for it:

| Belongs on WhatsApp                                                                             | Must be email                                             | Must be a signed letter (PDF, on letterhead, sent by email and, for a few, by post)                |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "Are you free at 4?" · directions · a screenshot of an error · a voice note explaining a screen | Anything with a number in it that either side may rely on | **Any change to price, or to the commercial terms**                                                |
| A link to the release note                                                                      | The release note itself, and the monthly ledger summary   | Any **termination, suspension or data-deletion** notice                                            |
| A nudge that a ticket has been answered                                                         | The ticket thread and its resolution                      | Any **incident that touched money or personal data** ([[C24-crisis-comms-and-statement-kit\|C24]]) |
| Confirming that a payment was received _after_ the receipt was emailed                          | The receipt, the invoice, the credit note                 | Anything a **CA, an auditor, a lawyer or an OMC** may one day ask to see                           |
| Field coordination with a dealer's staff                                                        | Anything involving a third party's data                   | Anything you would be uncomfortable reading aloud in a dispute                                     |

The one-line version to give your team: **WhatsApp is where the conversation happens. It is not where the record lives.**

### 7.4 The record-keeping problem WhatsApp creates — and the fix

Four distinct problems, of which only the first is obvious.

1. **It is on a personal phone.** The thread belongs to a person, not the company. When that person leaves — a field contractor, an ops associate, a co-founder — the company's entire history with those dealers walks out with them, unsearchable and unrecoverable. A company that runs on personal WhatsApp does not own its customer relationships; individuals do.
2. **It is not a record you can produce.** Electronic records in India are now governed by **Section 63 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023**, which replaced Section 65B of the Indian Evidence Act. Section 63(1) makes an electronic record admissible "without further proof or production of the original", but sub-section (4) requires a certificate submitted along with the record, identifying the record and the device, addressing the conditions in sub-section (2), and signed both by the person in charge and by an expert ([Section 63, Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam 2023 — Indian Kanoon](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/125020475/)). **VERIFY LIVE** with a lawyer before you rely on any of this in an actual dispute, including the current position on hash values in the prescribed certificate form. The point for you as CEO is not the procedure; it is the consequence: **a screenshot is not evidence, and the effort of turning a WhatsApp thread into something a court will look at is far larger than the effort of writing one email at the time.**
3. **It leaks personal data.** A ledger screenshot showing a transport fleet's outstanding balance, forwarded into a group, is somebody else's financial information travelling on an uncontrolled channel. Under the DPDP framework you are the data fiduciary for what your dealers' customers put in your product; §9.2 deals with what a breach obliges you to do. The cheap discipline is a rule your team can actually follow: **no ledger screenshots, ever. Send a link to the screen inside the app.**
4. **It has no memory you can query.** In six months, "what did we promise that dealer about the reconciliation module?" has exactly one answer and it lives in a thread nobody can find.

The fix is one mechanism, and it takes ninety seconds each time:

> **The confirm-back rule.** Anything agreed on WhatsApp that involves money, a date, a commitment or a third party's data is followed, the same day, by one email or one ticket that begins _"As discussed on WhatsApp today —"_ and states the thing in three lines. Send it to the dealer. File it against the tenant.

Four supports around it, none expensive:

- **Use a company-owned number**, not the CEO's personal one, for anything customer-facing. When a person leaves, the SIM and the account stay. Do this before there are twenty threads on your own phone, because migrating later is painful and visible to customers.
- **One thread per dealer company, never a group** for commercial matters. Groups make it impossible to say who agreed to what, and a dealer's group may contain his customers.
- **Copy the substance into the tenant record.** The COO's [[T25-customer-support-sop|T25]] owns where that is; the CEO's rule is only that it happens for anything with a rupee sign.
- **Never negotiate price on WhatsApp.** Say the sentence out loud and hold it: _"Main aapko aaj hi email pe likh ke bhej deta hoon"_ — I will write it to you in an email today. It reads as professionalism, not evasion.

### 7.5 Worked example — the price-increase letter

The situation, illustrative: VSYST's launch price was ₹1,499 per GSTIN per month, billed annually on the web ([[08-capital-runway-and-fundraising|lesson 08]] and [[C11-pricing-and-packaging-decision-sheet|C11]] own the real number — **VERIFY LIVE** against the pricing sheet before using any figure here). Sixteen months in, the price for new tenants moves to ₹1,999, and existing tenants move to ₹1,799 at their next renewal. Six rules govern the letter, then the letter itself.

1. **Signed by the CEO, on letterhead, as a PDF attached to an email.** Not a WhatsApp message, not an in-app banner alone.
2. **Notice is at least 30 days, and 60 is better.** Never let the first notice of a price change be the invoice.
3. **Existing annual terms are honoured to the end of the term.** Say so explicitly; it is the sentence that keeps the relationship.
4. **State the old price, the new price, and the effective date in the first four lines.** A dealer reads the top of the page and files it.
5. **Give one honest reason and no more than one.** "Costs went up" is honest. Four reasons read as an argument you are having with yourself.
6. **Say what happens if he does nothing, and give him a named person and a date to reply to.** And check the GST consequence: where a price is revised after an invoice has already been issued, Section 34 of the CGST Act, 2017 governs the credit and debit notes that follow, and a debit note "shall include a supplementary invoice" ([Section 34, CGST Act 2017 — CBIC](https://taxinformation.cbic.gov.in/content/html/tax_repository/gst/acts/2017_CGST_act/active/chapter7/section34_v1.00.html)). **VERIFY LIVE** with your CA — for a forward-dated subscription change the usual answer is simply that the new price appears on the next invoice, but confirm it rather than assume it.

```

To,

<Firm Name>, <Location>

We wish to inform you that with effect from 01/06/2027, the subscription charge
```

Two notes on the register. The letter uses conventional Indian commercial English — _"We wish to inform you", "Kindly", "Thanking you, Yours faithfully"_ — deliberately. That form is what a dealer's office files, forwards to its CA, and treats as a document. Sentences stay short inside that frame; formality of address and plainness of language are not in conflict, and GOV.UK's guidance on front-loading meaning and cutting jargon applies inside the courtesies ([GOV.UK — Writing for GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/content-design/writing-for-gov-uk)). Second: **the exit clause in point 4 is the most important paragraph in the letter.** Offering a free, complete data export at the moment you raise a price is what separates a company from a lock-in, and roughly nobody takes it up. Refusing it converts a price rise into a grievance the dealer will repeat at the association meeting.

### 7.6 Worked example — the routine incident note

The situation, illustrative: on a weekday morning, driver-OTP SMS delivery fails through the upstream aggregator for 2 hours 40 minutes. Dispatches cannot be released. No data was lost, no money was misposted, and eleven tenants were affected. This is a service incident, not a crisis — §9 covers the escalation test that decides which one you are in.

Rules: send it the same day, **after** it is fixed but on the day it happened; state the clock in real times, not "a brief period"; name the number affected; do not blame the vendor by name in the customer-facing version even when it is the vendor's fault; and say the one thing you changed so it does not recur. The COO's [[T17-incident-postmortem|T17]] holds the internal analysis; this note is its public face and must not contradict it.

```
Subject: DZZLO — dispatch OTP delivery issue this morning (09:05–11:45), now resolved

Dear Sir,

Between 09:05 and 11:45 today, OTP messages for dispatch release did not
reach drivers' phones. If you tried to release a dispatch in that window,
the app would have shown the OTP as sent, but the driver did not receive it.

What this means for you:
- No order, invoice or ledger entry was affected. No data was lost.
- Dispatches that were held during this period can be released normally now.
- If you released any dispatch manually and want it recorded against the
  correct time, reply to this mail with the dispatch number and we will
  correct the timestamp today.

Eleven dealer accounts were affected, including yours. We are sorry.

What happened: our SMS delivery partner had a failure on the route used for
Chhattisgarh numbers. We detected it at 09:20 and switched to a second route
at 11:45. The gap between detection and switching was ours, not theirs — we
did not have the fallback configured to switch automatically.

What we have changed today: automatic failover to the second SMS route is now
live, and we have added an alert that pages us within five minutes if delivery
falls below normal. We are also adding an in-app fallback so that a dispatch
can be released with an OTP shown on the dealer's screen and read out to the
driver, if SMS fails entirely. That will be live by <date>.

If anything about today's dispatches looks wrong on your ledger, please write
to me directly and I will personally check it.

Thanking you,

<Name>
Director & CEO, VSYST Technologies Private Limited
<mobile> · <email>
```

Read the middle paragraph again: _"The gap between detection and switching was ours, not theirs."_ That sentence costs nothing and buys the only thing worth buying in an incident note. A dealer who is told the vendor failed learns that you will blame someone next time too. A dealer who is told what _you_ failed to do learns that your account of an incident can be believed — which is the asset you will need on the day the incident is much worse.

### 7.7 Where Hindi matters, and where English is the right choice

Chhattisgarh's business language is Hindi; its street language is often Chhattisgarhi; its documentary language is English. All three are correct in different places, and choosing wrongly is a real cost — English at the forecourt reads as arrogance, Hindi on a contract reads as amateurism.

| Communication                                                                   | Language                                              | Script                                                             | Why                                                                                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| WhatsApp field messages, nudges, coordination                                   | **Hindi**                                             | **Roman** (Hinglish) — _"Kal subah rate confirm karna na bhoolen"_ | This is what people actually type and read on phones. Devanagari on WhatsApp reads as a bank's automated message     |
| In-app text a **driver or attendant** sees — OTP screens, errors, confirmations | **Hindi**, alongside English                          | Devanagari                                                         | The driver is not the buyer and did not choose your product. If he cannot read the screen, the OTP loop breaks       |
| Training material, one-page how-tos for pump staff                              | **Hindi**                                             | Devanagari, large type, screenshots                                | It will be printed and stuck near the till                                                                           |
| The WhatsApp-forward version of the pitch (§3.3)                                | **Hindi**                                             | Roman                                                              | It travels between dealers unedited. It must sound like a dealer wrote it                                            |
| Release notes, onboarding note                                                  | **English**, with a two-line Hindi summary at the top | Both                                                               | The dealer's son reads English; the manager reads the Hindi lines                                                    |
| **Price letters, contracts, notices, incident notes**                           | **English**                                           | —                                                                  | These are documents. They get filed, forwarded to a CA, and possibly produced later. Keep one authoritative language |
| Anything to an OMC, a bank, a regulator                                         | **English**                                           | —                                                                  | Their internal record is English                                                                                     |
| A difficult conversation on the phone                                           | **Whatever the dealer opens with**                    | —                                                                  | Match, do not choose                                                                                                 |

Three details that matter more than they should. **Write ₹ amounts in lakhs and crores, not millions** — "₹18.5 lakh" is read instantly; "1.85 million" is read twice. **Use DD/MM/YYYY**, because 03/09/2027 means September in every office in India and March in none. And **use the dealer's honorific**: _Shri_, or the form he uses himself. A letter that opens "Hi Rajesh" to a fifty-eight-year-old dealer with two outlets is not friendly; it is a small failure of attention, and he will read the rest of the letter through it.

## 8. Public and market communication

Start from the honest premise, because every mistake in this section comes from forgetting it: **nobody has heard of VSYST, nobody is looking for it, and nobody is thinking about you at all.** Public communication for a pre-revenue B2B company is not reputation management. It is distribution — the cheapest way to be _findable_ by the eleven people a year who matter, and to be _credible_ when a dealer's son types your name into Google after a demo.

That reframe changes the target. You are not trying to reach an audience. You are trying to survive a search.

### 8.1 The founder's LinkedIn as distribution

A three-person company from Raipur has no brand, no press office and no budget. It has one asset: a named human being who knows something specific that very few people know. That is exactly the asset the B2B evidence says works. Edelman and LinkedIn's long-running research on B2B thought leadership finds that decision-makers say they trust an organisation's thought leadership more than its marketing materials when judging capability, that it makes them substantially more receptive to sales outreach, and — the finding that should reorganise your effort — that the buying group contains "hidden" stakeholders in finance, operations, compliance and procurement who consume this material heavily and whose internal misalignment stalls a large share of deals ([Edelman — 2025 Edelman-LinkedIn B2B Thought Leadership Impact Report](https://www.edelman.com/expertise/Business-Marketing/2025-b2b-thought-leadership-report); [full report PDF](https://www.edelman.com/sites/g/files/aatuss191/files/2025-07/2025%20Edelman-LinkedIn%20B2B%20Thought%20Leadership%20Impact%20Report_FINAL.pdf)). The same research reports that buyers rate most of what they read as poor quality, which is the opening: the bar is genuinely low, and specificity clears it.

Translate that to VSYST. Your hidden buyer is the dealer's son, the accountant, and — one day — an OMC's digital team. What reaches all three is not opinions about SaaS. It is **the mechanics of a business you are inside and they are not.**

| Publish                                                                                | Why it works                                                                                                                                                                  | Do not publish                                                                        | Why not                                                                                                         |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| How a three-tier invoice actually splits, with a worked example and no product mention | Nobody else has written it down. It ranks, and it proves domain, which is your only real moat signal ([[06-strategy-ii-moats-positioning-and-the-business-model\|lesson 06]]) | **Any customer's data**, even anonymised volumes that a local reader can de-anonymise | In a district, "a dealer near Bhilai doing 400 KL" names one man                                                |
| What the overnight rate-confirmation window does to a dealer's working capital         | Useful to the buyer, unusable by a competitor                                                                                                                                 | **Roadmap promises with dates**                                                       | You will miss the date, and a dealer will quote it back to you in a renewal conversation                        |
| A build note: what we got wrong in the dispatch OTP flow and why                       | Engineering credibility, recruiting, and it is honest                                                                                                                         | **Funding rumours, "in talks", valuations**                                           | It is untrue until it is signed, it may breach §6.7, and it makes every future statement discountable           |
| A one-paragraph note when something real ships                                         | Legitimate news, low frequency                                                                                                                                                | **Attacks on named competitors**                                                      | Punching sideways tells the market you have a peer. You do not want one                                         |
| Hiring posts, with the real number and the real location                               | The only recruiting channel a Raipur company has ([[10-building-the-team-hiring-equity-and-firing\|lesson 10]])                                                               | **Anything about an OMC relationship** before it is public and cleared                | The fastest way to end an OMC conversation ([[14-partnerships-ecosystem-and-founder-relationships\|lesson 14]]) |
| A short, dated correction when you were wrong in public                                | Costs nothing, buys the thing §7.6 buys                                                                                                                                       | **Internal disagreements, or anything about a co-founder**                            | §9.5                                                                                                            |

Cadence: **one post a fortnight, permanently, is worth more than eight in a week and then silence.** Write from the personal profile, not the company page — a company page with 40 followers is a signal of smallness; a founder with a specific point of view is a signal of expertise, and the algorithmic reality of the platform favours the person over the page in any case.

The single most useful discipline is a subtraction: **before publishing anything, ask what a competitor could do with it, and what a dealer could quote back to you.** If the answer to the first is "build the feature faster" or the answer to the second is "you promised", do not publish.

### 8.2 Indian tech press — the realistic payoff

The four outlets a founder in India will be pointed at are [Inc42](https://inc42.com/), [YourStory](https://yourstory.com/), [Entrackr](https://entrackr.com/) and [ETtech](https://tech.economictimes.indiatimes.com/). They are real, they are read, and their central subject is **capital events**: funding rounds, acquisitions, financial results, executive moves, policy. Entrackr in particular is built around funding, financials and exclusives on transactions; Inc42 pairs news with a startup-data product; YourStory runs founder-story features; ETtech covers the business of technology inside a mainstream business daily.

Which produces an uncomfortable, useful conclusion:

> **A pre-revenue, bootstrapped, non-consumer company from Raipur has almost nothing that Indian tech press wants, and pursuing coverage before a raise is a bad use of a founder's month.**

| What you might want          | What it actually costs                                         | What it actually returns at VSYST's stage                                                             |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| A funding announcement       | Nothing — the round pays for the coverage                      | High. This is the one moment the press is genuinely useful. Prepare for it in advance, not on the day |
| A "founder story" feature    | Weeks of chasing, or a PR agency's retainer you cannot justify | Low. Reads well to your family. Does not sell a pump in Durg                                          |
| A quote in a sector round-up | An hour, if a journalist already knows you                     | Modest and cumulative — worth doing when asked, never worth chasing                                   |
| Paid "brand story" placement | Real money                                                     | **Negative.** Indian dealers can tell advertorial from journalism, and so can investors               |

The house call: **do not pitch tech press until there is a raise, a real customer number, or a genuinely novel fact about the sector.** Do build the relationship cheaply in the meantime — follow two or three journalists who cover fuel, retail-tech or SME software, respond usefully when they ask the internet a question, and never send a cold pitch that is really a request for validation. When the round closes, you will have three names rather than none, and that is the whole of press strategy at your size.

### 8.3 The channels that actually matter here

Industry and association channels outrank tech press for VSYST by a wide margin, because they are read by the person who signs the cheque.

| Channel                                               | What it is                                                                                                                                                                    | Realistic access today                                           | What to do                                                                                                                                                      |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **District and state dealer associations**            | Petrol-pump dealers organise locally and meet regularly; national bodies such as the All India Petroleum Dealers Association exist above them ([AIPDA](https://x.com/_AIPDA)) | High. Your domain-expert director is already inside this network | Get on one meeting agenda a quarter. Not a pitch — a fifteen-minute talk on something useful, e.g. what TCS at the turnover threshold means for their invoicing |
| **FIPI and the formal industry bodies**               | The Federation of Indian Petroleum Industry is the apex society for the hydrocarbon sector and the government-facing interface ([FIPI](https://www.fipi.org.in/))             | Low today; it is an OMC-and-major-company room                   | Watch it. Relevant when an OMC relationship is real                                                                                                             |
| **Trade publications for fuel retail and lubricants** | Small circulations, read closely by exactly your buyer                                                                                                                        | Medium                                                           | One well-written technical piece a year beats a year of LinkedIn for this audience                                                                              |
| **The OMC's own field network**                       | Territory Managers, area offices, dealer meets                                                                                                                                | Medium via dealers                                               | Never go around a dealer to his TM. Go with him, if he offers                                                                                                   |
| **WhatsApp groups of dealers**                        | Where reputation actually propagates in a district                                                                                                                            | High, and dangerous                                              | You do not control it. What you control is the twenty-word forward version of the story (§3.3) and never giving anyone a reason to forward a complaint          |

**Trade shows.** The large national event is India Energy Week, held under the Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas and organised jointly by FIPI and dmg events; the 2026 edition ran 27–30 January in Goa with several hundred exhibiting companies ([India Energy Week](https://www.indiaenergyweek.com/); [PIB — India Energy Week 2026 press release](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2219127&reg=3&lang=1)) — **VERIFY LIVE** for the current year's dates, venue and cost. Be honest about what that is: an oil-and-gas industry event where a three-person software company's booth would be an expensive way to be ignored.

The house call on shows, for the next two years: **attend one national event without a booth, and speak at three district association meetings.** Walking a large show with a list of twelve people you intend to find is cheap and occasionally decisive. Renting three square metres of it is not. The district meeting, where forty dealers are in one room and your director knows a third of them, is worth more than all of it — and it is the venue where §10's rules about presenting will actually be tested.

## 9. Crisis communication

[[18-risk-crisis-and-the-hard-things|Lesson 18]] owns crisis _management_ — the pre-mortem, the triggers, the decisions, the psychological load. This section owns only the **communication**, and it exists because of one fact: in a crisis you will not write well. You will be frightened, tired and defensive, and every instinct will be wrong. So the sentences must be written now, in a calm room, and stored in [[C24-crisis-comms-and-statement-kit|C24]].

### 9.1 The escalation test — is this an incident or a crisis?

Answer four questions. **Any one yes makes it a crisis, and a crisis means the CEO speaks.**

| Question                                                                                                                                                                              | If yes |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------ |
| Did a number that represents **money** become wrong — an invoice, a ledger, a balance, a tax figure?                                                                                  | Crisis |
| Did **personal or financial data belonging to someone who is not our customer** — a dealer's credit customer, a driver, an employee — become visible to anyone who should not see it? | Crisis |
| Did the **22:00–06:00 rate-confirmation window** fail such that a dealer could not set or confirm tomorrow's rates?                                                                   | Crisis |
| Will a **third party** — a dealer's customer, an OMC, a journalist, a regulator, a lawyer — hear about this from someone other than us?                                               | Crisis |

Everything else is a service incident and belongs to the COO's queue and the §7.6 note. Write these four questions on a card. The point of a pre-agreed test is that the person who applies it at 2 a.m. is not deciding under pressure whether to wake the CEO; they are reading a card.

### 9.2 The clocks you do not control

Two Indian regulatory clocks may start before your own communication does. Neither is a matter of judgment.

| Clock                       | What it requires                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   | Trigger                                                                                                            | Where it bites                                                                                                  |
| --------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **CERT-In, 6 hours**        | The April 2022 directions under s.70B(6) of the IT Act require service providers, intermediaries, data centres and body corporates to report specified cyber incidents to CERT-In **within six hours of noticing or being brought to notice of** the incident ([CERT-In Directions No. 20(3)/2022-CERT-In, 28.04.2022](https://www.cert-in.org.in/PDF/CERT-In_Directions_70B_28.04.2022.pdf))                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      | A reportable cyber-security incident — unauthorised access, data breach, targeted scanning and a long annexed list | Six hours is faster than your investigation. You will be reporting a partial picture. Report anyway             |
| **DPDP, breach intimation** | Section 8(6) of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 requires a Data Fiduciary, in the event of a personal data breach, to give **the Board and each affected Data Principal** intimation in the prescribed form and manner ([Section 8, DPDP Act 2023 — Indian Kanoon](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/186118625/)). The Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025 — notified by MeitY in November 2025 (sources differ on whether the gazette date or the publication date is cited; **VERIFY LIVE**) — prescribe that form and manner, including intimation to the Board without delay followed by a detailed report within 72 hours ([Wikipedia — Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_Personal_Data_Protection_Rules,_2025); [S.S. Rana & Co. — MeitY notifies final DPDP Rules 2025](https://ssrana.in/articles/meity-notifies-final-digital-personal-data-protection-rules-2025/)) | A personal data breach, as defined                                                                                 | Every dealer's credit customer in your database is a Data Principal. You are the Data Fiduciary for all of them |

**VERIFY LIVE, with a lawyer, before you need this:** the DPDP Rules are being brought into force in phases over roughly eighteen months from notification, so the exact obligation that binds VSYST on a given date depends on which provisions have commenced; the CERT-In annexure of reportable incident types is long and has been clarified since 2022; and the notification formats change. The action item is not to memorise the rules. It is to **have the CERT-In reporting email address, your CS's number and a data-protection lawyer's number inside [[C24-crisis-comms-and-statement-kit|C24]] today**, because at hour one of a breach you will not be able to research this.

One consequence worth saying plainly: **the regulatory clock is shorter than the "let's understand it fully first" instinct.** You will be filing an incomplete report to CERT-In while still reading logs. That is what the rule contemplates. Say what you know, say what you do not, and update.

Two structural rules cut across all four. **One voice**: nobody at VSYST speaks publicly during a crisis except the CEO, and that includes a developer explaining the root cause helpfully in a dealer's WhatsApp thread. Write the rule down before you need it. And **one clock**: appoint someone whose only job during an incident is communication, separate from whoever is fixing it — Google's SRE incident-management structure separates the Incident Commander, the Operations Lead and a **Communications Lead** who issues periodic updates to stakeholders precisely so that the person fixing the system is not also the person writing to customers ([Google SRE Book — Managing Incidents](https://sre.google/sre-book/managing-incidents/)). At three people, the CEO is the Communications Lead and must therefore not be the one debugging. That is uncomfortable for a technical CEO and it is the correct allocation.

On tone and cadence, Atlassian's incident-communication guidance is the most practical public write-up of the mechanics — acknowledge fast, update on a predictable rhythm even when there is nothing new, and keep templates written in advance ([Atlassian — Incident communication best practices](https://www.atlassian.com/incident-management/incident-communication)). The rhythm matters more than the content: **a promise to update at a stated time, kept, is what converts panic into waiting.**

### 9.4 The first statement — fill in the blanks

Print this. Put it in [[C24-crisis-comms-and-statement-kit|C24]]. It is deliberately dull, because dull is what survives being quoted back to you.

```
Subject: DZZLO — <plain description of the issue> — status and what we are doing

Dear Sir,

At approximately <HH:MM> on <DD/MM/YYYY>, we identified <one sentence, plain
language, no jargon, no minimising adjective>.

What we know now:
- <fact 1 — specific, verified, with a number or a time>
- <fact 2>
- <what is NOT affected — state this explicitly, only if you have verified it>

What we do not know yet:
- <the honest gap>
We expect to know this by <date/time>.

What this means for your business:
- <the concrete consequence for the dealer, in his terms>
- <what he should do, or explicitly: "no action is needed from you">

What we have done so far:
- <action, with the time it was taken>
- <action>

What happens next:
- We will send the next update by <specific time>, whether or not the
  position has changed.
- <the named person> is available on <number> for anything urgent.

We are sorry this happened. I will write to you again with the full account
once we have it.

<Name>
Director & Chief Executive Officer
VSYST Technologies Private Limited
```

Seven rules for filling it, each of which corresponds to a way founders make crises worse:

1. **Never write "no data was compromised" until you can prove it.** Write "we have not found evidence that X was accessed, and we are still checking." The retraction of an over-confident denial is a bigger event than the original incident.
2. **Give a time for the next update, and keep it even if nothing changed.** "No change since 14:00; next update 20:00" is a message that builds trust.
3. **Do not apologise in the first line.** Apologise in the last. A first line of apology signals that the note is about your feelings; the reader wants to know what happened to his money.
4. **No adjectives.** "Minor", "brief", "isolated" and "limited" are all read as spin, and if any of them later turns out to be wrong, everything you said is re-read as spin.
5. **Name what is _not_ affected, but only what you verified.** For a dealer, "your invoices and your GST filings are not affected" may be the entire content he needs.
6. **Do not blame a vendor, an employee or an attacker.** You may state a technical cause. You may not distribute responsibility.
7. **Sign it with a name and a mobile number.** An unsigned incident note from a company nobody has heard of is indistinguishable from an evasion.

### 9.5 The founder or partner dispute — a different animal

Crises A, B and C are about a system. D is about people, and it follows opposite rules.

- **Say almost nothing, once.** Every additional sentence gives the story somewhere to go. The prepared line is: _"There is a disagreement between the founders which we are resolving. It does not affect our customers or our operations, and we are not commenting further."_ Then do not comment further, including to a friendly journalist, including off the record.
- **Tell customers and partners before they read it.** A short, factual note to your dealers and to any OMC or vendor contact — same day — saying operations are unaffected and naming who they should contact. Silence to a customer is what turns a private dispute into a commercial problem.
- **Never let the internal version differ from the external one.** In a family-founded company the informal channel is faster than any statement you write (§4.6). Assume every internal sentence becomes external.
- **The paperwork is the real answer.** Public communication cannot fix a governance problem. The founders' agreement, vesting, decision rights and the board resolution do ([[C02-founders-agreement-and-vesting-checklist|C02]], [[C03-decision-rights-matrix|C03]], [[03-the-ceo-core-value-and-the-founder-contracts|lesson 03]]). Communication only buys time for those to work.

### 9.6 What makes the follow-up worth more than the first statement

Every crisis has two documents, and founders write only the first. The follow-up — sent days later, when nobody is asking any more — is the one that decides what the event means permanently.

| The follow-up contains                                                                                             | Why                                                                                                                    |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| The **real timeline**, in times, including the parts where you were slow                                           | If you hide the slow part, someone will find it                                                                        |
| The **actual cause**, in language a dealer can read                                                                | A cause you can explain is a cause you understand                                                                      |
| **What specifically changed** — a test, an alert, a limit, a review step                                           | This is the only paragraph that reduces the chance of a repeat, and the only one that a dealer's son will judge you on |
| The **cost to the customer**, and what you did about it — a credit, a corrected statement, a signed reconciliation | Money errors are closed with money and with paper, not with regret                                                     |
| **One sentence of accountability with a first-person subject**                                                     | "We did not have the fallback configured" (§7.6). Not "the configuration was not in place"                             |

Then do the thing almost nobody does: **put the follow-up in the next monthly stakeholder update (§6.6) and, if it is a class of failure other people can learn from, in a public post (§8.1) with all customer detail removed.** A company that publishes its own postmortems is making a statement about its standards that no marketing sentence can imitate — and it is the single most effective recruiting document a small engineering team can produce.

## 10. Presenting: the CEO in a room

### 10.2 Structure: answer first

Use one structure everywhere, and use it because it matches how a busy listener's attention actually decays. Barbara Minto's Pyramid Principle organises ideas "as a pyramid under a single point" — the recommendation at the top, two to four supporting arguments beneath it, evidence at the base — introduced by the SCQ frame: Situation, Complication, Question ([Barbara Minto — The Minto Pyramid Principle](https://www.barbaraminto.com/); a clear teaching summary at [Monash University — Business paper using the Minto approach](https://www.monash.edu/student-academic-success/excel-at-writing/how-to-write/business-paper-using-the-minto-approach)).

Applied to an OMC committee in four sentences: _Situation_ — your dealers run credit sales on paper. _Complication_ — you have digitised the forecourt but not the dealer's ledger, and disputes and working-capital leakage sit outside your visibility. _Question_ — what would it take to close that loop without adding to the dealer's compliance burden? _Answer_ — this, and here is what we would need from you.

The corollary, which founders resist: **put the conclusion in the first ninety seconds.** Nobody in an Indian procurement meeting is waiting for a reveal. They are deciding, in the first two minutes, whether to keep listening or open their phone.

### 10.3 Hostile questions

Four types, and only the fourth is actually dangerous.

| Type                          | Example                                                             | What it really is                                | The response                                                                                                                                                 |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **The test**                  | "What happens to my data if you shut down?"                         | A legitimate risk question, asked bluntly        | Answer it completely and unemotionally. Have a real answer ready: export format, escrow, notice period                                                       |
| **The status move**           | A long statement ending in "…isn't it?"                             | Someone establishing seniority in front of peers | Agree with the true part out loud, correct the false part in one sentence, move on. Never win it                                                             |
| **The trap**                  | "So you're saying our current system is useless?"                   | An invitation to insult the room                 | Refuse the frame, do not repeat the hostile words. "The register works — that's why it's still there. What it can't do is settle a dispute four weeks later" |
| **The one you cannot answer** | "How do you handle multi-state GSTIN consolidation?" and you do not | Reality                                          | **"I don't know. I'll find out and write to you by Thursday."** Then do it by Wednesday                                                                      |

The fourth is dangerous only if you improvise. A founder who invents an answer in an OMC room has created a commitment that a technical reviewer will check, and being caught once ends the relationship. Saying "I don't know" costs a fraction of a second of comfort and buys a follow-up email — which is a second contact you did not have.

Three more disciplines. **Answer the question actually asked, then stop.** The most common failure in a hostile room is answering the question you prepared for, which everyone can hear. **Do not fill the silence after your answer** — the pause is the other side thinking, and founders who talk into it invariably say the sentence they regret. And **rehearse out loud**, on your feet, timed: Chris Anderson's account of how TED prepares speakers is essentially that the structure is decided early and then rehearsed until the delivery is invisible ([HBR — How to Give a Killer Presentation, Chris Anderson](https://hbr.org/2013/06/how-to-give-a-killer-presentation), 2013). For a twelve-minute association talk, three run-throughs aloud is the difference between being listened to and being tolerated.
