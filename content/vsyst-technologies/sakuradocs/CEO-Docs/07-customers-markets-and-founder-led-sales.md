# 07 — Customers, Markets and Founder-Led Sales

_Phase 2 · The Outward Job · Months 1–3. After this lesson you can state why founder-led selling is a strategy instrument rather than a stopgap and name the two gates that end it, describe the Indian fuel dealer as a person with a day, a ledger and a price anchor of ₹0, run a Jobs-to-be-Done switch interview from a written 15-question script and map the four forces from it, hold a discovery conversation that survives the Mom Test and detect a polite Indian "no", score a prospect against a nine-criterion ICP rubric and disqualify without guilt, run the whole motion from sourcing in one district to signed paperwork using a written call script, an eight-minute demo run-of-show and a ten-entry objection bank, structure a paid pilot that produces truth instead of a free customer, and route what every conversation teaches you into the four artefacts that must change because of it._

## Explain-it-like-I'm-5

Imagine a man who has bred a better variety of paddy seed. He takes a sack of it to the weekly haat and sits down between the onion seller and the man selling plastic buckets. Nobody buys. Not because the seed is bad — because a farmer who sows the wrong seed loses a whole year, and no farmer risks a year on a stranger's sack.

So he stops selling at the haat. He walks into the village, finds the one farmer everybody else watches, and asks a question instead of making a pitch: _"last season, that field near the canal — kitna nikla per acre?"_ He listens for twenty minutes. Then he offers one bigha's worth of seed, free, on one condition: he gets to walk the field every Sunday and write down what he sees. Ten weeks later that one bigha is visibly taller than everything around it, and every neighbour has been walking past it twice a day.

That is founder-led sales. The seed is the product. The one bigha is the pilot. The Sunday walks are the discovery interviews. And the man who bred the seed has to do every part of it himself — because he is the only person alive who can tell the difference between _"the seed is wrong"_ and _"the sowing was wrong."_ That distinction is not a sales detail. It is the entire company.

## 1. The rule: the CEO owns the customer relationship until it is a machine

Write this on the wall:

> **The CEO personally owns the customer relationship until selling is a machine that someone else can run. Not until it is comfortable. Not until it is fun. Until it is a machine.**

Most founders treat founder-led sales as an embarrassing phase to escape — a thing you do because you cannot yet afford a salesperson. That framing is wrong and it is expensive. At pre-revenue, **founder-led selling is the primary instrument of strategy**, because it is the only apparatus you own that can falsify the diagnosis you wrote in [[05-strategy-i-diagnosis-and-the-strategy-kernel|lesson 05]]. A strategy kernel says: _this is the crux, this is our guiding policy, these are the coherent actions._ Nothing on that page is a fact. Every line of it is a hypothesis about a human being in a district, and there is exactly one machine for testing it — sitting across a plastic table from that human being with his ledger open.

Paul Graham's essay is the canonical statement of the principle: startups do not take off by themselves, and "the most common unscalable thing founders have to do at the start is to recruit users manually" — the point being not the work but what it teaches you, which automation never could ([Paul Graham — Do Things that Don't Scale](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html), 2013). First Round's version, from Matt Lerner, is blunter about the delegation instinct: founders' three characteristic failures are overthinking, underbuilding and **premature delegation**, and "founders need to be the ones that figure out how their business is going to grow" ([First Round Review — You're Not Ready for a Head of Growth](https://review.firstround.com/founder-led-growth-playbook/)). SaaStr's advice to a founder asking whether to hand sales off at the start is a flat no: you sell the first customers yourself, because that is how you refine the pitch, discover the objections and learn what actually works ([SaaStr — Should a Startup Founder Handle Sales Themself When First Getting Started?](https://www.saastr.com/should-a-startup-founder-handle-sales-when-first-getting-started/)).

### 1.2 The trigger that ends it — argued, not asserted

The standard Western advice comes in two forms, and both need translating before they touch VSYST.

The **count** form: do not hire an AE before you have personally closed roughly 10–20 customers, and hire two reps rather than one so you can tell a rep problem from a process problem ([SaaStr — When Should I Hire My First AE?](https://www.saastr.com/dear-saastr-when-should-i-hire-my-first-ae); [SaaStr — How Do I Hire a Great First 1–2 Sales Reps?](https://www.saastr.com/dear-saastr-how-do-i-hire-a-great-first-1-2-sales-reps)). The **revenue** form: below about \$1M ARR most startups do not generate enough qualified leads to keep a salesperson busy, and a VP Sales belongs after \$1–2M ARR with two reps already at quota ([SaaStr — One Tough (But Rewarding) Job: Being the First Sales Rep at a SaaS Startup](https://www.saastr.com/one-tough-but-rewarding-job-being-the-first-sales-rep-at-a-saas-startup/); [SaaStr — I've bootstrapped to \$3M ARR with founder-led sales, am I ready for a VP Sales?](https://www.saastr.com/dear-saastr-ive-bootstrapped-to-3m-arr-with-founder-led-sales-am-i-ready-to-hire-a-full-time-vp-sales)). First Round's own framing agrees on the direction and warns the other way too: hire a salesperson inside the first five customers and you have not validated the problem or the buyer deeply enough.

**The revenue form does not import.** \$1M ARR is roughly ₹8.7 Cr. At the ticket sizes this segment supports — the [[09_PRODUCT_VS_SALES|product-vs-sales analysis]] puts a realistic pure-SaaS subscription at ₹500–5,000 per outlet per month — ₹8.7 Cr of ARR means somewhere between 1,450 and 14,500 paying outlets. Waiting for that before your first sales hire would keep the CEO of VSYST personally selling until the 2030s. Any Indian SMB-SaaS founder who imports an American ARR gate will either never hire or will conclude the business is broken. It is not broken; the gate is denominated in the wrong currency.

**The count form does import,** because it is denominated in learning, and learning does not care about the exchange rate. So the house call for VSYST is a **two-gate trigger** — a learning gate and a cash gate — and both must be green. §9 does the arithmetic. Until then, the one-line version:

> **Founder-led sales ends when the script is stable and the recurring revenue already on the books can pay a salesperson's full first-year cost before that salesperson sells anything.** Not at an ARR number. At a document plus a bank balance.

## 2. Knowing the customer: the fuel dealer as a human being

You cannot run a switch interview with a segment. You run it with a man who has a name, a father-in-law, a bank overdraft and an opinion about the last software salesman who came to his pump. Everything in this section exists so that when you walk in, you already know roughly whose life you are in.

### 2.1 The modal owner

The oil marketing companies' own eligibility filters shape who exists in this population before you meet anyone: Indian citizen, minimum 10th pass for rural outlets and 12th pass for urban, age band roughly 21 to 55–60, and demonstrated financial capacity, against a total outlet investment commonly reported in the ₹70 lakh to ₹1.5 crore range excluding land ([Franchise India — Petrol pump business plan in India](https://www.franchiseindia.com/insights/en/article/petrol-pump-business-plan-in-india-the-ultimate-guide.56771); [Boss Wallah — petrol pump opening cost](https://bosswallah.ai/blog/startup-guide/petrol-pump-opening-cost/)) — **VERIFY LIVE** on the relevant OMC's current dealership advertisement, because eligibility bands and reservation categories change per tender.

Those filters cascade into a recognisable person, described at length in the [[12_OWNER_ACQUISITION|owner-acquisition research]]: age 45–65, overwhelmingly male, DODO (dealer-owned dealer-operated) so the land under the forecourt is usually his, from a landowning or trading family, **asset-rich and cash-tight**. He is not a "SaaS buyer". He is a small industrialist with a licence, a compliance file and a receivables problem.

The market he sits in is large and countable, which matters when you argue territory in [[C06-market-map-sizing-and-segments|C06]]: India crossed 1,00,266 retail outlets as of end-November, with IOC at 41,664, BPCL at 24,605 and HPCL at 24,418 — the three PSU marketers running over 90% of the network ([Energy Watch — India's petrol pump count crosses 1 lakh](https://www.energywatch.in/oil-and-gas/indias-petrol-pump-count-crosses-1-lakh-nearing-us-china-in-network-scale), Dec 2025; [Petrosoft — Fuel Retail Trends India 2026](https://petrolbunksoftware.com/blog/fuel-retail-trends-india-2026)). State-wise counts, including Chhattisgarh's, are published by PPAC as a downloadable state-wise retail-outlets file — **VERIFY LIVE** at [PPAC — Retail Outlets](https://ppac.gov.in/infrastructure/retail-outlets) before you put a district number in any document; that file was last refreshed 24 August 2026.

### 2.2 The ₹-scale of his business — and why it makes your price feel enormous

Do this arithmetic with him, on his own paper, in front of him. It is the single most effective thing you can do in the first ten minutes, and it is not a sales trick — it is you finding out whether he is your customer.

Dealer commission is the number his whole business turns on. Reported rates put a dealer at roughly **₹3–4 per litre on petrol and ₹2–3 per litre on diesel**, structured as a fixed amount per kilolitre plus a small percentage of base price — over ₹3,100/KL for petrol and over ₹2,300/KL for diesel under the latest revision — after an October 2024 increase of about ₹0.65/litre on petrol and ₹0.44/litre on diesel, the first meaningful revision in years. In Delhi, dealer earnings work out to roughly 3–5% of the pump price ([Business Today — Pump commission rules explained](https://www.businesstoday.in/india/story/pump-commission-rules-explained-how-much-petrol-pump-owners-earn-per-litre-in-india-what-costs-do-they-have-to-manage-and-more-520996-2026-03-18), March 2026; [Business Standard — Petrol pump dealer commission raised](https://www.business-standard.com/companies/news/petrol-pump-dealer-commission-raised-retail-prices-remain-unchanged-124103000842_1.html), Oct 2024; [IndianOil media release](https://www.iocl.com/MediaDetails/45687)). **VERIFY LIVE** against the current PPAC/OMC circular before quoting any per-litre figure to a dealer — he knows it to two decimal places and getting it wrong ends the meeting.

Illustrative, for a mid-sized district pump — **do not present these as facts; derive them live from his numbers**:

| Line                                                                                                      | Illustrative                        | How to get it in the room                |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| Monthly throughput                                                                                        | 150 KL                              | "Mahine mein kitna sale hai — kitne KL?" |
| Topline at ~₹95/L                                                                                         | ≈ ₹1.42 Cr/month, ≈ ₹17 Cr/year     | multiply in front of him                 |
| Blended commission at ~₹3.50/L                                                                            | ≈ ₹5.25 lakh/month, ≈ ₹63 lakh/year | his real "revenue"                       |
| Out of that: 6–12 attendants, electricity, banking/QR charges, maintenance, compliance, licence, interest | the majority of it                  | "isme se kya-kya jaata hai?"             |
| Share of volume sold on B2B credit                                                                        | 30–50%                              | "udhaar pe kitna jaata hai?"             |
| Receivables outstanding at any moment (40% of volume, 30-day terms)                                       | ≈ **₹57 lakh**                      | this is the number that keeps him awake  |

Now hold the two numbers next to each other. A ₹2,000/month subscription is **0.038% of his annual commission** and about **0.35% of one month's receivable exposure**. Arithmetically it is nothing. Psychologically it is enormous, because it is a _new recurring outflow_ in a business where every recurring outflow — an extra attendant, a higher electricity slab, a bank charge — is fought over line by line. Indian SMEs buy "only what's absolutely necessary" ([Evoma — SME sales for startups](https://evoma.com/business-centre/sme-sales-for-startups/)). You are not competing on price. You are competing against the category "recurring expense", and the only way to win that category is to make the software sit next to the ₹57 lakh, not next to the electricity bill.

### 2.4 The family system — you are selling to three people

The highest-leverage persona fact in the [[12_OWNER_ACQUISITION|owner-acquisition research]]: many pumps have a **second-generation, technology-capable family member**, 28–42, engineering or MBA educated, fluent in English and the regional language, who is the de facto CTO of the pump — while the father remains the legal signatory, holds the OMC relationship and has final veto.

| Person                          | What they respond to                                                                                               | What kills the deal with them                                           |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **The father / signatory**      | Trust signals: the dealer next door by name, the CA's nod, the association, a person who physically shows up twice | Anything that smells like a stranger asking for money and data          |
| **The son / second generation** | Data, dashboards, the ledger view, a live demo, "this is what Sharma Transport owes you, right now"                | Being talked down to; a demo that looks like 2009                       |
| **The accountant**              | Tally-compatible output, GST-correct invoices, a statement he can reconcile                                        | Any hint that the software makes his job smaller, or exposes his errors |

The choreography is: **get the son to lean in, get the father to stop objecting, get the accountant to stop blocking.** Three different sentences in one meeting. Write which sentence belongs to whom into your [[C10-founder-led-sales-pipeline-and-script|C10]] script.

### 2.5 His customers' customers — and why their leverage decides your adoption

The dealer's B2B buyers are transport fleets, factories, hospitals, construction contractors and agricultural operations. They buy on 30-, 45- or 60-day credit. They do not pay VSYST anything and they ride free on the dealer's tenant ([[00_OVERVIEW|Product Overview]]).

This is the structural problem the [[11_EDUCATION_GAP|education-gap analysis]] names precisely, in Andrew Chen's vocabulary: the customer is **the hard side** of the network — no incentive to onboard, lower digital literacy, infrequent interaction — while the dealer is the **powered-on side** ([a16z — The Cold Start Problem](https://a16z.com/books/the-cold-start-problem/)). Two consequences you must carry into every sales conversation:

1. **Never sell an outcome that depends on the customer adopting.** The guard-rail is single-player value: DZZLO must pay for itself for the dealer even if zero customers ever join. Demo the ledger and the invoice — which need nobody's cooperation — before you demo anything that needs a transporter to tap a link.
2. **But do price in the dealer's leverage,** because it decides how fast the customer side lights up. A dealer who supplies 60% of a fleet's diesel on credit can say _"confirm tonight's rate here or the morning trip waits"_ and be obeyed. A dealer who is one of five suppliers to that fleet cannot. **Leverage over B2B customers is an ICP criterion**, not a nice-to-have — it appears in §5's rubric as credit concentration.

Christensen's definition is the discipline here: **a job to be done is the progress a person is trying to make in a particular circumstance** — not a demographic, not a feature request ([Clayton M. Christensen, Taddy Hall, Karen Dillon and David S. Duncan — Know Your Customers' "Jobs to Be Done"](https://hbr.org/2016/09/know-your-customers-jobs-to-be-done), HBR, September 2016; [FullStory — Christensen's JTBD framework](https://www.fullstory.com/blog/clayton-christensen-jobs-to-be-done-framework-product-development/)). Notice how many of the six jobs above are emotional or social. If your pitch only addresses row 1, you are competing on features with an Excel file — and Excel is free, familiar, and already open.

## 3. Jobs to be Done, done properly

### 3.1 Christensen's framing, and why it changes what you ask

The milkshake study is the standard illustration: a chain tried to sell more milkshakes with segmentation and focus groups, launched improvements, and moved nothing — until the research shifted to _what job customers were hiring the milkshake for_, which turned out to be a long, boring morning commute and a spare hand ([HBR, 2016](https://hbr.org/2016/09/know-your-customers-jobs-to-be-done)). The operational consequence for you is small and total: **you stop asking about the product and start asking about the circumstance.**

### 3.2 The four forces — Moesta and Spiek

Bob Moesta and Chris Spiek turned Jobs theory into an interviewing method by naming the four forces that act on any switch:

| Force                        | Direction     | In this business it sounds like                                                                                           |
| ---------------------------- | ------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Push of the situation**    | drives change | "Pichhle saal do party ka paisa doob gaya." A rate dispute with the biggest fleet. A GST notice. The old accountant left. |
| **Pull of the new solution** | drives change | "Bete ne dikhaya, phone pe sab dikh raha tha." A dealer he respects showing him his own ledger on a screen.               |
| **Anxiety of the new**       | blocks change | "Data kahan jayega?" "Staff seekh payega?" "Beech mein band ho gaya to?"                                                  |
| **Habit of the present**     | blocks change | The DSR he has written by hand for 22 years. The accountant's Tally. The diary in the drawer.                             |

The equation: **Push + Pull > Anxiety + Habit → the switch happens** ([JTBD.org — The Four Forces of Progress](https://jobstobedone.org/the-four-forces/); [JTBD Radio — Unpacking the Progress Making Forces Diagram](https://jobstobedone.org/radio/unpacking-the-progress-making-forces-diagram/)). Moesta's book-length treatment of selling from the buyer's side is _Demand-Side Sales 101_ — "stop selling and help your customers make progress" ([demandsidesales.com](https://www.demandsidesales.com/)).

The trap this fixes: founders spend all their energy on **Pull** — more features, a better demo, a lower price — because Pull is the only force they control directly. But in this segment, **Anxiety and Habit are almost always the binding constraint**, and neither is affected by a feature. Anxiety is answered with proof and reversibility. Habit is answered by keeping the old thing alive for 30 days, not by mocking it.

### 3.3 The timeline of a switch

Every real switch has six moments, and your job in the interview is to date them:

```
[1] FIRST THOUGHT        something breaks; "iss tarah nahi chalega"      (often months before)
[2] PASSIVE LOOKING      he notices what others use; no action yet
[3] THE EVENT            a deadline, a loss, an inspection, a fight      <- the force multiplier
[4] ACTIVE LOOKING       he asks people; sees two or three options
[5] THE DECISION         a specific room, a specific day, a specific person present
[6] FIRST USE / HABIT    week 1 reality; then what he stopped doing
```

Most founders only ever ask about [5] and [6]. **The money is in [1] and [3].** [1] tells you who is even in the market; [3] tells you what event you should be watching for so you arrive the week it happens. In this business, the recurring [3] events are: a receivable written off, a rate dispute with a large fleet, a Legal Metrology or OMC inspection, the accountant quitting, the second-generation son returning to the business, and the OMC mandating something new.

### 3.4 The switch-interview script

Use this verbatim. It goes into [[C09-icp-and-customer-discovery-guide|C09]] and it is what exercise 11.3 books.

**Who to interview:** a dealer who, in the last 6–12 months, **adopted or refused** any new business system — a billing package, a Tally upgrade, an automation/ANPR vendor, a UPI QR provider, a CCTV system, a fleet-card scheme, even a new accountant. **Not DZZLO.** Ideally not your customer at all. You are studying the physics of switching in this population, not fishing for a lead.

**Setup:** at his pump, not your office. 45–60 minutes. Two of you — one asks, one writes and draws the timeline. Ask permission to record. Hindi or Chhattisgarhi throughout. **Do not describe DZZLO until the last five minutes**, and only if he asks.

```
THE FIRST THOUGHT
 1. Take me back. What was the first moment you thought "ab iss tarah nahi chalega,
    kuch badalna padega"? Which month was it? What had just happened that day?
 2. Before that day — what were you using? Walk me through one full day with it,
    from the morning shift to the night close.
 3. Had you thought about changing before that? What stopped you then?

THE GAP
 4. Between that first thought and actually doing something — how long was it?
    Weeks? Months? What happened in between?
 5. Was there a specific thing that finally made you start looking — a date,
    an inspection, a customer, a number that got too big?

THE SEARCH
 6. Where did you look first? Who did you ask?
 7. What else did you consider? Include "carry on as before", "ask the accountant
    to handle it", "put my son on it" — those count.
 8. Who came to see you? What did they show you, and in what order?

THE FEARS
 9. What worried you? What was the worst thing you imagined going wrong?
10. Who else in the family or at the pump had to agree? What did they say —
    exactly, in their words?
11. What did you have to give up, or stop doing, to make the change?

THE DECISION
12. What was the moment you decided? Where were you sitting, who was with you,
    what was said?
13. What did you pay? How did you decide that number was acceptable —
    what did you compare it against?

AFTERWARDS
14. First week — what actually happened? What surprised you? What did your staff say?
15. Today: what do you use it for that you didn't expect? What have you stopped
    using it for? If it vanished tomorrow morning, what would you do?

    [If he REFUSED:] What would have had to be true for you to say yes?
    And who did say yes, that you know of?
```

**Six rules for running it.** (1) The only follow-up you need most of the time is _"aur phir kya hua?"_ — and then silence. (2) Ask for the day and the month; vagueness is where reconstructed stories live. (3) If he starts giving you opinions or advice, gently return him to the timeline: "us din wapas chalte hain". (4) Write down his exact words, not your paraphrase — the verbatim is the product. (5) Never correct him. (6) If you find yourself explaining, stop; you have left the interview.

The structured 10-step interview arc — set the stage, life before, first thought, the struggle, the search, the choice, first experience, evolution of use, current pains, then map the four forces — is documented, with timings, in June's write-up of Moesta's own method ([June — How to run a JTBD interview like the co-creator of the framework](https://www.june.so/blog/how-to-run-a-jtbd-interview-like-the-co-creator-of-the-framework)); Moesta's own account of what these interviews reveal is worth an hour ([SaaS Club — 10 customer interviews that reveal why people really buy](https://saasclub.io/podcast/jobs-to-be-done-bob-moesta-423/)).

## 4. Discovery interviews that don't lie to you

A switch interview studies the past. A discovery conversation happens with someone who might buy, and it is far more dangerous — because he wants to be nice to you and you want to hear good news.

### 4.1 The Mom Test

Rob Fitzpatrick's rule is that you should be able to ask your own mother about your business and still get useful data — which is only possible if the questions never mention your idea. Three rules ([The Mom Test — Rob Fitzpatrick](https://www.momtestbook.com/); [Unusual Ventures — 5 takeaways from The Mom Test](https://www.unusual.vc/rob-fitzpatricks-mom-test/)):

1. **Talk about their life, not your idea.**
2. **Ask about specifics in the past, not generics or hypotheticals about the future.**
3. **Talk less and listen more.**

And three kinds of bad data to refuse: **compliments, fluff and ideas.** A compliment feels like progress and contains none. Fluff is any sentence in the future or conditional tense. An "idea" is him designing your product for you, which is flattering and worthless — the underlying problem is the signal, the suggested feature is noise ([Koji — The Mom Test for customer interviews](https://www.koji.so/blog/mom-test-customer-interviews-2026)).

### 4.2 Questions to never ask — and the replacement

| Never ask                                     | Why it lies                                         | Ask instead                                                                                                   |
| --------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "Kya aapko ye idea accha lagta hai?"          | Invites a compliment. He will say yes to be polite. | "Pichhli baar rate ka jhagda kab hua tha? Kya hua tha?"                                                       |
| "Kya aap aisa software kharidenge?"           | Hypothetical. Free to say yes.                      | "Abhi credit ka hisaab rakhne ke liye aap kya use karte hain? Kisne set kiya tha? Kitna dete hain uske liye?" |
| "Kitna dene ko tayyar hain?"                  | He has no idea, and his guess anchors you wrongly.  | "Pichhle saal software ya accounting pe kitna kharch hua? Kis-kis ko?"                                        |
| "Kya ye feature useful hoga?"                 | He will design your roadmap.                        | "Aakhri baar aisi problem kab aayi? Us waqt aapne kya kiya?"                                                  |
| "Aapki sabse badi problem kya hai?"           | Produces a rehearsed, abstract answer.              | "Pichhle hafte sabse zyada time kis cheez mein gaya jo nahi jana chahiye tha?"                                |
| "Main aapko demo dikha doon?" (in minute two) | Ends discovery and starts a pitch.                  | "Do minute apni process dikha sakte hain? Jaise aap roz karte hain."                                          |

### 4.3 Detecting a polite Indian "no"

In this culture a direct refusal to a guest is close to rude, and negative feedback is routinely delivered indirectly — the same variation across cultures in how directly criticism is given that Erin Meyer maps in _The Culture Map_ ([erinmeyer.com](https://erinmeyer.com/books/the-culture-map/)). A dealer will therefore almost never say no. He will say something warmer and less useful, and if you do not learn to read it, your pipeline fills with ghosts and your strategy gets confirmed by people who were being kind.

| What he says                                                   | What it usually means                                 | The probe that resolves it                                                                                      |
| -------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| _"Haan haan, bahut accha hai. Zaroor."_                        | Politeness. No commitment has occurred.               | "Accha lagna alag baat hai — agla step kya rakhein? Main Mangalwar 11 baje aa jaun?"                            |
| _"Aap office aa jaiye kabhi."_                                 | A friendly dismissal with no date.                    | "Zaroor — Mangalwar ya Guruwar, kaunsa theek rahega?" A refusal to pick a day _is_ the answer.                  |
| _"Abhi season chal raha hai, thoda baad mein."_                | Not enough push yet, or the wrong person.             | "Season kab tak? Aur us waqt tak ye problem badhegi ya kam hogi?"                                               |
| _"Bete/bhai se poochhna padega."_                              | Either real (the son decides) or an exit.             | "Bilkul — main unko bhi dikha doon? Kab free hote hain? Aap dono ke saath 15 minute."                           |
| _"Bhej dijiye WhatsApp pe, dekh lenge."_                       | Deferral. Sending a PDF ends the conversation.        | "Bhej deta hoon — par 10 minute mein aapke apne ek customer ka ledger bana ke dikhaun? Wahi zyada kaam ka hai." |
| _"Kitna hai? … theek hai, dekhte hain."_                       | Price was never the issue; he is closing the meeting. | "Paisa alag rakhte hain ek minute — agar ye free bhi hota, to aap kal se use karte? Kya rokta?"                 |
| Tea is ordered, the meeting extends warmly, nothing is decided | Hospitality, not interest.                            | Ask for an advance (§4.4) before the second cup.                                                                |
| He gives his number readily but never opens your WhatsApp      | You were a pleasant visitor, not a candidate.         | Stop chasing. Log the loss reason honestly.                                                                     |

The meta-rule: **warmth is not signal.** In this market you will be treated well by almost everyone. Treat that as background radiation and measure something else.

### 4.4 The advance — the only honest output of a meeting

Neil Rackham's research programme — built on observation of tens of thousands of real sales calls and now sold as the SPIN methodology ([Huthwaite International — SPIN Selling](https://www.huthwaiteinternational.com/spin-selling)) — gives the distinction that fixes the previous section. Every call ends in one of four ways: an **order**, an **advance** (a specific action that moves the sale forward, agreed with a date), a **continuation** (it will go on, but nothing was agreed), or a **no-sale**. Founders systematically record continuations as progress. They are not progress; they are the sound a polite market makes.

Fitzpatrick's version of the same idea is that a real signal costs the customer something — **time, reputation or money** ([Unusual Ventures](https://www.unusual.vc/rob-fitzpatricks-mom-test/)). So:

| Advance                                                                                | Currency spent    | Strength                                         |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| A second meeting **with a date** and the son or accountant present                     | Time + reputation | Good                                             |
| An introduction to another named dealer, made by him, in a WhatsApp group or on a call | **Reputation**    | Strongest non-cash signal in this market         |
| Real data handed over — last month's DSR page, the credit-customer list, one statement | Time + risk       | Very strong; it also makes your demo real (§6.3) |
| A refundable deposit or a pilot fee, however small                                     | **Money**         | Strongest                                        |
| "Send me the details"                                                                  | nothing           | Not an advance                                   |

**The rule:** every conversation ends with an advance or with a recorded no. There is no third state. Write the advance into the WhatsApp thread before you leave the forecourt — in writing, in his thread, in his language. The COO's pipeline enforces this structurally: an open deal without a dated next step gets a verdict at the weekly review ([[11-revenue-operations-and-partnerships|COO 11]] §4).

## 5. ICP — who VSYST's best-fit first customer actually is

An **ICP** (ideal customer profile) is not a description of everyone who could use the product. It is a description of the customer who, if you had a hundred of them, would produce a company you actually want. It is a strategic choice, which is why it belongs to the CEO and lives in [[C09-icp-and-customer-discovery-guide|C09]] next to the kernel — not in a marketing deck.

### 5.1 The definition in one paragraph

> **VSYST's first-customer ICP:** a DODO petrol-pump dealer or bulk-diesel operator in or within ~80 km of Raipur, doing meaningful monthly throughput, with **more than five active B2B credit customers** who together account for **at least a quarter of volume**, who has had a real receivable or rate dispute in the last twelve months, whose business already pays for _some_ software (Tally, a billing package) so the category "paid software" is not new, who has **a second-generation family member or a literate manager inside the business**, whose owner will personally sit in the demo, and who ideally holds **more than one GSTIN** across sister firms.

Every clause in that sentence is doing work, and each maps to a row below.

### 5.2 The scoring rubric

Score each prospect out of 100 before the first visit where you can, and correct it after. The tab is `Pipeline` in [vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx](toolkit/vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx); the rubric itself is [[C09-icp-and-customer-discovery-guide|C09]].

**Bands.** ≥70: pursue this week, personally. 45–69: nurture — one useful WhatsApp asset a month, revisit in 90 days. <45: decline politely and log why. Note row 6 carefully, because it is the row founders get backwards. A dealer running on pure paper looks like the biggest opportunity and is usually the hardest sale: he has never bought software, has no budget line for it, and his habit force is at maximum. The dealer who already pays a Tally AMC and still keeps a paper DSR and an Excel credit sheet has _already lost that argument with himself_ — he just has no product that covers orders, rates and credit. That is your customer.

### 5.3 The disqualification list

Saying no early is a CEO skill because founder hours are the company's scarcest capital, and a bad-fit customer does not merely waste them once — he generates support load, feature pressure, a bad reference and a churn statistic. Decline these regardless of score:

The sentence to have ready, so that "no" costs you nothing socially: _"Sir, sach batayein to abhi hamara system aapke jaise pump ke liye utna kaam nahi karega — aapke paas udhaar customer hi nahi hain. Main jhooth bolke aapka paisa nahi lena chahta. Chhe mahine baad wapas aaunga."_ Dealers remember that conversation, and it is one of the cheapest reputation purchases available to you in a district where everyone talks.

### 6.2 First contact and the first call

First contact is a WhatsApp voice note, because this buyer replies to voice notes and ignores email. Thirty to forty seconds, in Hindi, in your own voice.

```

"Namaste [Name] ji. Main [your name], VSYST Technologies, Raipur se.
```

| 4:15–5:15 | **The ask is a visit, not a sale.** "Main aapke pump pe aake das minute mein aapke apne ek customer ka ledger bana ke dikha doon? Mangalwar subah ya Guruwar shaam?"    | Offer to "send details"                              |

Three hard rules for the first call: **no price**, **no PDF before a visit**, **two time choices, never "when suits you"**. ### 6.3 The demo run-of-show

Lead with the ledger because it needs nobody's cooperation; follow with the rate window because it kills the dispute he just described to you on the phone.

**Before you open anything:** ask for one real customer's name and last month's rough numbers, and enter them live. A demo built from his own data is a different species of event from a demo with `Sharma Transport` pre-seeded.

### 6.5 The close and the paperwork

The close in this market is not a signature moment. It is the smallest next commitment that costs him something (§4.4), and then the next one.

The ask, at minute 7 of the demo, in this order of preference:

1. **The pilot:** "Ek mahine chala ke dekhte hain — aapke top paanch udhaar customers ke saath. [Date] se shuru, [date+30] ko baithkar decide karenge." (§7)
2. If not that: **the son/accountant meeting with a date.**
3. If not that: **his top-10 customer list**, so the next demo is fully real.
4. If not that: **an introduction** to one dealer he thinks has the same problem.
5. If none of the four: a recorded no, with the reason in his own words, into the loss log. Not "he's thinking about it".

Paperwork stays deliberately thin and entirely owned by the COO's machinery — the quote from ERPNext with a validity date, the order form with parties and GSTINs, plan, term, price, start date, liability cap, DPDP clauses, termination and data-export terms, and the advance-payment rule; then a five-line handoff note to onboarding ([[11-revenue-operations-and-partnerships|COO 11]] §5). The CEO's only jobs at this stage are: **nothing verbal that is not on the order form**, and **no side deal you would not want the neighbouring dealer to discover**, because in one district he will.

## 7. Pilots and design partners

### 7.1 Three things founders confuse

|               | Free trial                        | **Pilot**                                     | Design partner                                |
| ------------- | --------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Purpose       | Let him look                      | **Test whether the product changes his week** | Co-build a capability that does not exist yet |
| Who it is for | Anyone                            | An ICP-scoring prospect                       | 3–5 dealers, maximum, chosen                  |
| Duration      | Open-ended                        | **30 days, dated**                            | One or two quarters                           |
| Price         | ₹0                                | **Not ₹0**                                    | Discounted, but written down                  |
| Success       | Undefined                         | **Written, numeric, agreed before day 1**     | A shipped capability + a reference            |
| What you get  | Nothing reliable                  | A yes/no you can trust                        | Depth, and a story                            |
| Main risk     | Becomes a permanent free customer | Becomes a free trial by drift                 | Their roadmap becomes your roadmap            |

### 7.2 The five rules of a pilot that produces truth

1. **Charge something.** Even a small, real amount, collected in advance. A pilot that costs nothing tests nothing: the commitment is the instrument. Cialdini's foot-in-the-door effect is the mechanism — once a small commitment exists, follow-through rises sharply ([Cialdini's 7 principles of persuasion](https://www.cognitigence.com/blog/cialdini-7-principles-of-persuasion)) — and the [[12_OWNER_ACQUISITION|owner-acquisition research]] proposes exactly this in the form of a small refundable deposit as a qualifying filter. The amount is a [[C11-pricing-and-packaging-decision-sheet|C11]] decision and VSYST has no published price yet ([[11-revenue-operations-and-partnerships|COO 11]] §7) — so quote it deal by deal and log every quote.
2. **Time-box it to 30 days, with both dates written on day 0** — the start date and the decision date. The decision meeting is scheduled _before_ the pilot begins. Pilots that end by drifting never convert; they just stop.
3. **Define success in writing, in his numbers, before day 1.** Not "we'll see how it goes." Something like: _by day 30, my top five credit customers' balances are correct in DZZLO without me checking the diary; rates are set here every night; and I have raised at least 20 invoices from it._ Note how closely this tracks the COO's activation definition — ≥1 customer linked and ≥1 invoice inside 14 days ([[10-customer-operations-support-and-success|COO 10]] §7). Keep them consistent: **the pilot's success bar is the activation bar plus habit.**
4. **Name the owner on his side.** A person, not "the pump". If the only name he can give is his own and he is never at the counter, the pilot will fail and you will not know why.
5. **Write the honest exit.** "Agar 30 din baad aapko lage ki fayda nahi hua, hum band kar denge, data export karke de denge, aur main aapse ek ghanta lunga ye samajhne ke liye ki kya galat tha." An exit you offer out loud is the single strongest answer to the anxiety force in §3.2 — and the exit interview is worth more to you than the pilot fee.

### 7.3 The pilot one-pager

One page, in Hindi and English, signed by both. Not a contract — the order form does the legal work ([[11-revenue-operations-and-partnerships|COO 11]] §5). This is the truth-producing instrument.

```
DZZLO PILOT — [Dealer name], [Pump], [District]

Start date: ____________         Decision meeting: ____________ (start + 30 days)
Pilot fee:  ₹__________ (paid in advance, adjusted against the first month if we continue)

WHO
  Owner / decision-maker: ______________________
  Day-to-day operator at the pump: ______________________  (name, phone)
  Accountant: ______________________

SCOPE — what we are testing
  Customers in scope (top 5 by outstanding): 1______ 2______ 3______ 4______ 5______
  In scope:      rates · orders · dispatch · invoices · ledger · statements
  NOT in scope:  __________________________________________

SUCCESS — agreed before we start (tick on day 30, together)
  [ ] Balances for the 5 customers are correct in DZZLO without checking the diary
  [ ] Rates set in DZZLO on at least 25 of 30 nights
  [ ] At least ___ invoices raised from DZZLO
  [ ] Owner or operator says: "agar ye kal band ho jaye to dikkat hogi"

WHAT VSYST DOES
  [ ] Enter the opening balances and the top-10 customer list ourselves
  [ ] On-site on day 0 and day 1; a call on day 3, day 7, day 14
  [ ] One named person on WhatsApp for the whole 30 days

WHAT THE DEALER DOES
  [ ] Gives 45 minutes on day 0 and day 1
  [ ] Sets rates in DZZLO nightly
  [ ] Tells us honestly when it is not working, the same week

IF IT DOES NOT WORK
  We stop, we export your data and hand it to you, and you give us one hour to
  understand what went wrong. No charge beyond the pilot fee.

Signed: ______________ (Dealer)      ______________ (VSYST)
```

### 7.4 Converting a pilot to paid

Hold the decision meeting on the date, even if the answer is obvious, and even if you are frightened of it. Walk the four tick-boxes out loud. Then one of three things:

- **All four ticked → ask for the year, or the month, on the spot.** The quote goes out the same day; the advance is collected before onboarding continues ([[11-revenue-operations-and-partnerships|COO 11]] §5–§6).
- **Two or three ticked → diagnose which force failed** (§3.2), fix the one thing, and extend once by 30 days with a new written bar. **Once.** A second extension means the answer was no and you are the one who cannot hear it.
- **Zero or one ticked → close it honestly, export the data, and take the hour.** Then write the loss into the log with his words, and read it again at the Friday readout (§8).

The number that matters across pilots is not the conversion percentage in month one — with single-digit counts, percentages are noise ([[11-revenue-operations-and-partnerships|COO 11]] §11). It is **which of the four success ticks fails most often across pilots**, because that is a product instruction, and it goes to [[C25-product-bets-and-the-no-list|C25]].

### 7.5 The bespoke-feature trap

The most expensive thing a founder-CEO can do during pilots is build a feature for one dealer because he is standing in front of you and the ask sounds small. It is never small: it is a permanent maintenance obligation, a distortion of the roadmap and a precedent.

The rule to write into [[C25-product-bets-and-the-no-list|C25]] now, before the first pilot:

> **Build it only if (a) three or more ICP-qualified dealers have asked for it unprompted, or (b) it is on the roadmap anyway and this dealer merely moved it forward, or (c) he is paying for it as a separately quoted line and we would still own the code.** Anything else gets logged, never promised — and the dealer who asked hears first the day it ships ([[10-customer-operations-support-and-success|COO 10]] §10).

Say it out loud, in the room: _"Main aapko haan nahi bol raha abhi — likh raha hoon. Agar teen-chaar dealer yahi maangte hain to banayenge, aur sabse pehle aapko bataunga."_ Dealers respect that far more than a promise they suspect you cannot keep. And a founder who says yes to every ask in month two has, by month six, a services business wearing a SaaS costume — the exact failure mode the [[09_PRODUCT_VS_SALES|product-vs-sales analysis]] names.

### The Friday field readout — 45 minutes, non-negotiable

Sit down alone, or with the domain-expert director, every Friday, and do exactly this. It belongs in the CEO's weekly template ([[C27-ceo-weekly-template-and-calendar-audit|C27]]) and in [[15-the-ceo-operating-cadence-and-calendar|lesson 15]]'s calendar.

1. **List this week's conversations** (10 min). Name, ICP score, stage, what advance was obtained. Anything with no advance gets a recorded no.
2. **Five verbatim quotes** (10 min). Written in his language, not translated into yours. Paste into [[C08-positioning-and-messaging-canvas|C08]].
3. **New objections** (5 min). Any genuinely new one becomes a row in [[C10-founder-led-sales-pipeline-and-script|C10]] with a drafted answer. **Count the weeks since the last new row** — that counter is gate one in §9.
4. **One change** (15 min). Exactly one. A line in the script, a re-ordered demo, a rubric weight, a proof asset to build. Not five. One change per week is 50 changes a year, and each one is attributable.
5. **The kernel check** (5 min). Did anything this week contradict the diagnosis in [[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager|C04]]? If yes, it goes into [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] as an open question with a review date, not as a panic.

The honesty test on this ritual: **if the objection bank, the messaging canvas, the market map and the pricing sheet have not changed in a month, you are not listening — you are performing.**

### 9.2 Gate two — the cash gate (can it be afforded?)

The American gate is an ARR number and §1.2 showed why it does not translate. Here is the gate that does. All figures **illustrative** — VSYST has no price yet — and all **VERIFY LIVE** against real local salary data and real quotes.

```
A  = annual contract value per paying GSTIN        illustrative ₹24,000  (₹2,000/mo)
C  = fully loaded annual cost of one district
     field-sales executive in Chhattisgarh:
       salary       ₹22,000–35,000/mo
     + travel/fuel  ₹6,000–10,000/mo
     + phone/misc   ₹2,000/mo
                                        ≈ ₹3.6–5.6 lakh/yr; take C = ₹4.8 lakh

PRODUCTIVITY TEST
   A rep should produce ~3x cost in new ARR in year one
   3C / A = (3 x 4,80,000) / 24,000 = 60 new paying GSTINs/year = 5/month
   Sanity check against field reality: 25 visits/week x 5–12% cold close
   ≈ 1.5–3 closes/week is achievable ONLY in a warm, saturated district
   ([[12_OWNER_ACQUISITION|owner-acquisition research]] channel table)

AFFORDABILITY TEST  (the bootstrapped version — the one that binds)
   Committed recurring revenue must cover 12 months of C
   BEFORE the rep sells anything:
   C / A = 4,80,000 / 24,000 = 20 paying GSTINs  -> minimum
   plus buffer for ramp (a first rep is unproductive for 3–4 months)
   HOUSE CALL: 30–40 paying GSTINs, i.e. roughly ₹7–10 lakh committed ARR
```

**The house call, stated plainly:** VSYST hires its first salesperson at roughly **15–20 personal closes, a stable objection bank, and 30–40 paying GSTINs on the books** — not at ₹8.7 Cr of ARR, which at these ticket sizes would mean thousands of tenants and a decade of founder selling. Recompute the whole block the day a real price exists, and log the recomputation in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]]. The runway consequences of the hire belong to [[08-capital-runway-and-fundraising|lesson 08]] and [[C13-runway-burn-and-scenario-planner|C13]].

One SaaStr point survives translation and is worth the extra cost: **hire two, not one, when you can** — with one rep you cannot tell a rep problem from a process problem ([SaaStr](https://www.saastr.com/dear-saastr-how-do-i-hire-a-great-first-1-2-sales-reps)). At VSYST's scale two may be unaffordable at first; the honest substitute is **one rep plus the domain-expert director selling in parallel with the same script**, which gives you the same comparison at no extra headcount.

### 9.5 What the CEO keeps forever

Handing over the motion is not handing over the customer. Three things never leave the CEO's desk, at any size:

1. **The top N accounts.** Start with the top ten by revenue or by reference value — the dealers whose name in a sentence closes the next deal. A quarterly call, a visit a year, and the CEO's personal number. This is a small time commitment and it is the company's cheapest insurance.
2. **The OMC relationship.** IOCL-class relationships move on seniority and continuity and cannot be delegated to a field rep without losing altitude. This is [[14-partnerships-ecosystem-and-founder-relationships|lesson 14]]'s territory, with the tracker and MoU discipline in [[C26-partnership-evaluation-and-mou-checklist|C26]] and the operational tracker in [[11-revenue-operations-and-partnerships|COO 11]] §9.
3. **The losses. Always personally review the losses.** Every lost deal and every churned tenant, monthly, in the dealer's own words. Wins tell you what worked once; losses tell you where the strategy is wrong, and they are the only market research that arrives free and unsolicited. When the CEO stops reading losses, the diagnosis in [[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager|C04]] quietly goes stale and nobody notices for two quarters.

## 10. At VSYST — applying this now

- **Founder-led sales is not a phase VSYST is in. It is VSYST's current strategy.** The company is three people, pre-revenue, with one product in production and no price. Every hour the CEO spends at a pump is a strategy experiment, and the deliverable is [[C09-icp-and-customer-discovery-guide|C09]] plus [[C10-founder-led-sales-pipeline-and-script|C10]] — documents — with revenue as the proof they are true.
- **The unfair advantage is structural and perishable: use it now.** The office sits inside the family's petrol pump, and the domain-expert director has three decades of relationships in exactly the district the first hundred tenants must come from. That is a pre-built warm channel of the type that sits at the very top of the [[12_OWNER_ACQUISITION|acquisition ranking]] — and it will not stay warm if it goes unused for a year. **But** — see §9.1 — count the cold closes separately, or you will mistake a family network for a repeatable motion.
- **Raipur district, and nothing else, until it is saturated.** The atomic-network argument from the [[11_EDUCATION_GAP|education-gap analysis]] and the COO's territory rule agree: saturate one district before touching a second. Get the district's real outlet count from [PPAC's state-wise file](https://ppac.gov.in/infrastructure/retail-outlets) rather than a guess — **VERIFY LIVE** — and write the denominator into [[C06-market-map-sizing-and-segments|C06]].
- **Book five switch interviews before you book five demos.** VSYST already has free tenants using DZZLO today. Interview them — and, more valuable, interview two dealers who considered any new system in the last year and said **no**. The refusals are worth more than the adoptions right now, because the anxiety and habit forces are what the company does not yet understand.
- **Score before you visit.** Ten names a week through the §5.2 rubric, and be willing for eight of them to score under 45. A three-person company that visits every dealer who will take a meeting is a three-person company that runs out of Fridays.
- **Pilots are paid from the first one.** Even a token amount, in advance, with the §7.3 one-pager signed. VSYST has no published price and should not create one to run a pilot — quote deal by deal, log every quote, and let those logs become [[C11-pricing-and-packaging-decision-sheet|C11]].
- **The demo opens on the ledger, then the rate window.** Not the DIP module, not the invoice tiers, not the architecture. The technical CEO's specific temptation is to demo the thing that was hardest to build; the dealer's attention goes to the thing that costs him money tonight.
- **What not to do yet:** no sales hire (§9's gates are both red); no second district; no pricing page; no association sponsorship spend before you have five referenceable dealers to put on the stage with you; no cold-email campaign; no white-label or reseller conversation; and no bespoke feature for any single pilot dealer without the §7.5 test being met and logged.
- **What to hand off the day it exists:** the moment a deal has a stage and a next step, it belongs in ERPNext under the COO's pipeline, not in the CEO's head or WhatsApp scrollback ([[11-revenue-operations-and-partnerships|COO 11]] §3). The CEO keeps the target list and the loss reasons; the COO keeps the machine.

**11.1 — Fill C09: the ICP, the rubric and the disqualification list (45 min).** Open [[C09-icp-and-customer-discovery-guide|C09]] and write VSYST's ICP paragraph in your own words using §5.1 as a starting draft — then argue every clause with the domain-expert director and change at least two. Enter the nine-criterion rubric with your weights (change them if you disagree; record why). Write the disqualification list, including the sentence you will use to decline. **Artefact:** a completed C09 ICP section plus a `Pipeline` tab in [vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx](toolkit/vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx) with the rubric columns as headers.

**11.2 — Score ten real dealers and kill four (30 min).** Using the rubric, score ten named dealers you could reach this month — real names, real pumps, real routes to the owner. Rank them. Now **mark the bottom four as declined** and write one line each on why. Put the top six into the `Pipeline` tab with an ICP score, a source and a first advance to aim for. **Artefact:** ten scored rows, four explicit declines, six live targets — and this is the seed of [[C10-founder-led-sales-pipeline-and-script|C10]]'s pipeline of ten real prospects (add four more next week to reach ten live).

**11.3 — Book and run five switch interviews (40 min to book; 45–60 min each to run).** Use §3.4's script verbatim. Book five: at least **two dealers who said no** to some new system in the last year, at least one who said yes, and at most two who are current DZZLO users. Send the booking messages today — a WhatsApp voice note, per §6.2, with the honest framing that you are not selling. After each, fill the four-forces map from §3.5. **Artefact:** five booked interviews in the calendar, and five completed forces maps filed in [[C09-icp-and-customer-discovery-guide|C09]]. After the fifth, write the one paragraph that begins _"the thing I was most wrong about was…"_ and put it in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]].

**11.4 — Write the objection bank and build two proof assets (45 min).** Take §6.4's ten rows into [[C10-founder-led-sales-pipeline-and-script|C10]] and rewrite every "answer that works" in the exact Hindi you would actually say — read each one aloud; if it sounds like a brochure, rewrite it. Then pick the **two** objections you have personally heard most and actually build their proof assets this week (most likely: the one-page data-and-privacy note, and the "what stays in Tally / what moves to DZZLO" one-pager). **Artefact:** a ten-row objection bank in C10, and two proof assets that exist as files you can forward on WhatsApp.

**11.5 — Rehearse the eight-minute demo against a timer, twice (30 min).** Run §6.3's run-of-show cold, on a phone, with a stopwatch, to the domain-expert director playing a sceptical 58-year-old owner who has heard three pitches this year. Then run it again with him playing the second-generation son. Note where you ran over and what you cut. Then have **him** run it to you, because §9.1 requires two people who can deliver it. **Artefact:** the run-of-show table in [[C10-founder-led-sales-pipeline-and-script|C10]] with your actual per-minute timings written in, and a dated line in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] recording the first date on which someone other than the CEO delivered the full demo — that date is the clock-start on gate one.
