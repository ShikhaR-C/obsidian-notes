# 10 — Building the Team: Hiring, Equity and Firing

_Phase 3 · The Inward Job · Months 2–9. After this lesson you can say what share of your week hiring must take at 3, 10 and 30 people; name which hires you personally own and which you hand to the COO; state VSYST's talent bar in a sentence a stranger could apply; defend a sequenced first-ten-hires plan with a metric trigger on every row; run an executive loop and a reference call that gets truth; set compensation bands with no market data; design an Indian ESOP that is legal under Rule 12 and understood by the person receiving it; and fire someone lawfully, quickly and without destroying them._

## Explain-it-like-I'm-5

A family owns a small rice mill on the Raipur–Bilaspur road. The father can run every machine himself. What he cannot do is be at the destoner and at the mandi and at the bank on the same morning. So one day he hires a man to stand at the destoner.

Three things happen that he did not expect. First, **the man at the destoner decides what the mill is**. If he lets stones through, every bag the mill sells is a worse bag, and the traders find out before the father does. One person, hired in ten minutes, quietly re-set the quality of the whole business. Second, **the father cannot pay Raipur-city wages**, so he offers something else: a share of what the mill earns after the season, paid only if the man is still there at the end of it. That promise is worth nothing on the day it is made and everything three years later — and if it is not written down, it becomes a fight. Third, when the man does keep letting stones through, **the father waits four months to say anything**, because the man's uncle knows his uncle. Everyone in the mill already knew. The waiting cost more than the firing would have.

That is this lesson. Who you let stand at the machine. What you can pay them in when you cannot pay them in cash. And how to send someone home without wrecking them or yourself.

The COO course owns the machinery — the scorecard form, the offer letter, the appointment letter, the statutory thresholds, onboarding and offboarding checklists ([[09-people-operations|COO 09 — People Operations]], [[T10-hiring-scorecard-and-process|T10]]). This lesson owns the CEO's half: **the bar, the sequence, the seats you personally fill, the money and equity philosophy, and the decision to end someone's employment.** Those five do not delegate.

## 1. Wilson's second job, and what it costs in hours

Fred Wilson's reduction gives the CEO three jobs, and the second is unambiguous: "recruits, hires and retains the very best talent for the company" ([AVC — What A CEO Does](https://avc.com/2010/08/what-a-ceo-does/), 2010). Note the verbs. Not "approves hiring plans." Not "signs off on offers." **Recruits.** It is an active, personal, time-consuming job, and it is the one founder-CEOs most reliably fake — because unlike cash (which runs out on a date) and unlike strategy (which someone will ask you about), hiring badly produces no immediate signal at all. You feel productive. The bill arrives eleven months later.

The honest allocation, by company size:

| Company size        | Share of the CEO's week on people     | What that actually looks like                                                                                                                                                                     | The failure at this size                                                                        |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **3 (VSYST today)** | **5–10%, ~3 hours**                   | Not interviewing — _building the pipeline you will need in six months_: coffee with two good engineers a quarter, a running list of names, writing the scorecard for a role you cannot yet afford | Zero. Hiring is "later", so when the trigger fires you hire whoever is available in three weeks |
| **5–10**            | **20–30%**                            | Every single loop, personally: screen, work-sample review, final call, offer conversation, day-1 welcome                                                                                          | Delegating the loop to save time, then overruling it on feel                                    |
| **10–30**           | **30–50%**                            | Every leader hire personally; two levels of interviews on non-leader hires; running the bar, not the process                                                                                      | Stopping at 30% because the COO "has hiring now" — and losing the bar in one quarter            |
| **30–100**          | **Back to ~20–25%**, but only leaders | Exec loops, references you call yourself, the offer close, plus the retention conversations nobody schedules                                                                                      | Believing the drop from 50% to 25% means the job got smaller                                    |

At three people the number is small and the trap is specific. **The pipeline has a six-month lead time and your trigger has a three-week fuse.** A support hire you decide to make in October is a person you meet in November, offer in December and who is useful in February. If you start looking in October you will hire the person who is available in October, and availability is negatively correlated with quality. So the three hours a week are not interviewing hours. They are _knowing people_ hours — which in Raipur means the polytechnic and NIT Raipur placement cells, the two or three local software firms, the CA's client list, and the dealers' own sharp employees.

There is a second, uncomfortable reason the CEO cannot hand this over at VSYST. There is no HR function, no recruiter and no COO in the seat yet. Every hire is a founder hire by default. The choice is not "who does hiring" — it is "does anyone do it deliberately."

## 2. Which seats the CEO personally fills

The rule that scales: **the CEO personally hires every person until roughly the point where the CEO stops being able to describe every seat's actual daily work — call it 20–25 people — and personally hires every leader forever.** "Leader" means anyone who will manage someone, own a function, or be the company's face to a customer or a partner. Below that line, the CEO's job is to hold the bar, not run the loop.

What "personally" means is the part that gets fudged. It does not mean showing up to a 30-minute chat at the end and saying yes. Here is the split, with what the CEO must do _regardless of who runs the loop_:

| Seat                                                                                    | Who writes the scorecard                                             | Who runs the loop                  | Who makes the final call                                                                                                                                             | What the CEO must personally do, always                                                                              |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Co-founder / director**                                                               | The founders together                                                | No loop — this is a governance act | All existing founders unanimously, plus a board resolution and the founders' agreement amendment ([[03-the-ceo-core-value-and-the-founder-contracts\|lesson 03]] §6) | Everything. This is not a hire                                                                                       |
| **Any first-in-function hire** (first support, first sales, first non-founder engineer) | CEO + COO                                                            | CEO                                | CEO                                                                                                                                                                  | Write the scorecard, do the screen, review the work sample, make the offer call                                      |
| **Leader / function head** (first manager, first Head of Field Ops)                     | CEO                                                                  | CEO                                | CEO, with one dissenting voice able to block                                                                                                                         | Two interviews, both reference calls made by the CEO's own phone, the closing conversation                           |
| **Individual contributor, function already exists**                                     | Hiring manager + COO ([[T10-hiring-scorecard-and-process\|COO T10]]) | Hiring manager                     | Hiring manager, CEO can veto                                                                                                                                         | One interview for values and bar; read every written score before the decision meeting                               |
| **Contractor, defined scope**                                                           | Hiring manager                                                       | COO                                | COO within the [[T22-delegation-of-authority-matrix\|DoA limit]]                                                                                                     | Nothing, unless they touch code, the cap table or a customer's money                                                 |
| **Intern / trainee**                                                                    | Hiring manager                                                       | COO                                | COO                                                                                                                                                                  | Nothing — but see them on day 1, for ten minutes                                                                     |
| **CA / CS / lawyer / auditor**                                                          | CEO                                                                  | CEO                                | Board resolution where the Act requires it ([[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties\|lesson 09]])                                                             | All of it. These are relationships, not vendors ([[14-partnerships-ecosystem-and-founder-relationships\|lesson 14]]) |

Three notes on that table.

**The veto is one-directional and must stay that way.** A CEO who can veto a hire but cannot force one preserves the bar without destroying the hiring manager's ownership. The moment you overrule a "no" into a "yes" — which is the tempting direction when a seat has been open four months — you have told the company that the process is theatre.

**"Read every written score before the decision meeting" is the cheapest control you will ever install.** It costs eight minutes and it catches the two most common small-company hiring failures: the interviewer who wrote nothing (so scored on feel) and the panel that converged in the room because the most senior person spoke first. The COO course already mandates independent written scores before discussion; your job is to actually read them.

**Below-the-line hires are still yours culturally.** [[11-culture-and-values-as-a-ceo-instrument|Lesson 11]] makes the case that culture is what the mechanisms reward — and the hiring bar is the first and hardest mechanism. You do not need to interview the fourth support associate. You do need to know, from the scorecard, that the values competencies are still in it and still scored.

## 3. The bar, made concrete enough to use

"Hire A-players" is not a bar. It is a mood. A bar is a test that two different people apply to the same candidate and reach the same answer.

**Amazon's Bar Raiser** is the best-documented working version. A trained interviewer from outside the hiring team joins the loop, has no stake in filling the seat, and holds a specific standard: the candidate must **raise the average** — be better than roughly 50% of the people currently in that role — and the Bar Raiser can veto the hire outright ([Working Backwards — Bar Raiser hiring](https://workingbackwards.com/concepts/bar-raiser-hiring/); [IGotAnOffer — the Bar Raiser interview](https://igotanoffer.com/blogs/tech/amazon-bar-raiser-interview)). The detail people miss: practising Bar Raisers describe the veto as a "nuclear option" they almost never use — the mechanism's value is that an unconflicted person is in the room, not that heads roll ([4dayweek — Amazon Bar Raiser process](https://4dayweek.io/interview-process/amazon-bar-raiser)).

You cannot run a Bar Raiser programme with three people. You can steal its two working parts:

1. **The median test, stated out loud before the loop starts.** "Better than the median person doing this job here" is meaningless when N=0, so at VSYST it becomes: _would this person be in the top half of a team we would be proud of in three years?_ Write that sentence at the top of the scorecard, so it is asked before anyone is charmed.
2. **One unconflicted voice per loop.** At three founders, the unconflicted voice is the director who does _not_ need the seat filled. For the first support hire, that is the technical CEO drowning in tickets — so it must be one of the other two. Name them on the scorecard. Their "no" blocks.

**Why a company of three genuinely cannot carry a B-player**, stated as arithmetic rather than attitude. At three people each person is 33% of the company's capacity and 100% of some function. A weak fourth hire is not a 25% drag; it is a function that silently does not exist plus the founder-hours spent covering for it. The general research gives the shape: the US Department of Labor's often-quoted estimate puts a bad hiring decision at about **30% of that employee's first-year earnings**, and SHRM puts total replacement cost at **half to twice annual salary** ([Apollo Technical — cost of a bad hire](https://www.apollotechnical.com/cost-of-a-bad-hire/); [inop.ai — bad-hire statistics](https://inop.ai/the-true-cost-of-a-bad-hire-in-2026/)) — **VERIFY LIVE** if you ever quote a number externally; these figures circulate widely and are secondary citations of old studies.

The version that will actually change your behaviour is the calendar, not the rupees. Count in months:

| Stage                                                                          | Elapsed time (realistic, small company) |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------- |
| Hire starts; you tell yourself it is early days                                | Month 0–2                               |
| You notice, privately, and start explaining it to yourself                     | Month 3                                 |
| You mention it obliquely in a 1:1                                              | Month 4                                 |
| You act                                                                        | Month 6                                 |
| Notice period served                                                           | Month 7                                 |
| Seat re-opened, sourced, interviewed, offered, notice served by the new person | Month 10                                |
| New person productive                                                          | Month 12                                |

**A bad hire at ₹30,000/month costs roughly ₹2.1 lakh in salary you would not have spent, plus twelve months of the seat's output, plus the founder-hours spent covering — in a company whose entire monthly burn might be ₹1.5–2 lakh.** That is a quarter of a year of runway for one wrong decision made in a forty-minute conversation. Say the number out loud before you hire, not after.

**And now the counterweight, because "no B-players" curdles fast.** A high bar is a statement about _fit between a person and a seat_, not a ranking of human worth, and small Indian companies that adopt Silicon Valley talent language without the Silicon Valley severance culture end up simply being cruel. Three rules keep the bar honest:

- **The bar applies at the gate, not to people already inside.** Once someone is hired, your obligation flips from selection to development. If they are failing, that is at least partly a system failure — the wrong scorecard, no onboarding, an unclear charter (§10 and [[T29-org-chart-and-role-charters|COO T29]]).
- **A high bar obliges you to leave the seat open.** Slootman's rule — hire for demonstrated excellence and **leave the role open until you find it** — is only ethical if you actually do the second half. A "high bar" that becomes a rushed hire in week nine is just a slow low bar.
- **Never confuse the bar with the salary band.** If nobody clears the bar for three months, the likeliest cause is that your band is wrong or your sourcing is lazy, not that Chhattisgarh has no talent.

## 4. The first ten hires for VSYST, argued

This is the section to get right, because the order of the first ten hires is a strategy decision wearing a staffing costume. Most "first ten hires" lists are written for a venture-funded consumer product in Bengaluru: engineer, engineer, designer, growth marketer, PM, engineer. Almost none of that applies here.

Start from the actual shape of the business, because the sequence falls out of it:

- **The product is live and the market is not.** DZZLO OMS is on both stores and works. What is unproven is that a fuel dealer in Durg will change how his office runs and pay for it every month. So the binding constraint is not engineering capacity — it is **the number of dealers who get all the way from "yes" to a correctly-issued third month's invoice**.
- **Adoption is a human migration, not a signup.** Getting a dealer live means moving their customer master, their rate structure and their outstanding balances; teaching the office boy who actually punches the orders; standing in the pump office when the first GST invoice with TCS goes out; and answering WhatsApp at 10:40 PM when the rate-confirmation window opens. None of that is self-serve, and none of it is sales.
- **Distribution is district by district.** A district is a unit of geography, of dealer-association gossip and of drive time from Raipur — which means the marginal cost of the fifth dealer in Durg is far below the first dealer in Jagdalpur.
- **The failure mode is not "no customers", it is "a churned dealer in a district that talks."** In a market where dealers know each other through the association, one dealer who signs, struggles and quits costs you the district. That single fact re-orders everything below.

Now the four arguments that make this sequence different from the generic list.

### 4.1 Why implementation and support comes before sales

Because in a sales-assisted vertical SaaS sold to dealers who all know each other, **delivery capacity is the ceiling on sales capacity, and exceeding it is not a temporary problem but a permanent one.** A churned dealer does not go quiet; he tells the association meeting. In a district with maybe 40–120 relevant dealers, three loud failures is the district.

There is a second reason, specific to who you are. The technical CEO is currently the only person who can ship code _and_ the only person who can onboard a dealer, and those two draw from the same eight hours. Every support hour is an hour of engineering not done — but the reverse is also true, and only one of the two can be codified into a checklist by an ordinary careful person in three weeks. Onboarding can. Shipping the ledger cannot. **Hire against the one that codifies first**, which is exactly the COO course's rule — hire for whatever eats the most founder time that someone cheaper could do ([[09-people-operations|COO 09]] §2).

Third: hire #1 is also your cheapest source of product truth. A support associate sitting in a pump office for two months will hand you a better roadmap than six months of your own guessing ([[13-product-and-technology-leadership-for-a-technical-ceo|lesson 13]]).

### 4.2 Why the second engineer comes before a designer, a PM or a growth hire

**Bus factor, and it is not a metaphor.** One person can deploy. One person understands the voucher posting and the ledger. One person can be woken at 11:20 PM during the rate-confirmation window when a dealer's rates will not save. If that person has dengue for ten days, the company stops — and if the _ledger_ is what breaks, the damage is not downtime, it is a dealer's money being wrong, which is the one class of defect a fuel dealer will never forgive.

A designer is the obvious alternative and the wrong one, for a structural reason: **design work at this stage is bursty, high-skill and low-volume; engineering work is continuous, context-heavy and slow to transfer.** The right form for bursty high-skill work is a contractor with a scoped brief and a signed IP assignment; the right form for continuous context-heavy work is an employee. Buy design by the project. Hire engineering by the person.

A PM is worse. A PM at four people is a translation layer between two people who sit in the same room, and at VSYST it would translate between the technical CEO and the domain-expert director — who should simply talk.

### 4.3 The trigger, not the feeling

Every row above has a trigger that is a _number or an event_, not a mood. This is the single most valuable thing in the table, and it belongs in [[T21-headcount-plan|COO T21]] and in your own [[C13-runway-burn-and-scenario-planner|C13]] before the trigger fires — because the day you feel most overwhelmed is the day you will make the worst hiring decision of the year. A written trigger converts a panic into an already-made decision.

Write each trigger in the form: **"We open <seat> when <observable> holds for <duration>, funded from <source>, and we will know it worked when <outcome> by <date>."** If you cannot fill the funding clause, the role is not open; it is a wish.

## 5. Executive hiring — the loop you will not need for two years and must know now

Be honest about where you are: **VSYST has no executives to hire.** The first genuine leader hire is row 10, and it is 18–36 months out on any realistic reading. The reason this section exists now is that the first leader hire is the one you cannot undo cheaply — it costs six figures a month, it re-sets the culture of a whole function, and unwinding it takes two quarters and a lawyer.

**The scorecard is the whole method.** Smart and Street's _Who_ asks for three things before you meet anyone: the **mission** of the seat in one sentence, the **outcomes** — three to five measurable, dated results — and the **competencies**, including the company's values ([Who — book excerpt](https://geoffsmart.com/books/who-the-a-method-for-hiring/who-the-book-excerpt/)); a16z uses the same mission/outcomes/competencies document for executive hires ([a16z — executive hiring](https://a16z.com/executive-hiring/)). The template is [[C19-executive-hiring-scorecard-and-loop|C19]]; the individual-contributor version, which the COO owns, is [[T10-hiring-scorecard-and-process|T10]]. Do not maintain two rubrics — C19 is T10 plus four executive-specific competencies (hiring, firing, forecasting, and managing upward without flattery).

A workable loop for VSYST's first leader hire, six stages, four weeks:

| Stage                           | Who                       | Length              | What it must actually test                                                                                                        | Kill signal                                                                 |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------- | ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1. CEO screen                   | CEO                       | 30 min              | Motivation, and whether the seat as written is the seat they want                                                                 | They describe the job back to you as a bigger job than it is                |
| 2. Chronological interview      | CEO + one director        | 90 min              | Every role in order: what you were hired to do, against what number, what you actually did, why you left, who your boss was       | Vague numbers; "we" with no "I"; every departure was someone else's fault   |
| 3. Work sample, paid            | Hiring team               | 3–4 h of their time | A real artefact from the actual job — for a field-ops leader: a district plan for Durg with a route, a target and a staffing cost | A beautiful deck with no arithmetic in it                                   |
| 4. Focused competency interview | The unconflicted director | 60 min              | The two competencies scored lowest so far, plus values                                                                            | Cannot give a single example of firing someone, or of being wrong in public |
| 5. Reference calls              | **CEO, personally**       | 3 × 25 min          | See §5.2                                                                                                                          | Any reluctance to arrange the calls                                         |
| 6. Field day + close            | CEO + domain director     | Half a day          | Take them to a pump. Watch whether they talk to the office staff or to you                                                        | They stay in the car                                                        |

Stage 6 is not a formality. VSYST's business happens in a pump office at dispatch time; a leader who is uncomfortable there will manage from a spreadsheet, and you will find out in month five.

### 5.1 The two failure modes to name before they happen

**The big-company operator who cannot do the work.** The résumé says "led a 200-person distribution sales org at an FMCG major." What that person did was allocate targets, review dashboards and manage regional managers. At VSYST they will be asked to personally sit in a dealer's back office in Mahasamund and get a stubborn munim to punch orders into an app. Those are different jobs and the second is harder. The test is embarrassingly simple and almost never asked: **"In the last two years, what is the most recent thing you did personally, with your own hands, that someone two levels below you would normally do?"** Then ask for the date. If the answer is older than a year, they will not do it here either.

**Hiring your exact opposite as a reflex.** The instinct — "I am technical, so hire the commercial one" — is directionally sound and produces a real trap: an executive whose work you cannot evaluate, hired to compensate for a weakness you have not defined, reporting to a CEO who cannot tell good from bad in their domain. The COO course states the general form of this ([[01-what-is-a-coo|COO 01]] §2 on complementarity): complementary skills only work when the two people share a definition of the job and of what good looks like. So before you hire the person you cannot evaluate, do two things — write down the three numbers by which you _will_ evaluate them, and find one outsider (an advisor, the CA, a founder in a similar business) who can tell you whether their work is good. Complementarity without an evaluation method is just abdication ([[16-decisions-delegation-and-not-being-the-bottleneck|lesson 16]]).

A third, cheaper failure to note: **hiring an executive to avoid a decision you should make.** A "Head of Sales" hired because you dislike selling will not fix a positioning problem ([[06-strategy-ii-moats-positioning-and-the-business-model|lesson 06]]). Hire into a working motion, never into a broken one.

### 5.2 The reference call that gets truth

Reference calls fail for a structural reason: you call people the candidate chose, who are being polite, and you ask questions with obvious right answers. Fix all three.

**Set it up so honesty is the only option.** Bradford Smart's TORC — the "threat of reference check" — is simply telling candidates at the start that the final step is that _they_ will arrange calls with their former managers. Candidates who know their old bosses will not endorse them tend to select themselves out; the strong ones arrange the calls easily ([Topgrading — getting value from reference checks](https://blog.topgrading.com/new-blog/resources/blog/how-to-get-value-from-your-reference-checks); [Ask The Headhunter — a critical view of Topgrading](https://www.asktheheadhunter.com/10263/topgrading)). Note the criticism honestly: TORC pressures candidates and can be experienced as coercive, particularly for someone still employed. The proportionate version for VSYST: **ask for two managers, one of whom the candidate did not volunteer, and never contact a current employer without written permission.**

**The script.** Ten minutes of rapport, then:

1. "How did you work together, and for how long?" _(establishes standing — a peer who overlapped four months is not a reference)_
2. "What was she hired to do, and did she do it?"
3. **"On a scale of 1 to 10, how would you rate her performance?"** — then say nothing. The single most useful question in the call, because the number is rarely 10.
4. **"What would have made it a 10?"** This is where the truth lives. People will not volunteer a criticism but will happily describe an improvement.
5. "What kind of environment does she do her best work in — and her worst?"
6. "How did she handle being told she was wrong?"
7. "Who else should I speak to about her?" _(gets you the un-volunteered name)_
8. **"Would you hire her again, in what role, and is there any role you would not put her in?"**
9. Then silence. Count to five before filling it. The pause after question 8 produces more signal than questions 1–7 combined.

**Do it yourself, on the phone, and take notes into the scorecard, not into your memory.** A CEO who delegates the reference call on a leader hire has delegated the only stage that cannot be faked by a well-prepared candidate.

## 6. Selling the job — a pre-revenue Raipur company against a Bengaluru salary

At some point a good engineer will have your offer and another one, and the other one is 60% higher and has a brand on it. You cannot win that on money. You can win it — sometimes, with the right person — on four things that are actually true, and you lose instantly if you claim any that are not.

| What VSYST genuinely offers                           | Say it like this                                                                                                                                                          | The lie next to it that will destroy your credibility       |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Scope years earlier than a metro job gives it**     | "You will own the entire dispatch and invoicing surface. Not a ticket in it — all of it. At a 400-person company you would get that at 30, not 24."                       | "You'll be a founding engineer" said to person number four  |
| **Proximity to a real business**                      | "You will sit in a working petrol pump and watch the thing you built decide whether a tanker leaves. Most engineers never see their software touch physical reality."     | "We're disrupting the energy sector"                        |
| **A live product with paying customers**              | "It is on both stores, dealers use it every night in the rate window, and money moves through it."                                                                        | "We're pre-launch but about to explode"                     |
| **Stability of a family-owned, bootstrapped company** | "Nobody here gets laid off because a fund in Singapore changed its mind. There is also no fund, which cuts both ways."                                                    | Promising job security you cannot promise                   |
| **Cost of living arithmetic**                         | Do it explicitly: a ₹9L Bengaluru offer against a ₹6.5L Raipur offer, with rent, commute and time at home costed out — often a wash or better, and always better on hours | Pretending Raipur is Bengaluru                              |
| **Equity, with real arithmetic**                      | §8. Rupee value, an honest statement of when it might be worth anything, and the sentence "this may be worth zero"                                                        | Quoting a percentage with an implied valuation you invented |

**What you must say out loud, unprompted:** you cannot match metro cash; there is no big team to learn from; the brand will not open doors on their next CV the way a known company would; and the equity has no liquidity path today. Saying these first is not weakness — it is the only way the rest of what you said becomes believable. Candidates discount everything a founder says by some factor; volunteering the bad news is how you lower the factor.

**The closing conversation** is a separate meeting, held by the CEO, not over WhatsApp:

1. Restate what they told you they want — in their words, from your notes.
2. Say what the job will actually be for the first 90 days, including the bad parts (Saturdays during a rollout; the 10 PM rate window; driving four hours to Jagdalpur).
3. Give the number, the structure, the equity and the review date. No ranges, no "we'll see."
4. Ask the direct question: **"What would stop you saying yes?"** Then be quiet.
5. Answer only what is real. If the blocker is money and you have no more money, say so.
6. Give a decision date — three to five working days — and honour it.

**When to walk away.** If you find yourself past the third objection, still persuading, you are no longer hiring — you are recruiting a reluctant person into a company that will need conviction from them in month four. The rule to write down: **a candidate who needs to be convinced to join will need to be convinced to stay, at the worst possible moment, for +15%.** Withdraw warmly, stay in touch, and re-open the seat. This is much easier to write than to do at week nine of an open role, which is exactly why it belongs in the scorecard before sourcing starts.

## 7. Compensation philosophy when you have no market data

You will set your first salary band with no comparables, no HR function and no benchmark that covers "backend engineer, Raipur, vertical SaaS, 4 years' experience." Published India salary data is metro-weighted and the tier-2 discount is reported anywhere between 15–25% and 35–50% depending on the source ([Hyring](https://hyring.com/blog/software-engineer-salary-india/); [PeoplePilot](https://www.peoplepilot.io/blog/india-salary-benchmarking-tech-2026)) — a spread that wide means the data is thin, not that the answer is in the middle.

**The method that works without data**, in order:

1. **Three real conversations, not three websites.** Ask two local employers and one recruiter what they last _paid_ for this seat — not what they advertised. Ask the last two candidates you rejected what their current CTC is. That is your band.
2. **Set a band, not a number.** Three points: entry, mid, top-of-band. Write what moves someone from entry to mid (usually: independent delivery of the charter's outcomes for two quarters).
3. **Pay at the band, never above it, to close a candidate.** The first exception you make becomes the band. If you must go higher, move the whole band and tell the people already in it.
4. **Fix the structure once, with the CA.** Basic at ≥50% of total remuneration under the Labour Codes' uniform wages definition — the COO course owns this and the statutory add-ons ([[09-people-operations|COO 09]] §5). Budget **CTC ≈ gross + 13–16%** for PF, gratuity accrual, insurance and LWF once thresholds fire, so your headcount plan is not systematically 15% wrong.
5. **One review date a year, company-wide, on the calendar.** Not a negotiation triggered by whoever asks. A predictable annual review is worth real money in retention because it removes the incentive to threaten.
6. **Write down what you will not pay for.** Counter-offers, retention bonuses to people already leaving, and "market correction" for one person are the three that destroy a band fastest.

**The founder-salary question.** The instinct in a bootstrapped family company is to take zero. That is usually wrong, for two reasons: a founder under financial stress makes short-term decisions, and a ₹0 salary hides the company's true cost structure from you (your P&L says the company is profitable when it is actually subsidised by your savings). Benchmarks: bootstrapped founders commonly take nothing to a modest local-market number until first revenue, rising after; India founder-salary datasets cluster around a median in the low-₹20-lakh range, but those samples are metro and funded and are the wrong comparator for Raipur ([Kruze — startup CEO salary report](https://kruzeconsulting.com/blog/startup-ceo-salary-report/); [6figr — founder salaries in India](https://6figr.com/in/salary/founder--t); [startupcfo.ai — 2026 founder salary report](https://www.startupcfo.ai/insights/startup-founder-salary-report-2026)) — **VERIFY LIVE**, all secondary. The house call for VSYST: **a modest, real, booked salary for any founder working full-time — enough that nobody is dipping into savings — reviewed at the same annual date as everyone else's, and raised only when revenue crosses a pre-written number.** Never ad-hoc withdrawals; the finance course and the COO course both say the same ([[finance/00_README|Finance for Founders]], [[09-people-operations|COO 09]] §6).

**Directors' remuneration is a governance act, not a payroll act.** Section 197's 11%-of-net-profit cap on managerial remuneration applies to **public** companies, not private ones ([Section 197, Companies Act 2013 — Indian Kanoon](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/168868116/); [IncorpX — director remuneration in a private company](https://www.incorpx.io/blog/director-remuneration-private-limited-rules)), and remuneration paid to a director in his official capacity is generally outside the related-party regime of s.188 — but the paperwork still matters: it needs a **board resolution**, the interested director's disclosure of interest, minutes, and disclosure in the Directors' Report and financial statements, where the auditor will look for it. Anything paid _beyond_ remuneration for the office starts to look like a related-party transaction. **VERIFY LIVE with the CS** — the resolution wording, whether your Articles require anything more, and how it is disclosed. [[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties|Lesson 09]] owns the board mechanics; [[C17-board-pack-agenda-and-minutes|C17]] holds the resolution.

**Paying family differently is the fastest way to lose your first outside hire.** The rule: **the same band applies to a relative in the same seat.** If a family member is genuinely worth more, they are in a different seat — write the different charter. If a family member is willing to take less, that is a shareholder decision (a loan, a deferral, a distribution), not a salary decision, and it should be minuted as such. The moment your first non-family hire works out that the founder's cousin earns 40% more for the same work, you have spent your culture ([[11-culture-and-values-as-a-ceo-instrument|lesson 11]] §8).

**The transparency decision.** Three options, and you must pick one deliberately because the default — "nobody knows, everybody guesses" — is the worst of the three:

| Model                           | What it means                                               | Fit at VSYST today                                                                                                                               |
| ------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Fully open**                  | Every salary visible to everyone                            | No. It exports family-vs-non-family pay questions into a permanent argument before you have a band worth defending                               |
| **Open bands, private numbers** | The band for every role is published; individual pay is not | **Yes — the house call.** It answers "am I being cheated?" without answering "what does he earn?", and it forces you to actually write the bands |
| **Closed**                      | Nothing published                                           | Only defensible below ~5 people, and only if you say so explicitly rather than by silence                                                        |

Record the choice and the bands in [[C20-compensation-bands-and-esop-design|C20]] and the `Comp Bands` tab of [vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx](toolkit/vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx).

## 8. Equity — the full Indian ESOP treatment

This is the section where founders lose the most money and the most goodwill, usually by improvising. Read it with a pen, then take the output to your CS and your CA. **Every statutory item in this section is flagged VERIFY LIVE with a named professional, and you should assume at least one number below has moved since it was written.**

### 8.1 Why options, and not shares

The generous instinct — "just give them 1% of the company" — creates five problems at once. Options solve all five:

| If you issue shares directly                                                                                                                                                     | If you grant options                                                           |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| A perquisite tax bill lands on the employee **in the year of issue**, on a paper gain, with no cash to pay it                                                                    | Nothing is taxable at grant, and nothing at vesting                            |
| They become a shareholder with statutory rights immediately — notices, inspection, minority-protection petitions under ss.241–242                                                | No shareholder rights until they exercise                                      |
| If they leave in month seven you must get the shares back, and s.68 buy-back is a poor instrument for that ([[03-the-ceo-core-value-and-the-founder-contracts\|lesson 03]] §6.4) | Unvested options simply lapse; unexercised vested options lapse per the scheme |
| Every future signature — a rights issue, an SHA, an FC-GPR — needs them                                                                                                          | The pool is one line on the cap table until exercise                           |
| Transfer restrictions must live in the AoA or they do not bind                                                                                                                   | The scheme binds, and the register (SH-6) is the record                        |

There is a third instrument people confuse with both: **sweat equity** under **s.54** of the Companies Act and **Rule 8** of the same 2014 Rules — actual shares issued for know-how, IP or value addition, with immediate ownership and immediate perquisite tax, capped at **15% of paid-up capital or ₹5 crore in a year, whichever is higher, and 25% of paid-up capital in aggregate**, and available to a private company only after **one year from incorporation** ([CS Prashant Kumar — ESOP vs sweat equity vs phantom stock](https://csatwork.in/difference-between-esop-sweat-equity-and-phantom-stock-india/)) — **VERIFY LIVE with the CS**, including the startup relaxation on the aggregate limit. Sweat equity is for a specific non-cash contribution already made. It is not an incentive plan.

### 8.2 The legal machinery — s.62(1)(b) and Rule 12

| Requirement                  | What the law says                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 | What VSYST must do                                                                                                                                                           |
| ---------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Authority**                | ESOPs are issued under **s.62(1)(b)** of the Companies Act 2013                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   | Confirm the Articles permit it; amend if not                                                                                                                                 |
| **The rule**                 | **Rule 12, Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014** ([Rule 12 full text — CAIRR](https://ca2013.com/rule-12-companies-share-capital-and-debentures-rules-2014/))                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                    | Read it once yourself. It is two pages                                                                                                                                       |
| **Who is an "employee"**     | A permanent employee working in India or outside; a director, whole-time or part-time, **excluding an independent director**; employees of a subsidiary or holding company                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        | VSYST has no subsidiary. Contractors and advisors are **not** eligible — this catches people out                                                                             |
| **Who is excluded**          | A **promoter or a member of the promoter group**; a **director who, alone or through a relative or a body corporate, holds more than 10% of outstanding equity**                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  | All three founder-directors are caught by both limbs                                                                                                                         |
| **The startup proviso**      | Those two exclusions **do not apply to a DPIIT-recognised startup for ten years from incorporation** — extended from five years by the 2019 amendment to the Rules ([Mondaq — amendment to the Share Capital and Debenture Rules](https://www.mondaq.com/india/corporate-and-company-law/843806/amendment-to-the-share-capital-and-debenture-rules-new-regime-and-relaxations); [EquityList](https://www.equitylist.co/blog-post/esop-companies-act); [TaxGuru](https://taxguru.in/company-law/esop-effective-tool-startup.html)) | **VERIFY LIVE with the CS:** VSYST's incorporation date, the DPIIT recognition's validity, the turnover condition, and exactly when the ten-year clock runs out              |
| **Shareholder approval**     | Rule 12 says **special resolution**. Private companies may read that as an **ordinary resolution** under MCA exemption notification **G.S.R. 464(E) dated 5 June 2015** — sources genuinely conflict on whether to rely on it ([EquityList](https://www.equitylist.co/blog-post/esop-companies-act) says ordinary; [CS Prashant Kumar](https://csatwork.in/difference-between-esop-sweat-equity-and-phantom-stock-india/) says special)                                                                                           | **House call: pass a special resolution anyway.** It costs one extra line in the notice and removes an argument in diligence three years later. Get the CS's view in writing |
| **Separate approval needed** | For grants to employees of a subsidiary/holding company, and for a grant to any **identified employee of options equal to or exceeding 1% of issued capital** in a year                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           | Any grant above 1% to one person needs its own resolution — plan the pool so this is rare                                                                                    |
| **Explanatory statement**    | Must disclose ~12 items: total options, classes eligible, appraisal process, vesting requirements and period, exercise price or its formula, exercise period and process, lock-in, maximum options per employee and in aggregate, the valuation method, lapse conditions, an accounting-standards statement, and **the period within which an employee may exercise on termination or resignation**                                                                                                                               | Draft this once, properly. The last item is the clause that decides whether your ESOP is real (§8.6)                                                                         |
| **Minimum vesting**          | **At least one year between grant and vesting**                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   | The one-year cliff is not a convention here; it is the floor                                                                                                                 |
| **Exercise price**           | The company has "freedom to determine the exercise price in conformity with the applicable accounting policies"                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   | §8.4                                                                                                                                                                         |
| **Transferability**          | Options are **not transferable**, cannot be pledged or encumbered, and cannot be exercised by anyone but the employee (with defined death/incapacity carve-outs)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  | Say this in the grant letter in plain words                                                                                                                                  |
| **Register**                 | **Form SH-6**, the Register of Employee Stock Options, at the registered office, authenticated by the CS                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          | Open it the day the scheme is approved, not the day of the first grant                                                                                                       |
| **Variation of terms**       | Unexercised terms may be varied by special resolution, provided the variation is **not prejudicial** to option-holders                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            | You cannot quietly re-price or re-vest                                                                                                                                       |

**The sequence, in order, with who does what:**

1. **Board meeting** — approve the ESOP Scheme document and the pool size; constitute a compensation committee (or reserve grants to the Board); approve the notice of general meeting and the explanatory statement. _(CS drafts, board resolves, minuted — [[C17-board-pack-agenda-and-minutes|C17]].)_
2. **Notice of the general meeting** with the Rule 12 explanatory statement. _(CS.)_
3. **General meeting** — resolution approving the scheme and the pool. _(All three shareholders; they are also the whole board, which is convenient and still requires proper minutes.)_
4. **MGT-14 within 30 days** where a special resolution is passed. Private companies enjoy exemptions on certain board resolutions under s.117(3)(g); the general-meeting special resolution route generally still needs filing — **VERIFY LIVE with the CS** ([Lexology — issuing ESOPs by unlisted private companies](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=4c46cd9c-0afa-412e-8842-2417ed417172)).
5. **Grant letters** issued per board approval; each grant entered in **SH-6** on the day it is made.
6. **Vesting** happens by time; nothing is filed.
7. **Exercise** — written exercise notice plus payment of the exercise price; board allots; **PAS-3 within 30 days**; share certificates issued within two months; state **stamp duty** on the certificates; register of members updated.
8. **Tax** — the perquisite is computed and **TDS deducted in the month of exercise** (§8.5).

### 8.3 Pool size, and what a grant should look like

Indian benchmarks, all secondary and all **VERIFY LIVE**: roughly **5–8% at pre-seed/bootstrapped, 10–12% at seed, 12–15% at Series A**, with the seed pool expected to cover 15–20 key hires over 18–24 months ([ealkay — ideal ESOP pool size 2026](https://esop.ealkay.com/Ideal-Esop-Pool-Size-for-Startups-in-India-2026-guide); [Naraway — ESOP guide for Indian startups](https://naraway.com/Blogs/esop-guide-indian-startups-2026-complete.html); [PedalStart](https://www.pedalstart.com/blog/esop-for-startups)).

**House call for VSYST: authorise a 7% pool now and grant against actual hires only.** Reasons: a bootstrapped company with no funding round has no forced pool-top-up event, so the pool you create is the pool you have; 7% covers the first ten hires generously at the grant sizes below; and if you ever raise, the pre-money/post-money pool argument is a real dilution fight that [[08-capital-runway-and-fundraising|lesson 08]] §12 works through with arithmetic — do not pre-concede it by creating an oversized pool now.

Illustrative grant sizes (percentages of fully diluted equity, **illustrative, not benchmarks**):

| Seat                                 | Grant                                     | Why                                                                        |
| ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Employee #1 (implementation/support) | 0.30–0.60%                                | First non-founder. Highest personal risk, lowest market alternative        |
| Engineer #2                          | 0.50–1.00%                                | Bus-factor hire; the market alternative is a metro salary you cannot match |
| Field executive #1                   | 0.20–0.40% + cash incentive               | Their upside should be mostly variable cash, which they can actually spend |
| Employees #4–#9                      | 0.10–0.30%                                | Declining with company risk, as it should                                  |
| First leader (#10)                   | 0.75–1.50%                                | The seat that changes the company's ceiling                                |
| A relative who is also an employee   | **Exactly the band above for their seat** | §9                                                                         |

Three habits that separate a real ESOP from a decorative one:

- **Express every grant three ways: number of options, percentage of fully diluted equity, and rupee value at the last defensible valuation.** Indian practice increasingly insists on the absolute rupee number because a percentage without a denominator is meaningless ([Qapita — ESOPs in India](https://www.qapita.com/in/blog/how-esops-are-powering-indias-startup-revolution)).
- **Vesting: four years, one-year cliff, then monthly or quarterly.** Rule 12 sets the one-year floor; four years is convention.
- **Write the plan in plain words.** Indian survey work has found a large majority of employees never read their ESOP documents ([Qapita](https://www.qapita.com/in/blog/how-esops-are-powering-indias-startup-revolution)) — **VERIFY LIVE**, secondary. A one-page explainer (§8.7) is the fix.

### 8.4 Strike price, and the two valuations people confuse

Rule 12 gives you **freedom to set the exercise price**. That freedom is not free, because a low strike converts into a large taxable perquisite later.

Two different valuations exist, and conflating them is the most common professional error in this area:

| Valuation                       | Governing rule                                                                                                                                                                                                       | When it is needed                                                                                                                                                                                                                         | Who signs it                                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| ------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **FMV for the ESOP perquisite** | **Rule 3(9)(ii)** of the Income-tax Rules (some sources cite Rule 3(8)) — for unlisted shares, a **SEBI-registered Category I merchant banker**, on the exercise date or a date **not more than 180 days before** it | At **exercise**, to compute the perquisite                                                                                                                                                                                                | Category I merchant banker ([Treelife — ESOP taxation in India](https://treelife.in/taxation/esop-taxation-in-india/); [EquityList — perquisite tax on ESOPs](https://www.equitylist.co/blog-post/perquisite-tax-esops-india)) |
| **FMV under Rule 11UA**         | **Rule 11UA** of the Income-tax Rules — NAV or DCF methods                                                                                                                                                           | For share **issues and transfers** below FMV (s.56(2)(x)), for FEMA pricing on non-resident investment, and historically for **s.56(2)(viib) "angel tax"** — which the Finance (No. 2) Act 2024 **abolished with effect from AY 2025-26** | Merchant banker or registered valuer, depending on the provision ([Marcken — valuation report statutory checklist](https://marckenconsulting.com/valuation-report-statutory-checklist-india/))                                 |

**VERIFY LIVE with the CA**, hard, on both rows — the rule sub-numbering, whether the 180-day window still stands, and the current status of angel tax and Rule 11UA under the Income-tax Act 2025.

**House call while pre-revenue:** set the strike at face value or a small premium, document the board's reasoning in the minutes, and commission the merchant-banker valuation **before the first exercise, not before the first grant**. A pre-revenue company with no round has no defensible high valuation, and paying ₹40,000–₹1,00,000 for a valuation report nobody needs yet is runway spent on paper.

### 8.5 Taxation — two events, and the trap between them

| Event        | What is taxed                                                                                                                   | Head                           | Who pays / deducts                                                                                                                                                                                           | Cash consequence                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Grant**    | Nothing                                                                                                                         | —                              | —                                                                                                                                                                                                            | None                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| **Vesting**  | Nothing                                                                                                                         | —                              | —                                                                                                                                                                                                            | None                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| **Exercise** | **(FMV at exercise − exercise price) × number of shares**, as a **perquisite** under **s.17(2)(vi)** of the Income-tax Act 1961 | Salary, at the employee's slab | Employer **must deduct TDS** — **s.192** (ITA 1961) / **s.392** (Income-tax Act 2025, effective 1 April 2026), in the month of exercise; failure carries interest under s.201 and risks the salary deduction | **The employee owes real cash tax on a paper gain in a share they cannot sell.** This is the whole problem                                                                                                                                                  |
| **Sale**     | **(Sale price − FMV at exercise) × shares** as capital gains. Cost of acquisition is the FMV already taxed, not the strike      | Capital gains                  | Employee                                                                                                                                                                                                     | Unlisted shares are **long-term after 24 months**; **LTCG 12.5% without indexation**, **STCG at slab** ([Treelife](https://treelife.in/taxation/esop-taxation-in-india/); [Shahi & Co — ESOP taxation 2026](https://shahiandco.in/blog-esop-taxation.html)) |

**A live conflict you must resolve with your CA before quoting anything.** Several current Indian sources still describe LTCG on unlisted shares as _20% with indexation_ — that was the position before the Finance (No. 2) Act 2024 changed it to **12.5% without indexation**. Sources published in 2026 repeating the old figure are wrong or stale ([compare Treelife](https://treelife.in/taxation/esop-taxation-in-india/) with [ealkay — ESOP buyback and secondary liquidity](https://esop.ealkay.com/esop-buyback-secondary-liquidity-india-2026-guide)). **VERIFY LIVE.** This is exactly why every number in this section carries a flag.

**The deferral for eligible startups — and the condition almost everybody misses.**

- **What it does:** it lets the _employee_ postpone the perquisite tax at exercise, and the employer postpone the TDS, to the **earliest of three events**: expiry of the statutory window, the **date the shares are sold**, or the **date the employee ceases to be employed** by the company.
- **The window:** **48 months** from the end of the relevant assessment year under **s.192(1C)** of the ITA 1961 for shares allotted **before 1 April 2026**; **60 months** from the end of the Tax Year under **s.392(3) read with s.289(3)** of the **Income-tax Act 2025** for shares allotted **on or after 1 April 2026** ([Treelife — ESOP taxation](https://treelife.in/taxation/esop-taxation-in-india/); [Treelife — ESOP scheme design](https://treelife.in/taxation/esop-scheme-design-in-indian-startup-tax/)).
- **Who qualifies — the condition that disqualifies almost everyone:** the employer needs **DPIIT recognition _and_ a valid certificate of eligible business from the Inter-Ministerial Board under s.80-IAC** (s.140 under the new Act). Roughly **3,700 of about 1.97 lakh DPIIT-recognised startups** hold that certificate ([EquityList — s.80-IAC perquisite tax deferral](https://www.equitylist.co/blog-post/perquisite-tax-deferral-startups); [Patron Accounting — ESOP tax deferral under s.80-IAC](https://www.patronaccounting.com/blog/esop-tax-deferral-startup-employees-dpiit-section-80-iac)). The Income-tax Department publishes a return schedule specifically for it ([Income Tax India — Schedule: Tax Deferred on ESOP](https://www.incometaxindia.gov.in/w/schedulde_esop); [Income Tax India — taxation of ESOP](https://www.incometaxindia.gov.in/w/taxation-of-employee-stock-option-plan-esop-)).
- **It is a deferral, not an exemption.** The quantum does not change. And note the cruellest trigger: **leaving the company crystallises the bill** — so an employee who exercises and then resigns owes the tax immediately, still holding an unsellable share.

**VSYST's action item is specific:** VSYST is DPIIT-recognised ([[09-people-operations|COO 09]] §10). **Find out this month whether it holds an 80-IAC / IMB certificate.** If not, either apply for it — [[08-capital-runway-and-fundraising|lesson 08]] §5 treats the s.80-IAC tax holiday as a non-dilutive-capital item anyway, so the application pays for itself twice — or write plainly in the ESOP explainer that no deferral is available. Telling a hire "there's a startup tax deferral" when you have no IMB certificate is a lie with a five-year fuse.

**Worked grant, with the arithmetic** _(illustrative numbers, not a forecast; every rate **VERIFY LIVE** with the CA)_:

Assume 10,00,000 fully diluted shares. Priya joins as Implementation & Support Associate and is granted **6,000 options** — **0.6%** — at a **strike of ₹10**, four-year vest, one-year cliff.

| Step                                                           | Arithmetic                                                              | Result                                                                            |
| -------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Grant, year 0                                                  | 6,000 options @ ₹10                                                     | No tax. No cash                                                                   |
| Cliff, year 1                                                  | 1,500 options vest                                                      | No tax                                                                            |
| Fully vested, year 4                                           | 6,000 options vested                                                    | No tax                                                                            |
| She exercises all 6,000                                        | 6,000 × ₹10                                                             | **₹60,000 out of her pocket**                                                     |
| Merchant-banker FMV at exercise                                | ₹250/share                                                              | Shares "worth" ₹15,00,000                                                         |
| Perquisite                                                     | (₹250 − ₹10) × 6,000                                                    | **₹14,40,000 added to her salary income**                                         |
| Tax on the perquisite                                          | at ~30% + 4% cess on the top slice                                      | **≈ ₹4.5 lakh**, deducted by VSYST as TDS                                         |
| **Cash she must find**                                         | ₹60,000 + ₹4.5 lakh                                                     | **≈ ₹5.1 lakh, in a year she earned about ₹4.2 lakh, for shares she cannot sell** |
| With the deferral (only if VSYST holds the 80-IAC certificate) | Perquisite tax moves to the earliest of 60 months, sale, or her leaving | The ₹4.5 lakh waits — unless she resigns                                          |
| Sale, year 7, at ₹400                                          | (₹400 − ₹250) × 6,000 = ₹9,00,000 gain, held > 24 months                | LTCG at 12.5% ≈ **₹1.13 lakh**                                                    |

Read the fifth row again. **A grant designed without thinking about the exercise event hands your best employee a ₹5 lakh bill.** That is not generosity; it is a trap with your name on it. Which leads to the two clauses that decide whether your ESOP is real.

### 8.6 The two clauses that decide whether the ESOP is real

**Clause one: the post-termination exercise window.** Rule 12 requires the scheme to state the period within which an employee may exercise after termination or resignation, and most Indian schemes copy the American 90-day default. In a company with no liquidity, a 90-day window is a forfeiture clause wearing a suit: the leaver must find the strike price _and_ the perquisite tax in cash within three months, for an illiquid share, or lose everything they vested. **House call for VSYST: a long window — the full remaining option term, or at minimum five years, for good leavers — and say so on the grant letter's first page.** It costs the company nothing today and is the single most credible thing you can put in front of a sceptical candidate.

**Clause two: liquidity.** Be honest about the shape of this company. VSYST is bootstrapped, family-owned and selling into a niche vertical. **There may never be an IPO and there may never be an acquirer.** An ESOP whose only payoff is an exit that may not come is a lottery ticket with a tax bill stapled to it, and a 24-year-old in Raipur will work this out by year three.

The alternatives, compared honestly:

| Instrument                            | Legal basis                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         | Cost to the company                                                                        | Employee tax                                                                                                       | Fit at VSYST                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

| **Phantom stock / cash-settled SARs** | No statute; a contractual bonus plan. **Board resolution and a written agreement; no shareholder approval, no MCA filing.** SEBI's share-based regulations do not reach cash-settled plans at unlisted companies                                                    | **A real cash liability**, recognised and remeasured under Ind AS 102 and paid out of cash | **Salary in the year of payout**, TDS under s.192; company deducts under s.37(1) only when paid                    | **Strong fit later** for a company that will make cash but never exit — and the only route for advisors, who cannot hold ESOPs. **The VSYST design, stated in one paragraph so you can argue with it:** create a 7% ESOP pool by special resolution; grant to the first ten employees on four-year/one-year-cliff terms at a face-value strike; give a five-year-plus post-termination exercise window; commit in writing to a conditional annual buy-back window once free reserves exist; and layer a simple written profit-share for everyone from the first profitable year. Get the 80-IAC certificate so the deferral is actually available. Reserve phantom stock for advisors and for any senior hire who explicitly prefers cash certainty.

### 8.7 What you actually say to the person receiving it

One page, six sentences, handed over with the grant letter and read aloud:

1. "You are being granted **6,000 options**. That is **0.6%** of the company as it stands today, and it will go down if we ever issue more shares."
2. "They vest over **four years**, with nothing in the first year and then monthly. If you leave before the first anniversary, you get nothing."
3. "Your price to buy them is **₹10 each — ₹60,000 in total** — payable when you exercise, not now."
4. "If this company is ever worth ₹50 crore, your 0.6% is about ₹30 lakh before tax. **If nothing happens, it is worth zero. That is the honest range.**"
5. "When you exercise, **you will owe income tax on the difference between the valuation and ₹10, at your slab, in cash** — I will show you the arithmetic before you do it, and here is whether a startup deferral applies to us."
6. "You have **five years after leaving** to exercise, and here is exactly what we will and will not do about buying shares back."

If you cannot say sentence 4 out loud to a candidate, do not grant options — grant a bonus.

## 9. Equity for family and co-founders

Founder equity is not this lesson's subject. The split, the vesting mechanism, the leaver clauses and the reason an Indian Pvt Ltd cannot do "reverse vesting" the American way all live in [[03-the-ceo-core-value-and-the-founder-contracts|lesson 03]] §6.3 and §6.4, with the checklist in [[C02-founders-agreement-and-vesting-checklist|C02]]. **If you have not done that work, stop and do it before you grant a single option** — an ESOP pool created on top of an unvested, undocumented founder split is a second storey on a house with no foundation.

What this lesson owns is the situation lesson 03 does not reach: **the person who is a relative of a director and is also, genuinely, an employee.** In a family-founded company in Raipur this is not an edge case. It is the third hire.

### 9.1 The two hats, and why you must nail them apart

Almost every family-equity disaster comes from one confusion: **a relative who works in the company holds two entirely separate relationships with it, and people keep paying one with the other.**

| Hat                      | What it is                                                | What it earns                                                                 | The instrument                                      | Where it is recorded                |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| **Shareholder / family** | Ownership, usually from the founding or from family money | A share of value created, whenever it is created                              | Equity shares; dividends; a shareholders' agreement | Register of members; cap table; SHA |
| **Employee**             | A seat with a charter and outcomes                        | Salary at the band for that seat, and an ESOP grant at the band for that seat | Appointment letter; grant letter under the scheme   | Payroll; SH-6                       |

The failure mode is paying the employee hat in shareholder currency ("take less salary, you already own part of this") or paying the shareholder hat in employee currency ("give him a title and a salary, he's family"). Both feel generous in the moment. Both are, in company-law terms, a mess — the first is an unminuted shareholder decision dressed as payroll, and the second is a related-party transaction dressed as a hire.

**The rule, written so you can put it on a wall:** _the same seat gets the same band and the same grant, whoever's cousin holds it. If the family relationship is to be recognised, it is recognised in the shareholding, at a board or shareholders' meeting, on the record, with the reason minuted._

### 9.2 The company-law layer nobody warns you about

Three provisions bite here, and all three are cheap to comply with and expensive to discover late.

| Provision                                                                             | What it says                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       | What it means for a relative-employee at VSYST                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **s.2(77), Companies Act 2013 + Rule 4**                                              | "Relative" is a defined, closed list — spouse, father (incl. step), mother (incl. step), son (incl. step), son's wife, daughter, daughter's husband, brother (incl. step), sister (incl. step), and members of a HUF                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               | **Write the list out for each of the three directors, by name.** [[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties\|Lesson 09]] §3.3 makes this a governance artefact. You cannot apply s.188 without it                                                                                                                                                             |
| **s.188(1)(f) + Rule 15(3), Companies (Meetings of Board and its Powers) Rules 2014** | Appointment of a related party to **any office or place of profit** in the company is a related-party transaction needing a **board resolution**; where monthly remuneration exceeds the prescribed threshold — **₹2.5 lakh a month** under Rule 15(3) — **prior shareholder approval by resolution** is also required ([s.188 — CAIRR](https://ca2013.com/188-related-party-transactions/); [Rule 15 — CAIRR](https://ca2013.com/rule-15-companies-meetings-of-board-and-its-powers-rules2014/); [Vakilsearch — s.188 compliance guide](https://vakilsearch.com/article/related-party-transactions-section-188/)) | Hiring a director's brother is a **board agenda item with a resolution and the interested director not voting**, even at a ₹22,000 salary. | **Rule 12, Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules 2014** (§8.2)               | Options may not go to a **promoter or a person belonging to the promoter group** — but that exclusion is **switched off for a DPIIT-recognised startup for ten years from incorporation**                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          | A director's relative may fall inside the _promoter group_. If the startup proviso applies to VSYST, a relative-employee can hold options normally. **If the ten-year clock has run out, or DPIIT recognition lapses, the grant becomes void.** Get the CS to write the answer down with a date                                                                   |

The practical consequence is small and specific: **a relative-employee hire generates three pieces of paper an outside hire does not** — the s.188 board resolution, the interested director's disclosure, and a line in the Directors' Report. Twenty minutes of the CS's time. Skipping it is how a routine hire becomes an audit qualification and, at diligence, a discount.

### 9.3 The grant itself — five rules

1. **Grant at the band for the seat, from §8.3's table.** No premium for blood, no discount for it either. If you would give a stranger 0.30% for Implementation Associate #1, that is what your cousin gets.
2. **Same vesting, same cliff, same leaver terms — and mean them.** A four-year vest with a one-year cliff that everyone knows will never be enforced against a relative is worse than no vesting: it teaches the non-family hires exactly what the rules are for.
3. **The grant letter is signed and filed the same way**, entered in SH-6 the same day, and the exercise price is the same.
4. **Do not let a relative's grant substitute for family shareholding, or the reverse.** If the family intends someone to own more of the company, issue shares or transfer them, at a board meeting, with a valuation and a reason. Do not quietly hand them 3% of the ESOP pool because it is easier than a family conversation.
5. **Write the answer to one question before you sign:** _if this person resigned tomorrow, would I go looking for a replacement with this charter?_ If the honest answer is no, the seat does not exist and the grant is a gift. Gifts are legal. Call it one.

### 9.4 The four conversations to have before the first family hire

Have them separately, and write down what is agreed — this is exactly the ground-rules work [[11-culture-and-values-as-a-ceo-instrument|lesson 11]] §8.3 makes a board-minuted artefact.

- **"Who is this person's manager, and is it a director?"** A relative reporting to a relative removes the only mechanism that makes performance conversations possible. Where you can, put a family employee under a non-family manager, or if there is none yet, name in writing who takes over when there is.
- **"What happens if it does not work out?"** Agree the answer _before_ the hire, at a time when nobody is angry: probation applies, the review is written, and a decision at the end of it is a company decision, not a family one. §10.9 is the hard version of this conversation and it goes far better if this one happened first.
- **"What do the non-family employees see?"** Bands published (§7's house call), the same appointment letter, the same review date, the same leave policy. The first non-family hire will audit this within a month, silently, and will draw a permanent conclusion.
- **"Where does the family talk about this?"** Not in the board meeting, not in the standup, not on the shared WhatsApp group. A named, separate forum ([[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties|lesson 09]] §8.7). If a family employment matter has ever been decided in a board meeting because everyone was already in the room, you have a governance problem and a family problem, and you will fix neither.

### 9.5 The one founder-equity question this lesson does own

Everything about the original split — how the three of you divided the company, whether it vests, what happens if one of you leaves in year two — is [[03-the-ceo-core-value-and-the-founder-contracts|lesson 03]] §6, and re-deriving it here would only give you a second, slightly different answer to argue with. Go and read it.

The question lesson 03 does _not_ reach is the one that arrives eighteen months from now: **someone joins who is plainly better than a hire and plainly not one of the original three.** A senior operator who wants in. The dealer-side expert who has been informally advising for a year. The engineer who has, honestly, been carrying the product. Somebody says the word "co-founder" in a meeting and nobody corrects it, and three months later you are in an equity negotiation you never decided to have.

The mistake is picking the frame by feeling. Pick it with a test:

| Question                                                                                                                                                                                                            | If yes → **founder**                                    | If no → **senior hire with a large grant**                                                      |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Are they taking **founder-level financial risk** — below-market or no cash, for a year or more, with no other income?                                                                                               | Founder economics may be warranted                      | It is a job. Pay them for it                                                                    |
| Are they signing up for **unlimited scope and unlimited liability** — including becoming a **director**, with s.166 duties and personal exposure ([[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties\|lesson 09]] §10)? | Yes, and they understand what they are signing          | Then they are not a founder, whatever the business card says                                    |
| Would you give them a **veto** on the company's direction — permanently?                                                                                                                                            | Founder                                                 | Senior hire. A large grant buys alignment; it should not buy a veto                             |
| Is the company **still pre-product-market-fit**, so that what they contribute is invention rather than execution?                                                                                                   | Arguably founder                                        | After the product exists and dealers pay for it, later joiners are executing someone else's bet |
| Is there any version of this where they leave in year two?                                                                                                                                                          | Then the answer must be **vesting first, title second** | Same                                                                                            |

**House call for VSYST: default to "senior hire with a large grant" and make the grant genuinely large** — the 0.75–1.50% leader band from §8.3, or above it if the case is real, with the same four-year vest as everyone else. Reasons: founder equity at VSYST is _already issued shares_ in a family cap table, so making a fourth founder means the three of you transferring or issuing shares, with a valuation, s.56(2)(x) exposure and a permanently changed governance arithmetic; whereas an ESOP grant is reversible by vesting, costs nothing today, and is exactly what the person's next employer would have given them. And the honest point: **the title "co-founder" is free to give and impossible to take back.** If after two years they have behaved like a founder, you can make them a director then, on the record, with evidence. Nobody has ever regretted waiting.

If you do decide founder — do the whole of lesson 03 §6 again with four people in the room, amend the founders' agreement, and pass the board resolution. Half-doing it is how a co-founder dispute starts.

## 10. Firing — the CEO skill nobody teaches

You will read fifty articles on hiring for every one on firing, and you will get practice at hiring long before you get practice at this. That asymmetry is not because firing matters less. It is because firing is unpleasant to write about, unpleasant to read about, and — in India — legally intricate enough that most founder-facing writing simply skips it.

So it goes unlearned, and then it is done badly: too late, too vaguely, without documents, with a reason that is not the real reason, on a Friday evening, over a phone call, followed by silence to the rest of the team. Every one of those is a choice you can make differently, and every one of them costs money, people and reputation in a district where everyone knows everyone.

**The frame to hold:** ending someone's employment is not a punishment you are administering. It is an admission — usually that you put the wrong person in a seat, or built a seat that should not have existed, or managed badly. The person in front of you is rarely the one who made the first mistake. That does not make the decision wrong. It makes the _manner_ of it your entire responsibility.

### 10.1 The "you already know" test — and why you are always late

Ask yourself two questions, honestly, and write the answers down before you consult anyone.

1. **"If this person resigned tomorrow morning, what is my first honest feeling?"** If the answer is relief — even relief mixed with guilt and worry about coverage — the decision is already made. Everything after that is you negotiating with yourself about the date.
2. **"Knowing everything I know now, would I hire this person into this seat again today, with enthusiasm?"** This is the test Netflix's culture memo made famous, and it works precisely because it strips out sunk cost, tenure and personal affection. It asks about the seat, not the person's worth. _(The Netflix memo is rewritten periodically — read the current version rather than a 2009 slide deck someone forwarded you.)_

If both answers point the same way, you already know. The literature on this is unusually consistent, and Ben Horowitz's version is the bluntest: the CEO's problem is not deciding, it is that by the time a CEO is asking the question, everyone who works with the person answered it months ago ([Horowitz, _The Hard Thing About Hard Things_](https://a16z.com/books/the-hard-thing-about-hard-things/), a16z).

**Why you are always late**, in a company of three, has four specific causes worth naming so you can catch yourself:

| Cause                          | What it sounds like in your head                       | What it actually costs                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Coverage panic**             | "If he goes, who does the dealer onboarding?"          | You are trading a two-month coverage gap for a twelve-month drag on quality — and you will have the gap anyway, later, at a worse time                                                                        |
| **Sunk cost**                  | "I spent four months training her"                     | Those four months are gone whichever way you decide. The only live question is the next four                                                                                                                  |
| **Hope disguised as fairness** | "One more quarter, it might click"                     | You have already given three "one more quarters" without saying so out loud. He does not know he is on his fourth                                                                                             |
| **Family proximity**           | "His uncle knows my uncle" / "she is my cousin's wife" | The single largest tax a family-founded company pays, and the reason §10.9 exists. In Raipur the social cost is real, not imagined — which is exactly why it must be decided in advance and not in the moment |

**The cost of being late, stated plainly.** A poor hire in a three-person company is not one-third of a problem; it is closer to all of it, because the other two people spend their time compensating, re-checking work, and quietly deciding what the standard around here really is. Estimates of the cost of a bad hire are wide and mostly American — commonly quoted at **30% of first-year salary at the low end and several multiples of salary for senior roles** ([Apollo Technical](https://www.apollotechnical.com/cost-of-a-bad-hire/); [inop.ai](https://inop.ai/the-true-cost-of-a-bad-hire-in-2026/)) — **VERIFY LIVE**, secondary and self-interested sources. Ignore the number. The cost that actually bites at VSYST is not rupees: it is that **your second hire watches how long you tolerate your first hire's work, and calibrates permanently.**

**The counter-rule, so this section is not read as licence.** Being decisive is not the same as being quick, and "hire slow, fire fast" is bad advice taken literally. Fast means _fast once you know_. It does not mean firing someone who has never once been told, clearly and in writing, what was wrong. That is §10.2, and it is not optional — legally or morally.

### 10.2 Is this a firing, or is it a management failure wearing a costume?

Four questions. Answer them in writing. If any answer is "no", **you owe this person a real, time-boxed attempt before you owe them an ending** — and if you skip it, you will make the same hire again in six months because you never found out what actually went wrong.

| Question                                                                                                                                              | If "no", what you owe them                                                                                                                                                                                              | Time      |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------- |
| **Did they ever receive a written charter** — outcomes, not activities — for this seat?                                                               | Write it now, using [[T29-org-chart-and-role-charters\|COO T29]]. You cannot fire someone for missing a target that lived only in your head                                                                             | 1 hour    |
| **Have I told them, in plain words, at least twice, that this specific thing is not working?** Not hinted. Not "we should improve turnaround." Told   | Tell them. Then again in writing the same day. Most under-performance in tiny companies is under-communication ([Buckingham & Goodall, _The Feedback Fallacy_, HBR 2019](https://hbr.org/2019/03/the-feedback-fallacy)) | 30 min ×2 |
| **Is the gap a skill they could plausibly acquire, or a trait they cannot?** Skills are trainable; values, judgment and reliability generally are not | If skill: one named person teaches it, with a checkpoint date. If trait: skip to §10.3                                                                                                                                  | Varies    |
| **Is the seat itself real?** Did I hire a person into a job I had not defined, or into a job that stopped existing when strategy changed?             | Then this is a **redundancy, not a performance problem**, and calling it performance is both dishonest and legally riskier (§10.3)                                                                                      | —         |

**The 30-day written attempt, when it is warranted.** Not the 90-day HR-theatre PIP written by lawyers to build a file. One page, given in person, that says: _here are the two outcomes that must be true on this date; here is what I will do to help; here is what happens if they are not._ Weekly 20-minute checkpoints, written down each time. And you must mean it — a plan you have privately decided cannot succeed is a lie that wastes a month of somebody's life and, in India, is exactly the kind of pretextual document a tribunal reads sceptically.

**The honest statistic to hold:** most of these end in an exit anyway. That is fine. The point of the 30 days is not to save the hire — it is to make sure that when you end it, both of you know why, and that you learned something you can feed back into [[C19-executive-hiring-scorecard-and-loop|C19]] so the next loop catches it.

**The one case where you skip all of this:** conduct. Theft, falsified records, a safety breach on a tanker, harassment, leaking dealer data, lying to a customer. Those are §10.3's "for cause" row, they run a different process (an inquiry, not a plan), and treating them as a performance conversation is a category error that will cost you the trust of everyone who witnessed the conduct.

### 10.3 The four endings, named correctly — because the name decides the law

In India the _label_ you put on an exit determines which statute applies, which notice is due, what money is payable, and what a labour officer or tribunal will ask you to produce. Calling a redundancy a "performance issue" to spare someone's feelings is not kindness; it converts a clean, well-defined statutory process into a contested one.

**The one legal question that changes everything, asked first: is this person a "worker"?** Under the Industrial Relations Code 2020 the protective machinery — retrenchment notice, compensation, "last in, first out", dispute reference — attaches to _workers_, and generally excludes people employed in a **managerial or administrative capacity**, and supervisory staff drawing above a wage threshold (widely reported as **₹18,000 per month**, which is low enough that almost nobody clears it) ([LKS — Industrial Relations Code, key provisions](https://employmentlaw.lkslaw.com/labour-codes/industrial-relations)). **VERIFY LIVE with a labour-law advocate** — the threshold, the exact exclusion wording, and how Chhattisgarh's rules read.

The practical answer for VSYST: **assume your Implementation & Support Associate, your field executive and your junior engineer are all "workers", and that you personally, your co-founder-directors and any future functional head are not.** Design the exit accordingly. Assuming the protective regime applies and finding out it did not costs you fifteen days' pay per year of service. Assuming it did not apply and being wrong costs you a labour-court reference, back wages and a reinstatement argument.

### 10.5 What must exist on paper before you open your mouth

The rule: **if it is not written and dated, it did not happen.** Not because you are building a file to defend yourself — that mindset produces the pretextual documents tribunals see through — but because writing it down is how you find out whether your own case is sound. A reason you cannot write in four sentences is a reason you should not act on.

Before the conversation, these exist:

1. **The role charter or scorecard** the person was hired against ([[C19-executive-hiring-scorecard-and-loop|C19]], [[T10-hiring-scorecard-and-process|COO T10]]). This is the document that makes the whole thing fair rather than personal. It is also the reason to write scorecards at hiring time rather than at firing time ([Smart & Street, _Who_](https://geoffsmart.com/books/who-the-a-method-for-hiring/who-the-book-excerpt/)).
2. **The feedback trail** — at least two dated instances where you said the specific thing, in writing or followed up in writing the same day. The two-line WhatsApp-then-email habit is enough: _"Following up on what we discussed at 11 — the three dealer onboardings due last week are still open, and this is the second month. I need them closed by Friday."_
3. **The 30-day plan and its checkpoints**, if performance (§10.2).
4. **The charge sheet, reply, inquiry report and show-cause**, if cause (§10.4). Nothing happens before these exist.
5. **The written reason**, in four sentences, that you will put in the file and that matches what you will say out loud. If the letter says one thing and your mouth says another, you have created the evidence against yourself.
6. **The F&F computation from the CA** — salary to last day, notice or pay in lieu, leave encashment, retrenchment compensation if applicable, gratuity if applicable, reimbursements, recoveries, TDS — with the payout date. Printed. The person sees it in the meeting.
7. **The letters, drafted and reviewed**: the termination or non-confirmation letter, the relieving letter, the experience certificate. Have them ready. Making someone chase a relieving letter for three weeks is a small cruelty with a large reputational cost in a district-sized market.
8. **The pre-filled [[T13-offboarding-checklist|T13]]** with the day-0 access-revocation list already built from their [[T12-onboarding-checklist|T12]] sheet — Workspace, Bitwarden, GitHub, AWS/Atlas, ERPNext, Play Console, App Store Connect, the WhatsApp Business phone. **For an involuntary exit, T13's rule is that the access block happens as the conversation ends, not the next morning.**
9. **The announcement text** for the team (§10.8), written before the meeting so you are not composing it while shaken.
10. **The lawyer's sign-off** — a WhatsApp message from your labour-law advocate saying "this route is fine, use this notice period, send me the letter before you sign it" is enough for a routine exit. For anything involving cause, a family member, a director, or a person who has previously raised a complaint, it is a proper consultation.

### 10.7 The script

Read this, adapt the words to how you actually speak, and then **say it out loud twice before the meeting** — once alone, once to your co-founder. Rehearsal is not theatre; it is the only way the first sentence comes out clean when your heart rate is at 110.

```
[Sit down. No small talk. No "how was your weekend."]

"Rahul, I have some difficult news and I want to give it to you
 straight, because you deserve that.

 We have decided to end your employment with VSYST. Your last
 working day is 30 September. This decision is final and I have
 already made it — I am not here to discuss whether, I am here to
 tell you why and to tell you exactly what happens next.

 The reason is [ONE specific, true sentence]. We talked about this
 on 14 July and again on 6 August, and it has not changed. I know
 that is hard to hear."

[STOP. Say nothing. Let the silence run — ten seconds, twenty, however
 long it takes. Do not fill it. This is the single hardest instruction
 in this lesson and the most important one.]

[Then, when they speak or when they are ready:]

 "Here is what happens. You will be paid through 30 September, plus
 [notice / compensation]. This sheet is the full computation — my CA
 prepared it, please take it and check it, and ask me anything about
 it. It will be in your account within two working days of your last
 day.

 Your relieving letter and experience certificate are ready — here
 they are. If anyone calls me for a reference, here is exactly what
 I will say: [say the sentence]. I will not say anything beyond that.

 [If they hold vested options:] You have vested X options. You have
 five years to exercise them. Here is the sheet explaining the cost
 and the tax, and I will walk you through it whenever you want, this
 month or next year.

 For the handover, [COO/name] will work with you this week on the
 SOPs and the reassignments. Take today off if you want to; it will
 not affect anything.

 I am sorry. Not for the decision — I think it is the right one for
 the company and honestly for you too. But for the fact that it took
 me longer than it should have to tell you, and that made this worse
 for you than it needed to be."
```

**The rules underneath the script:**

| Rule                                                                                           | Why                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **The decision comes in the first thirty seconds**                                             | Everything else in the meeting is heard through the news. Leading with context means they hear none of the context and then panic when the news arrives                                               |
| **Past tense, singular, and owned: "I have decided", not "we have to let you go"**             | "We" hides behind the company. "Have to" pretends you had no choice. Both are cowardice and both are transparently so                                                                                 |
| **One reason. True. Specific. Never a list**                                                   | A list invites negotiation of each item, and the moment you concede any item the whole decision looks arbitrary. If the real reason is "the fit is wrong", say that and give the one clearest example |
| **Never a reason that is not the reason**                                                      | "It's a cost decision" said to a poor performer, or "performance" said about a genuine redundancy, is the sentence that later becomes a legal claim. Also, they will find out — everybody finds out   |
| **Do not debate. Do not defend.** "I understand you see it differently. The decision is made." | Debating implies reversibility. If you might genuinely reverse it, you should not be in this meeting                                                                                                  |
| **Do not say "I know how you feel"**                                                           | You do not, and they know you do not. Say "I know this is hard" instead                                                                                                                               |
| **Do not compliment as anaesthetic**                                                           | "You're brilliant, this just isn't working" is confusing and unkind. It makes the decision look irrational and gives them a story that stops them learning anything                                   |
| **Do not discuss the replacement, the team's reaction, or your own difficulty**                | Your discomfort is not their problem today                                                                                                                                                            |
| **Do have the numbers, on paper, in the room**                                                 | Money uncertainty is the thing that keeps a person awake that night. Removing it is the kindest single act available to you                                                                           |
| **Do offer something real, if you have it**                                                    | An honest reference sentence. An introduction to a dealer who is hiring. A month's extra pay. Not vague promises — one specific thing you will actually do                                            |

**The four reactions, and one line each:**

- **Silence.** Wait. Then: _"Take your time. I can also come back in an hour if you'd rather."_
- **Anger** — at you, at the company, at a colleague. Do not match it. _"That's fair. I would be angry too. The decision doesn't change, and I'll listen to whatever you want to say."_ If it escalates towards threat or damage, end the meeting calmly and call the advocate.
- **Bargaining** — a pay cut, a different role, one more month. _"I've thought about that, and no. I'm not going to give you false hope."_ The instinct to accept a bargain is the same instinct that made you late.
- **Tears.** Do not leave, do not look away, do not talk over it. Push the tissues across, wait, and then continue with the logistics — logistics are steadying.

**And one thing that is not in the script:** never fire someone while you are angry. If the trigger was an incident this morning, the meeting is tomorrow morning, and it is a different meeting with a different tone. Anger produces extra sentences, and every extra sentence in a termination is a liability.

A team that believes it will be told the truth about its own security stops spending three weeks speculating.

**Do not eulogise and do not trash.** The rule for both: **you will still be in the same city in ten years.** In Raipur your ex-employee will meet your next candidate. What you say about someone after they leave is the loudest possible statement about what it is like to work for you — louder than any value on a wall ([[11-culture-and-values-as-a-ceo-instrument|lesson 11]] §4).

### 10.9 Firing a co-founder or a family member — the hardest case in this lesson

This is the section you will hope never to need and the one most likely to decide whether VSYST survives its fifth year. Read it now, while nobody is angry.

Two facts set it up. First, **founder and co-founder exits are ordinary, not exceptional** — India's startup press reports them by the dozen every year, at every stage, including at companies that were doing well ([Inc42 — founder and co-founder exits, 2025](https://inc42.com/buzz/founder-exits-startup-cofounders-ceos-that-quit-in-2025/)). Second, **family enterprise is the default form of Indian business, not a quirk of yours** ([Deloitte India — family enterprises](https://www.deloitte.com/in/en/about/press-room/india-s-family-enterprises-drive-economic-power.html)). Both facts should reduce your shame about the situation and increase your urgency about the paperwork.

#### The three hats, again — and the mistake that ruins companies

A co-founder or family member in your company wears up to three hats, and **ending one does not end the others**:

| Hat                                                                                        | How it ends                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      | Instrument                                                                                                                                                           | Who decides                                                                                                                             |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Employee** (they hold a seat and draw a salary)                                          | Everything in §10.3–§10.8 applies unchanged, plus the s.188 paperwork that put them in the seat (§9.2)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           | Termination or negotiated separation; board resolution rescinding the office-or-place-of-profit appointment                                                          | The board, with the interested director not voting                                                                                      |
| **Director** (they carry s.166 duties, sign things, appear on MCA)                         | **Resignation under s.168** — a written notice to the company, the board takes note, and the director may also file DIR-11 ([CAIRR — s.168, resignation of director](https://ca2013.com/168-resignation-of-director/)). Or **removal under s.169** — **special notice**, a copy sent to the director immediately, the director's **right to be heard at the meeting**, and an **ordinary resolution** of members ([CAIRR — s.169, removal of directors](https://ca2013.com/169-removal-of-directors/); [Section 169 — Indian Kanoon](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/127830193/); [TaxGuru — removal of director under s.169](https://taxguru.in/company-law/removal-director-section-169-companies-act-2013.html)) | Board resolution taking note of the resignation, or the members' resolution; **DIR-12 filed within 30 days**; MCA master data, bank mandates and DSC returns updated | Resignation: the director alone. Removal: **shareholders, by simple majority — which in a three-way family cap table you may not have** |
| **Shareholder** (they own part of the company, forever, unless something moves the shares) | **Nothing you do above touches this.** Only the founders' agreement / SHA leaver clauses, a negotiated transfer at a valuation, or a s.68 buy-back moves shares                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  | [[03-the-ceo-core-value-and-the-founder-contracts\|Lesson 03]] §6.3–§6.4, [[C02-founders-agreement-and-vesting-checklist\|C02]]                                      | The parties, or the SHA                                                                                                                 |

**The mistake, stated once so it lodges:** founders routinely believe that removing someone from the company removes them from the cap table. It does not. **A departed co-founder with 30% of a company they no longer work at is a permanent passenger** — they vote, they must be asked for consents, they can block a fundraise, and they will own a third of whatever the remaining founders build over the next decade. This is the entire reason [[03-the-ceo-core-value-and-the-founder-contracts|lesson 03]] §6.3 makes founder vesting and leaver clauses a _before-anything-else_ task, and why [[C02-founders-agreement-and-vesting-checklist|C02]] exists. **If VSYST does not have signed leaver terms today, this sub-section has no mechanism to offer you, and that is the honest answer.** Fix that this quarter; everything below assumes you did.

#### The sequence, when it is a co-founder

Do these in order. Skipping step 1 is the most common failure and the most expensive.

1. **Separate "wrong seat" from "wrong company" — and be sure.** A co-founder who was right for a three-person company and is now out of depth as a functional head is a _role_ problem. The honest fix is a role change with a real charter and a real title, decided once, discussed openly, and never revisited by hints. It works surprisingly often and it is far cheaper than an exit — but it only works if you actually mean it, if the new seat has real outcomes, and if you never treat them as a demoted person. If you cannot do that with a straight face, do not offer it.
2. **Have the conversation alone with them, first, before any board mechanics.** Not the three of you. Not a meeting titled "board meeting". You say the thing, plainly, in the same shape as §10.7's script: what is not working, that you believe it must change, and what you propose. Then you listen for a long time. This conversation may take three sittings across two weeks. That is fine — this is the one exit where slow is correct.
3. **Bring in a neutral third party early** — not your lawyer and not a family elder with a stake in the outcome, but a genuinely neutral person both of you respect. In an Indian family business this is often the long-standing CA, a family friend who runs a business, or a formal mediator. Their job is not to decide; it is to make sure both people are heard and the same words mean the same thing to both.
4. **Agree the commercial terms in writing before the governance steps.** What happens to their shares (vested / unvested per the SHA; price; payment schedule); their salary and notice; their directorship; their DSC and bank mandate; their access; what you both say publicly. Write it as a term sheet, then have both sides' advocates convert it into a separation deed with mutual releases.
5. **Then run the governance steps, cleanly.** Board resolution, s.168 resignation (**always prefer resignation to removal** — s.169 is a public, adversarial act that leaves a permanent record and a right-to-be-heard confrontation in a general meeting), DIR-12, updated MCA data, revised bank mandates and DSC returns, the register of members updated if shares moved, and the minutes recording it factually and unemotionally ([[C17-board-pack-agenda-and-minutes|C17]]).
6. **Announce it once, together, in one agreed sentence** — to the team, to dealers, to IOCL and any partner who knows them by name, and to the auditor. A jointly agreed sentence, used by both of you, kills the rumour before it forms. Disagreeing publicly about why a founder left is a strategy-grade own goal ([[12-communication-the-ceo-as-chief-storyteller|lesson 12]] §9).
7. **Decide, in advance, which relationship you are protecting.** You cannot always keep both the company and the closeness. Say to yourself, in writing, before you begin: _"I am willing to lose X to preserve Y."_ Founders who never answer this end up losing both, because they take half-measures for six months and then act badly under pressure.

#### The additional layer when it is family

Everything above, plus five things:

- **Two forums, two conversations, in the right order.** The _family_ conversation happens first and separately, with the affected person and the family members who matter — not in a board meeting, and not on the shared WhatsApp group ([[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties|lesson 09]] §8.7). The _company_ decision follows, at a properly convened meeting, minuted factually. Collapsing the two is how a business decision becomes a family verdict and a family grievance becomes a governance crisis.
- **Never at a festival, a wedding, or a hospital.** Obvious, and violated constantly because those are the times everyone is in one place. Pick a neutral day.
- **The generational and gender dynamics are real.** A younger CEO ending the employment of an older relative is, in most Indian families, doing something socially transgressive regardless of how right it is. That does not make it wrong; it means you must plan the _how_ with as much care as the _whether_, and you should expect the family's judgment of you to lag the company's by years.
- **Money and dignity are separable — use that.** Very often the sustainable settlement is: **the seat ends, the shareholding stays, the title changes to something honest (advisor, board member, nothing at all), and the money is handled as a shareholder matter, minuted.** That is not weakness; it is the version that keeps a family intact and a cap table clean, provided the seat genuinely ends and does not quietly continue.
- **Write the ground rules before you need them.** [[11-culture-and-values-as-a-ceo-instrument|Lesson 11]] §8.3's family ground rules and §9.4's four conversations above exist so that this moment has a precedent to point at. A rule agreed in calm ("a family employee's performance is reviewed by their manager on the same date as everyone else's, and a decision at the end of a review is a company decision") is worth ten arguments made in the moment.

#### What "good" looks like afterwards

You will know you did it well if, twelve months later: the company's paperwork is clean and MCA reflects reality; the person is not surprised by anything that happened; the team can describe what changed without describing why; nobody outside heard two different stories; and you can still be in the same room at a wedding. That is the whole bar. It is achievable, and it is achievable almost only by people who decided the sequence before they needed it.

### 10.10 Severance — what to pay above the law, and why

The statute gives you a floor (§10.4). The floor is not the answer, because you are not optimising for the cheapest exit; you are optimising for **the next ten hires, all of whom will be able to reach this person by phone.**

| Situation                               | House call for VSYST                                                                                                                                                         | Reasoning                                                                                                                                        |
| --------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Non-confirmation at probation**       | Notice per the letter, paid in full even if you waive the working of it, plus a clean relieving letter                                                                       | The relieving letter is worth more to them than the money and costs you nothing                                                                  |
| **Performance exit, good-faith person** | Statutory minimum **plus one month's gross per completed year, minimum two months**, plus the long ESOP window (§8.6), plus a written reference sentence you actually honour | Two months is roughly a realistic job search in a tier-2 market. Below that you are transferring your own delay (§10.1) onto their rent          |
| **Redundancy / bet killed**             | The same as above, and say clearly and publicly that the seat ended, not the person                                                                                          | You may well want to hire them back when the bet is revived. Several of the best re-hires in small companies are people who were let go honestly |
| **Cause, proven through inquiry**       | Statutory dues only. Gratuity forfeiture **only** in s.53(6)'s narrow cases, on the advocate's advice                                                                        | Paying ex-gratia after proven misconduct signals to the team that conduct rules are negotiable                                                   |
| **Co-founder / family**                 | Whatever the SHA and the separation deed say, paid on schedule without being chased                                                                                          | The schedule being honoured is the entire signal                                                                                                 |

Two conditions on any ex-gratia amount: **pay it unconditionally and on time** (a severance held hostage to a signature is remembered as a threat), and **write down what it is** in the settlement so it does not get re-characterised later. Ask the CA about the tax treatment of ex-gratia on termination before you quote a net figure to anyone.

And one thing that is not money: **an honest, specific reference you commit to in the room.** _"If someone calls me, I will say you worked here for two years, you were excellent with dealers, and the analytical part of the role was not your strength. I will not say more than that."_ People remember that sentence for a decade, and it is free.

### 10.11 The week after — the part nobody warns you about

Three things happen and all three are your job.

**You will feel worse than you expect, and you will be tempted to over-correct.** The classic over-corrections are hiring a replacement in a hurry (the exact mistake that produced the exit), lowering the bar in the next loop because you cannot face doing this again, and going quiet with the remaining team because talking about it is uncomfortable. Name them in advance so you can catch them.

**The team is watching for two signals**, and neither is about the person who left: _does the work get covered, or does it silently land on me?_, and _does the CEO tell the truth about hard things?_ Answer both in the first 72 hours — publish the coverage plan with names on it, and hold a normal all-hands where you say the honest thing about why the company is where it is ([[C22-internal-comms-calendar-and-all-hands|C22]]).

**Then run the post-mortem, on yourself, in writing.** Not the person's failings — yours. Four questions, into [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]]:

1. **On what date did I first know?** Subtract that from the exit date. That number, in weeks, is your personal firing lag. Track it; it should shrink.
2. **What in the hiring loop let this through?** Which competency in [[C19-executive-hiring-scorecard-and-loop|C19]] was untested, which reference call did I skip, which "we'll figure it out" did I say?
3. **Was this really a seat, or did I hire a person because they were available?** (§4.3's trigger rule exists for this.)
4. **What did I fail to tell them, and when?** Almost always there is a specific month where the honest sentence was available and you chose the comfortable one.

Then change exactly one thing in the loop before the next hire — one added competency, one added work sample, one added reference question. One. A post-mortem that changes nothing is a diary entry ([[T17-incident-postmortem|COO T17]] holds the general form; this is its people-shaped cousin).

## 11. Retention: why good people leave a three-person company

Wilson's second job has two verbs, and founders reliably hear only the first: _recruit_ **and retain** ([AVC — What A CEO Does](https://avc.com/2010/08/what-a-ceo-does/), 2010). Retention is where the money actually is, because at your size a single departure is 25–33% of the company's capacity, six months of context, and — if it is your first non-founder hire — the story every future candidate will hear before they meet you.

And the standard advice is useless to you. You cannot out-pay anyone. You cannot offer a career ladder, because there are no rungs. You cannot offer learning from senior colleagues, because there are none. You have no HR function to run engagement surveys, and you should not want one.

What you have is four levers, all of them free, all of them CEO-owned, and all of them impossible to delegate to a process.

### 11.3 The stay conversation — the highest-ROI 30 minutes in the CEO's quarter

Exit interviews tell you the truth too late. The COO's process owns the exit interview ([[T13-offboarding-checklist|COO T13]]); the CEO owns its opposite, and it has no process at all: **a deliberate, calendared, half-hour conversation with each person, once a quarter, whose only purpose is to find out what would make them leave.**

Six questions. Ask them in this order, and write the answers where you will read them again.

1. **"What part of your work in the last three months are you proudest of?"** — you are calibrating what they value, which is often not what you were rewarding.
2. **"What is the most frustrating part of working here right now?"** Then wait. The first answer is polite; the second answer, after silence, is the real one.
3. **"What are you learning? What do you want to be able to do a year from now that you can't do today?"** — lever 1, extracted from them rather than assigned by you.
4. **"Is there anything about how I work that makes your job harder?"** — you will get nothing the first two times you ask, and something important the third. Keep asking. Skip-levels do this at scale; at three people you are the skip-level ([Radical Candor — skip-level meetings](https://www.radicalcandor.com/blog/skip-level-meetings/)).
5. **"If you got a call tomorrow from a company in Bengaluru at +50%, what would make you say no?"** — the single most useful question in the set, because it surfaces the actual bond and its actual price, and it does so _before_ the call comes.
6. **"What should I have asked you that I didn't?"**

Two rules. **Do not defend anything** while they are answering — the moment you explain why the frustrating thing is unavoidable, the conversation is over and will not restart for a year. And **come back within a week with one thing you changed**, however small, and say explicitly that it came from the conversation. A stay conversation that changes nothing teaches the person that telling you the truth is a waste of their afternoon.

Feed the answers into [[C27-ceo-weekly-template-and-calendar-audit|C27]]'s people block, and put the next date in the calendar before you leave the room.

### 11.4 The counter-offer, and letting people go well

**The rule to write down before it happens: VSYST does not counter-offer.** Not "rarely" — does not. Once you match an external offer, three things become true: every other employee learns that the way to get paid is to interview elsewhere; your bands are fiction (§7 rule 3); and you have bought, on average, a few months of a person who has already left mentally.

What you do instead, when someone tells you they have an offer:

1. **Thank them for telling you** rather than resigning cold, and mean it — that they told you at all is a sign the four levers were partly working.
2. **Ask the §11.3 question five properly**, once, without an offer attached: what would make you stay? If the honest answer is something structural you can fix (scope, a manager problem, a broken piece of the job) and you would fix it for anyone, fix it and say so — that is not a counter-offer, that is management arriving late.
3. **If the answer is money, say the truth**: "I cannot match that, and if I did I would have to lie to two other people about what our bands mean. I think you should take it."
4. **Then help them leave well.** Announce it warmly, hand over properly, pay F&F within two working days (§10.4), keep their ESOP window open (§8.6), and stay in touch. In a district-sized market, **your alumni are your best future recruiters, and sometimes your best re-hires.**

The half of this that founders find hardest is that **a good exit is a retention tool for everyone still there.** People watch how the leaver is treated more closely than they watch how the stayers are paid.

### 11.5 What the CEO owes that the COO's process cannot supply

The COO's people system is real and necessary: role charters, hiring loops, onboarding, offboarding, 1:1 templates, policies, the compliance calendar ([[09-people-operations|COO 09]]). None of it retains anybody by itself. Five things sit outside it, and all five are yours:

- **Meaning.** Why this company exists and why this person's specific work matters to a dealer in Jagdalpur at 10 PM. Nobody can write that into a template ([[12-communication-the-ceo-as-chief-storyteller|lesson 12]]).
- **Honesty about the company's odds.** Runway, the bet, whether the last quarter went badly. People tolerate risk they were told about and resent risk they discover. Telling three people the truth about cash is not a governance breach; hiding it is a retention time-bomb ([[08-capital-runway-and-fundraising|lesson 08]] §3).
- **Being believed.** Which is a slow accumulation of small kept promises — a review date honoured, a raise given on the date you said, a reference call you actually made.
- **Consequences for the people who do not meet the bar.** The fastest way to lose your best person is to keep your worst one. Everything in §10 is, read from this angle, a retention section.
- **Your own steadiness.** In a three-person company the CEO's mood is the weather. You are allowed to be worried; you are not allowed to be unpredictable ([[20-the-ceo-own-operating-system-and-succession|lesson 20]]).

**The decisions to take now and stop re-opening:**

- **7% ESOP pool, granted against actual hires only.** Not 10%, not 15%, and not "we'll decide when we hire" (§8.3).

- **F&F within two working days of the last day** — be the company that does this (§10.4).
- **Six-month written probation, with a written mid-point review, used honestly** (§10.3). It is the cheapest correction mechanism you will ever own.

**And the honest "not yet" list**, so you do not spend runway on theatre: no executive search, no recruiter, no HRMS, no engagement survey, no phantom-stock plan, no 80-IAC-dependent promises until the certificate exists, no gratuity provision until employee number ten is in sight, and **no ESOP grant at all until founder vesting is signed.**

## 13. Exercises

Five exercises. Do **13.1 first** — every other one attaches to a row in it. Each produces a dated artefact that goes into the vault or the toolkit, not into your head.

**13.1 — Write the first-ten-hires sequence, with a trigger on every row (45 min, all three directors, one whiteboard).** Take §4's argued sequence and make it yours: ten rows, in order, each with **seat · one-line charter · the metric trigger that fires the hire · the band from §7 · the ESOP band from §8.3 · employee or contractor · Raipur or remote**. The trigger is the hard part and the whole point — it must be a number you already measure (active dealer tenants, support tickets per week, overdue onboardings, monthly recurring revenue), not a feeling and not a date. Argue every row until all three of you can state why hire #3 comes after hire #2. **Artefact:** a filled `Headcount Plan` tab in [vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx](toolkit/vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx), mirrored into [[T21-headcount-plan|COO T21]], with a named owner for watching each trigger. **Success test:** for at least two rows, the trigger has _not yet_ fired and you can say exactly how far away it is.

**13.2 — Fill [[C19-executive-hiring-scorecard-and-loop|C19]] completely for the next hire — the one whose trigger is closest (45 min, alone, then 20 min with a co-founder).** Write the mission in one sentence; **three to five outcomes with numbers and dates** (not responsibilities — outcomes); the competencies split into _must-have_ and _trainable_; the work sample the candidate will actually do (§3); the loop, with who runs each stage and what each stage is uniquely testing; and the **two reference questions from §5.2** you will not skip. Then do the thing that makes it real: write the **kill criteria** — the two findings that end a candidacy regardless of how much you like the person. **Artefact:** a completed, dated C19 for one named seat, filed in the vault and linked from the headcount plan row. **Success test:** hand it to your co-founder and ask them to describe the ideal candidate. If their description differs materially from yours, the scorecard is not finished.

**13.3 — Fill [[C20-compensation-bands-and-esop-design|C20]]: bands, the pool, and two real draft grants (45 min + a 30-min call with the CS and CA).** Three parts. **(a)** Bands for the first five seats — entry / mid / top-of-band, plus what moves someone from entry to mid, plus the founder salary line, using §7's three-real-conversations method rather than a website. **(b)** The pool: **7% authorised**, with the arithmetic showing what that is in options against your current fully diluted share count, and the special-resolution wording the CS will need. **(c)** **Two draft grant letters**, in full, at the §8.3 bands — one for Implementation & Support Associate #1 and one for Engineer #2 — each expressing the grant **three ways** (options, % fully diluted, ₹ at a stated valuation), with the four-year/one-year-cliff vest, the face-value strike, the **five-year post-termination exercise window**, and §8.7's six sentences printed on the covering page. Then put six questions to the CS and CA in one call: the Rule 12 promoter-group position for each director's relatives, the s.80-IAC/IMB certificate status, the strike-price reasoning to minute, the current LTCG position on unlisted shares, the perquisite-TDS mechanics, and whether the s.188 fourth-proviso exemption applies to you. **Artefact:** a filled C20, a filled `ESOP Pool` and `Comp Bands` tab, two draft grant letters, and the CS/CA answers written down with the date you asked — your **VERIFY LIVE** record. **Success test:** you can read §8.7's sentence 4 — _"if nothing happens, it is worth zero"_ — out loud without flinching.

**13.4 — Rehearse the termination script, and build the exit-readiness pack (30 min, in private, out loud).** Pick a real scenario — the most likely one is non-confirmation of your not-yet-hired first employee at the end of probation — and write §10.7's script in your own words, with a **single true reason sentence**, the actual date, and the actual settlement lines. Then **say it out loud twice**, timed, standing up. The first attempt will be too long and too soft; cut it until the decision lands in the first thirty seconds and the whole thing runs under two minutes. Then build the pack you would need on the day: the pre-filled [[T13-offboarding-checklist|T13]] access list, the four-sentence written reason, the F&F line items your CA would compute, the draft relieving letter, the team announcement (§10.8), and the advocate's name and number. **Artefact:** `exit-readiness.md` in the vault — script, checklist, letter drafts, advocate contact — plus a [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] entry recording the severance policy you chose from §10.10 and why. **Success test:** you can deliver the first three sentences without reading them, and the pack contains a real phone number for a real labour-law advocate you have already spoken to.

**13.5 — Run the stay conversation for real, and change one thing (30 min per person + one week).** You have no employees yet, so run §11.3's six questions with the two people whose retention actually decides VSYST's next two years: **your co-founder-directors** — separately, one each, with the questions asked exactly as written and no defending. Then answer the same six questions about yourself, in writing, and give your answers to them. Within one week, **change one thing** that came out of it and say explicitly that it came from the conversation. Book the next round now, 90 days out. **Artefact:** three sets of written answers filed privately, one shipped change with a date, and a recurring quarterly calendar entry per person, logged in [[C27-ceo-weekly-template-and-calendar-audit|C27]]'s people block. **Success test:** at least one answer to question 2 or question 4 genuinely surprises you. If nothing surprised you, you defended something — do it again and stay quiet longer.
