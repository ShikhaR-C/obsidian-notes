# C05 — Choice Cascade Worksheet (Where to Play / How to Win)

_Toolkit · fills the cascade exercises in [[05-strategy-i-diagnosis-and-the-strategy-kernel|05 — Strategy I: Diagnosis and the Strategy Kernel]] §4 · Owner: CEO drafts; the domain-expert director owns the where-to-play refusals; all three directors sign the version that is adopted · Cadence: filled immediately after [[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager|C04]], reviewed quarterly with it, rewritten annually · Workbook tab: section **B** of `Strategy Kernel` in [vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx](vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx)._

## Purpose

[[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager|C04]] gives you the _shape_ of a strategy. This worksheet gives you the _interrogation_. A.G. Lafley ran Procter & Gamble as CEO; Roger Martin was Dean of the Rotman School of Management at the University of Toronto ([Rotman faculty bio](https://www.rotman.utoronto.ca/FacultyAndResearch/Faculty/FacultyBios/Martin)) and the two of them wrote up the method they had used on P&G's brands as _Playing to Win: How Strategy Really Works_ (Harvard Business Review Press, 2013) ([rogerlmartin.com](https://rogerlmartin.com/lets-read/playing-to-win); [a good one-page summary](https://sajithpai.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/Playing-to-Win-Summary.pdf)). Their claim is narrow and useful: **strategy is an integrated set of choices that positions you to win, and there are exactly five of them.**

The single most misread thing about the cascade is its arrows. It is drawn top-to-bottom, which makes it look like a sequence. It is not. It is a set of simultaneous equations: a where-to-play choice you cannot win in is not a choice, it is a wish; a how-to-win that needs a capability you cannot build is a fantasy; and a capability with no management system behind it decays inside two quarters. You fill the sheet by iterating, not by working downward once.

The two extra columns are what make this worksheet different from the picture in the book. Every row carries **"what must be true for this to work"** and a **confidence score**, because Martin's own account is that the useful question in strategy is not _what is true_ — which turns a meeting into a contest of confidence between people with no data — but _what would have to be true for this to be a good choice_, which turns the same meeting into a research agenda with owners and dates ([Roger Martin — What Would Have to be True?](https://rogermartin.medium.com/what-would-have-to-be-true-83dac5bd2189); [The Evolution of the Strategic Choice Structuring Process](https://rogermartin.medium.com/the-evolution-of-the-strategic-choice-structuring-process-653521519014)). Martin's warning about why this discipline is needed is worth keeping in view: planning processes gravitate to the comfortable boxes — budgets and capabilities — precisely because the uncomfortable ones, rows 2 and 3, force you to say where you will _not_ play and to admit that you might lose ([_The Big Lie of Strategic Planning_, HBR, Jan–Feb 2014](https://hbr.org/2014/01/the-big-lie-of-strategic-planning); [_A Plan Is Not a Strategy_, HBR video](https://hbr.org/video/6280466648001/a-plan-is-not-a-strategy)).

**Row 5 is not this course's to build.** "What management systems are required?" is the row where most strategies quietly die, because it is the row that requires the company to change its week — and it maps almost one-to-one onto artefacts that already exist in [[COO-Docs/index|COO Docs]]: the scorecard, the operating cadence, the SOPs, the delegation matrix, the quarterly rhythm. The CEO's job on row 5 is to name **which single number is the constraint this quarter** and then get out of the way. If you find yourself designing meeting agendas inside a strategy document, you have wandered into the wrong course.

## When to use

- **Immediately after the kernel is written** — same afternoon, while the diagnosis is still uncomfortable. The cascade without a diagnosis produces five confident answers with nothing underneath them.
- **When two directors are arguing about a decision and cannot resolve it.** Nine times in ten the argument is really about row 2 or row 3, unstated. Put the sheet on the table and ask which row you disagree about.
- **Before any territory, segment or channel expansion.** Expansion is a where-to-play change and should be an explicit, dated amendment to this sheet, not a drift.
- **Quarterly**, alongside [[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager|C04]] — 30 minutes, checking whether the "what must be true" rows got tested and whether any confidence score moved.
- **Before writing [[C09-icp-and-customer-discovery-guide|C09]]**, because the ICP is nothing more than row 2 made operational, and before [[C08-positioning-and-messaging-canvas|C08]], because positioning is row 3 made sayable.

## How to fill (rules)

1. **Fill it in pencil, then iterate.** Draft all five rows fast, then go back to row 2 and ask whether row 3 is actually possible there. Expect to change rows 2 and 3 twice before the sheet settles. A cascade filled top-to-bottom once, and never revisited, is a form, not a strategy.
2. **An aspiration is a state of the world, not a number.** "₹1 crore ARR by FY28" is a target: it says nothing about who, where, or why they chose you. An aspiration must be specific enough that a stranger could look at the world in three years and say whether it happened.
3. **Every where-to-play answer carries an explicit refusal.** Where-to-play at VSYST is not one question but three — **which segment, which geography, which buyer inside the firm** — and each needs a "not" attached. The refusal is the entire value of the row; without it you are playing everywhere, which at three people means nowhere.
4. **How-to-win must be a structural asymmetry, not effort.** "We will work harder / build a better product / care more" are not how-to-wins; they are hopes that the buyer evaluates products, and in a trust market he does not. Test it against Porter's line: strategy is about being _different_, and about the trade-offs that make the difference defensible ([_What Is Strategy?_, HBR, 1996](https://hbr.org/1996/11/what-is-strategy)).
5. **Write "what must be true" as a falsifiable sentence, in the future tense of a fact, not a feeling.** "Dealers trust peer recommendations" is a feeling. "Of 15 dealers asked who told them about the last business tool they bought, most will name another dealer" is falsifiable, and someone can go and find out this week.
6. **Confidence is a percentage you would bet at.** Anything at or below 40% is a live bet and needs a test with an owner and a date in [[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager|C04]]'s assumption register. Anything above 80% that has never been tested is not confidence, it is comfort — mark it and test it anyway.
7. **Capabilities are the few, not the many.** A capability is something without which the how-to-win is _impossible_. If it could be removed and the strategy still work, it is a competence, not a capability, and listing it costs you focus. Three or four rows, maximum.
8. **Row 5 is filled by reference, never by invention.** Point at the COO artefact that already exists ([[T05-kpi-scorecard|T05]], [[T03-weekly-ops-meeting-agenda|T03]], [[T08-sop-template|T08]], [[T22-delegation-of-authority-matrix|T22]], [[T19-quarterly-plan-and-review|T19]]). The CEO's only original contribution to this row is naming the constraint metric for the quarter.
9. **Run the interlock test before you sign it.** Pull each row out in turn and say what breaks in the others. If a row can be removed and nothing else gets harder, it is not part of an integrated set — it is a paragraph.
10. **Date it, version it, keep the old one.** A where-to-play change is a real decision with real cost; it belongs in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] and, for a segment or geography change, on a board agenda under [[C03-decision-rights-matrix|C03]].

**Now run the interlock test.** Pull row 2b's twelve-district refusal and row 3's hand-run onboarding stops being affordable — the founder is in Bilaspur and Pune in the same week. Pull row 3 and row 4's trust access converts into goodwill rather than paying dealers. Pull row 5's single constraint metric and row 3 silently degrades the first month the founders get busy shipping. Pull row 2c and the product gets sold to managers, who do not renew. **Every row's removal makes at least two others harder — so the set is integrated**, which is the only test the cascade actually has.

**And read the confidence column as an instruction, not a mood.** Two rows sit at or below 40%: whether twelve districts are enough (2b) and whether a founder can repeatably reach a first correct invoice in days (row 3, condition iii). Those are the two live bets of the quarter, they are both cheap to test, and they are the reason this worksheet is worth filling rather than admiring.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake                                      | What it looks like                                                    | Fix                                                                                              |
| -------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Filling it top-to-bottom once**            | Five rows, no iteration, signed in an hour                            | Rule 1. The rows are simultaneous equations. Expect rows 2 and 3 to change twice                 |
| **A target in the aspiration row**           | "₹1 crore ARR by FY28"                                                | Rule 2. Describe the state of the world in which you have won                                    |
| **Where-to-play with no refusal**            | "Fuel distribution in India"                                          | Rule 3. Three axes, three refusals, each with a mechanism that enforces it                       |
| **How-to-win that is really effort**         | "We will out-execute them" / "better product"                         | Rule 4. Name the structural asymmetry, or admit you do not have one yet                          |
| **"What must be true" written as a feeling** | "Dealers value trust"                                                 | Rule 5. Write the sentence someone could go and falsify next Tuesday                             |
| **Confidence scores that are all high**      | Every row at 80% before a single test                                 | Rule 6. Would you bet at those odds? Untested 80% is comfort                                     |
| **Twelve capabilities**                      | Every function listed as a capability                                 | Rule 7. If removing it leaves the strategy standing, it is a competence                          |
| **Building row 5 from scratch**              | Meeting agendas and scorecard designs inside a strategy document      | Rule 8. Point at the COO artefact. Name the constraint metric. Stop                              |
| **A cascade that contradicts the kernel**    | The kernel says one district; the cascade's aspiration says pan-India | They are two views of one strategy. If they disagree, the kernel wins and the cascade gets fixed |
| **Silent drift on where-to-play**            | Three out-of-cluster deals closed "because they came to us"           | Rule 10. Territory changes are logged decisions with a board line, not exceptions                |
