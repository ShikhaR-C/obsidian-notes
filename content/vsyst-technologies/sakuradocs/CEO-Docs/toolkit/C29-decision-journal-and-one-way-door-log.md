# C29 — Decision Journal and One-Way-Door Log

_Toolkit · fills the decision-journal exercises in [[02-how-a-ceo-thinks|02 — How a CEO Thinks]] §5 and the bottleneck exercises in [[16-decisions-delegation-and-not-being-the-bottleneck|16 — Decisions, Delegation and Not Being the Bottleneck]] · Owner: the CEO, personally — this is not a shared document · Cadence: written at the decision, before you act; reviewed quarterly, ninety minutes · Workbook tab: `Decision Journal` in [vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx](vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx)._

## Purpose

One table that turns experience into **calibration**. Without it, ten years in the seat produces one year of judgment repeated ten times, and there is no way to tell the difference from the inside.

It works against a specific defect in memory. Hindsight bias does not merely make the past look more predictable — it **rewrites what you believed you believed** ([hindsight bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindsight_bias)). Farnam Street's version is blunt: our brains "actively edit the past to make us look better and smarter", so the only honest record of what you thought is one written before the outcome was known ([FS — The Decision Journal](https://fs.blog/decision-journal/)). This is why the single most important rule here — the one that makes every other rule work — is that **the entry is written before you act, and never touched again except in the review column.**

The second thing it does is separate two things every founder fuses. Annie Duke's poker word for the fusion is **"resulting"**: judging the quality of a decision by the quality of its outcome ([Duke, _Thinking in Bets_, 2018](https://www.annieduke.com/books/)). A good decision with a bad outcome is still a good decision — you were unlucky, and abandoning a sound process after one bad result is how founders acquire superstitions. A bad decision with a good outcome is the dangerous one, because you got away with something and you will not next time. **The log exists so you stop rewarding luck**, in yourself and later in the people you hire. Nothing else in this course does that job.

Two filters shape every entry. The **door**, from Amazon's 2015 shareholder letter: Type 1 decisions are "consequential and irreversible or nearly irreversible — one-way doors" and must be made "methodically, carefully, slowly, with great deliberation and consultation", while most decisions "are changeable, reversible — they're two-way doors" and should be made quickly by high-judgment individuals or small groups ([Amazon, 2015 Letter to Shareholders](https://s2.q4cdn.com/299287126/files/doc_financials/annual/2015-Letter-to-Shareholders.PDF)). And the **clock** — week, year, decade — from [[02-how-a-ceo-thinks|lesson 02]] §3, which after twenty entries will show you something uncomfortable: almost everything you decide is on the week clock.

**Boundary, stated once.** The COO's [[T09-decision-log-and-adr|T09]] is the operational and architectural sibling: it is organisational memory — _what did we decide, who owns it, why does this module look like this_ — written for the engineer you hire in month nine. **C29 is about judgment and calibration**: what you were thinking, how sure you were, and whether you were right for the reason you thought. Same event, two artefacts, two audiences, three minutes versus ten. The COO's log answers _"what did the company decide?"_ This one answers _"am I any good at deciding?"_

## When to use

Not for everything. Journal everything and you will journal nothing by March. **Four triggers, and only four:**

1. **Every one-way door, without exception** — the cap table, a co-founder's terms, a partnership that binds the company, a pricing architecture customers will adapt to, an irreversible data-model choice in a ledger, anything signed.
2. **Every decision above a written rupee threshold.** ₹25,000 is a reasonable starting line at VSYST's scale — _illustrative_; set it once and align it with the COO's delegation-of-authority thresholds in [[T22-delegation-of-authority-matrix|T22]] so the company has one number, not two.
3. **Every decision where two directors disagreed** — including the ones that resolved amicably. Especially those.
4. **Every decision you feel certain about.** Certainty is the tell. The entries that teach you most are the ones you were sure you did not need to write.

Also open the tab when you are **not** deciding: a decision you have been sitting on for two weeks is itself an entry, with "do nothing" as the option currently winning by default.

## How to fill (rules)

1. **Write it before you act. This is not negotiable and there is no salvage path.** A journal entry written after the outcome is known is not evidence, it is a story, and it will be a flattering one ([FS](https://fs.blog/decision-journal/)).
2. **One question at the top, in the frame you are actually deciding in.** Bad frames are the most common root cause of bad decisions and the only place they become visible is a review, months later, when you read your own question and wince.
3. **Type the door honestly, then let it change the process.** Two-way door: decide today, alone, move on. One-way door: sleep on it, write a memo, get one outside opinion, and get both co-directors' written view before you act. Amazon's warning is about large organisations using the heavy process everywhere and going slow ([2015 letter](https://s2.q4cdn.com/299287126/files/doc_financials/annual/2015-Letter-to-Shareholders.PDF)); **a three-person startup fails the other way**, treating one-way doors as two-way because everything feels reversible when you are small. It is not. A cap table is not reversible. A dealer's trust is not reversible.
4. **Record the options you rejected, and always include "do nothing".** The rejected options are where all the learning is and nobody ever writes them down. Do-nothing is a real option with a real cost; if it is not on the list you did not evaluate it, you dismissed it.
5. **Put a number on confidence.** Without a number there is nothing to calibrate against. Use the probability that the expected outcome happens, not a mood. Forecasting research is unambiguous that people who record numeric probabilities and keep score improve, and that people who do not, do not ([The Good Judgment Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Good_Judgment_Project); [FS — Ten commandments for aspiring superforecasters](https://fs.blog/ten-commandments-for-superforecasters/); [calibrated probability assessment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calibrated_probability_assessment)).
6. **"What would change my mind" is written before you are attached to the answer.** Name the observation — a number, a count, an event — that would make you reverse. This single field is the difference between conviction and stubbornness, and it is the only defence you have against [confirmation bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confirmation_bias) and [escalation of commitment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escalation_of_commitment) once the decision is public and your name is on it.
7. **A review date, always, and put it in the calendar the same minute.** Without it the entry never returns and the loop never closes. Match the date to the clock: week-clock decisions review in a month, year-clock in six months, decade-clock in a year.
8. **Record your physical and mental state in one line.** It sounds soft and it is not. You will find a pattern in decisions made after 11 PM, after an argument, or in the hour after an avoided conversation.
9. **At the review, score outcome and process separately — and let them disagree.** The sheet computes a first-cut verdict using confidence as a rough proxy for "you had actually thought it through". Override it if the four process questions say otherwise: _Was the frame right? Did I consider do-nothing? Did I name a falsifier? Would I make the same call again knowing only what I knew then?_ Four yeses is a good decision whatever happened next.
10. **Review quarterly, ninety minutes, on the quarterly planning day.** Pull only the entries whose review date has passed. Score the diagonals. Tally calibration: of everything you marked 70%, how many happened? You need roughly twenty resolved entries before the number means anything, so do not compute it in month one — but **start collecting in week one, because you cannot backfill.** ([Brier score](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brier_score) is the formal version if you want one.)
11. **Never edit a written entry.** Corrections go in the review columns with a date. An editable journal is a diary of your current opinions.
12. **If a pattern appears three quarters running, it stops being a lesson and becomes a rule** in [[C01-ceo-charter-and-90-day-plan|C01]] or a row in [[C03-decision-rights-matrix|C03]]. The typical first finding for a founder is severe overconfidence on timelines and mild underconfidence on customer behaviour ([planning fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planning_fallacy); [Kahneman, _Thinking, Fast and Slow_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thinking,_Fast_and_Slow)).

## Template — the journal row

Thirteen columns, exactly as on the `Decision Journal` tab. Columns 1–10 are written **before** you act; 11–12 at the review date; 13 is computed.

| #   | Column                                    | Written     | Rule                                                                                                                                                |
| --- | ----------------------------------------- | ----------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | **ID**                                    | at decision | `DJ-001`, sequential, never reused. Referenced from [[C28-annual-plan-and-ceo-okr-sheet\|C28]], board minutes and [[T09-decision-log-and-adr\|T09]] |
| 2   | **Date**                                  | at decision | The day you decided, not the day you wrote it up                                                                                                    |
| 3   | **The decision**                          | at decision | One sentence, active voice, a verb and an object                                                                                                    |
| 4   | **Door**                                  | at decision | `One-way` / `Two-way` — and the cost to walk it back                                                                                                |
| 5   | **Clock**                                 | at decision | `Week` / `Year` / `Decade`                                                                                                                          |
| 6   | **Options considered (incl. do nothing)** | at decision | Lettered, each with one line on why it lost                                                                                                         |
| 7   | **Chosen because**                        | at decision | The reasoning, not the conclusion restated                                                                                                          |
| 8   | **What would change my mind**             | at decision | An observable event or threshold. Never "if it doesn't work"                                                                                        |
| 9   | **Confidence**                            | at decision | A %, of the expected outcome specifically                                                                                                           |
| 10  | **Review on**                             | at decision | A date, in the calendar today                                                                                                                       |
| 11  | **What actually happened**                | at review   | Facts. Plus the process-score override if rule 9 applies                                                                                            |
| 12  | **Outcome**                               | at review   | `Good` / `Bad`. One word, typed                                                                                                                     |
| 13  | **Verdict**                               | computed    | The 2×2 below                                                                                                                                       |

**The verdict matrix** — and note that only one cell tells you to change anything about how you decide:

|                    | **Outcome: good**                                                                                            | **Outcome: bad**                                                                                                            |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Process: sound** | Good decision, good outcome — repeat it                                                                      | **Good decision, bad outcome — do not change the process.** You were unlucky. This is the cell founders wreck themselves in |
| **Process: poor**  | **Bad decision, good outcome — do not repeat it.** Investigate this one hardest; you got away with something | Bad decision, bad outcome — change the process                                                                              |

**The one-way-door log** is not a second sheet. It is a filter: `Door = One-way`. Read the filtered view at every quarterly review and at every board meeting. Four rules apply only to these rows:

```
ONE-WAY DOORS — the different process
  1. Never same-day. Sleep on it, whatever the deadline says.
  2. Written before spoken — a memo, not a conversation (lesson 02 §6).
  3. Both co-directors' views in writing before you act. Silence is not agreement.
  4. One outsider who has done this before: CA, CS, lawyer, or an operator.
  Never delegated regardless of amount: anything signed for the company,
  the cap table, statutory filings, a binding partnership, the strategy
  kernel, related-party matters (§166, Companies Act 2013 — VERIFY LIVE).
```

## Worked example — VSYST (illustrative)

**DJ-001 · 29 July 2026 · Door: One-way · Clock: Year · Confidence 75% · Review 31 Jan 2027**

**The decision.** Charge per GSTIN rather than per user; bill on the web only, never in-app; gate the app server-side.

**Options considered.** (a) _Per user_ — the obvious SaaS default; rejected because it taxes the dealer for adding staff, and the staff are the ones who create the data that makes us sticky. (b) _Per transaction_ — aligns with value; rejected because it makes the bill unpredictable in a business whose volume swings with fuel prices, and unpredictable bills get cancelled. (c) _% of volume_ — rejected as unsellable to a dealer with thin per-litre margins. (d) _Per GSTIN_ — chosen. (e) _Free plus paid services_ — rejected: it converts a product company into a services company, and there are three of us. (f) _Do nothing, stay free_ — real cost: a year of usage data with no evidence anyone will pay, which is a year of the [[C13-runway-burn-and-scenario-planner|C13]] runway spent buying an answer we can get in ninety days.

**Chosen because.** GSTIN is the unit the dealer already thinks in and already pays tax against; it scales with his business without punishing him for adding staff; and it is countable, so we can never argue about the invoice. Web-only billing keeps the **15–30% commission** the app stores charge on in-app purchases ([Apple — App Review Guidelines §3.1](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/); [Google Play — service fees](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/112622); [comparison of store fees](https://splitmetrics.com/blog/google-play-apple-app-store-fees/)) — **VERIFY LIVE**, both stores have changed these terms repeatedly and both are under active regulatory pressure. At a pre-revenue company, 15–30% of every rupee of future subscription revenue is not a detail.

**Why one-way.** Not the price — the price is a two-way door and can move next quarter. The **architecture** is one-way: once dealers are provisioned per GSTIN, tenancy, entitlements, invoicing and the ledger are all built on that boundary, and once billing is web-only the app is shipped with no purchase surface at all. Reversing means a data migration, a store re-review, and a conversation with every existing dealer about why his bill changed shape. That is the cost of walking back, and writing it down is what earned this entry the slow process.

**What would change my mind.** _"If more than 30% of qualified dealers refuse to complete a web payment, or if multi-GSTIN dealers turn out to be under 10% of the base."_ Two numbers, both countable by January, neither of them a feeling.

**State of mind.** Written on a Sunday afternoon, unhurried, after two dealer conversations that week. No deadline pressure. Both co-directors agreed; the domain director's only reservation, recorded verbatim: _"the small dealer will not open a laptop."_

**At review — 31 January 2027 (illustrative).** _What actually happened:_ 11 of 14 qualified dealers completed a web payment (79%) — inside the falsifier. Multi-GSTIN dealers turned out to be 22% of the base, better than assumed. **But** median time from "yes" to first successful payment was 9 days, because the small dealer really does not open a laptop, and three of the eleven paid only after the CEO sat next to them. _Outcome:_ **Good**. _Verdict:_ good decision, good outcome. _Process override:_ none — the frame was right, do-nothing was priced, the falsifier was named and it held. **The lesson is not in the verdict, it is in the residue:** the domain director's one reservation was the only thing in the entry that turned out to be exactly right, and it is now a product problem (a WhatsApp-delivered payment link) rather than a pricing one — logged as DJ-021.

**The diagonal that teaches more, in one line.** **DJ-006 · two-way · confidence 85% · outcome Good · verdict: _bad decision, good outcome — do not repeat it_.** Bought a year of a monitoring vendor in one same-day call because a demo impressed you. It worked out. The process was: no options listed, no do-nothing, no falsifier, decided at 11:40 PM. Nothing about the good outcome makes that repeatable, and the next one of those costs you a quarter. **This is the cell the log exists for.**

## Common mistakes

| Mistake                                             | What it looks like                                                       | Fix                                                                                                          |
| --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Writing it after the outcome**                    | The entry is suspiciously well-reasoned                                  | Rule 1. Before you act, or do not bother. There is no partial credit                                         |
| **Resulting**                                       | "Bad result, so bad call — change how we decide"                         | The verdict matrix. Good decision + bad outcome = **do not change the process**                              |
| **Journalling everything**                          | Forty entries in January, none in March                                  | The four triggers only. Volume is how the habit dies                                                         |
| **Treating one-way doors as two-way**               | A cap-table change made on a Thursday call                               | Rule 3. Startups fail this way far more often than by being too slow                                         |
| **No do-nothing option**                            | Three variants of the same choice                                        | Rule 4. Price the cost of waiting; sometimes it wins                                                         |
| **Confidence as a word**                            | "Fairly confident"                                                       | Rule 5. A number, or there is nothing to calibrate                                                           |
| **"What would change my mind: if it doesn't work"** | Unfalsifiable, so you never reverse                                      | Rule 6. A count, a threshold, a dated event                                                                  |
| **No review date**                                  | Entries accumulate, nothing ever closes                                  | Rule 7. Into the calendar the same minute                                                                    |
| **Editing old entries**                             | The past keeps agreeing with the present                                 | Rule 11. Corrections go in the review columns, dated                                                         |
| **Merging this with the COO's log**                 | One bloated document serving nobody                                      | [[T09-decision-log-and-adr\|T09]] is memory; this is calibration. Different audiences, different lifetimes   |
| **Computing calibration at eight entries**          | "I'm well calibrated"                                                    | ~20 resolved entries minimum. Collect from week one anyway                                                   |
| **Skipping the quarterly review**                   | The only part everyone skips; also the only part that produces the value | Rule 10. It is a calendar block in [[C27-ceo-weekly-template-and-calendar-audit\|C27]], not a good intention |
