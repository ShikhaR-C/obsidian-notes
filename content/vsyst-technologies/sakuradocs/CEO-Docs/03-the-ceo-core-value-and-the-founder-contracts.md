# 03 — The CEO's Core Value and the Founder Contracts

_Phase 1 · Understand the Seat · Week 0. After this lesson you can say in plain words what a CEO is worth to a company that could otherwise just hire managers; describe what "good" looks like at 6, 12 and 24 months in evidence a stranger could check; run your own scorecard and name who reviews it when there is no board; recognise the eight ways this seat fails; and — the artefacts that matter — write the CEO's half of the CEO–COO contract, the founders' agreement with vesting, and the decision-rights matrix that reaches all the way up to the board._

## Explain-it-like-I'm-5

Three brothers own land outside a block town. They decide to stop only farming and open a seed-and-fertiliser shop on the main road. Somebody has to decide **what the shop will stock and which villages it will serve** — not once, but again every season when the rain fails or a new brand arrives. Somebody has to make sure there is **money in the box in June**, because stock has to be bought before sowing and paid for before anyone buys anything. Somebody has to decide **who is allowed to stand behind the counter** and to send home the cousin who is rude to farmers. Somebody has to tell farmers, and the bank, and the young man they want to hire, **why this shop and not the old one across the road**. Somebody has to make the **final call** when the three of them are split two–one. And somebody's name is on the **licence and the papers** when the inspector walks in.

That person is the CEO. Not the person who does the most work — the person who owns those six things.

Here is what actually goes wrong. They never wrote down who decides. For two years it doesn't matter, because they agree. In year three one brother has stopped coming in but still takes his third. Another brother's son wants a salary and a title. A supplier offers exclusivity and two of them want it. Now every conversation about the shop happens at dinner, in front of the mother, and nobody can say a hard thing without it costing them a relationship.

The shop did not fail because of the market. It failed because **the contracts were never written while everyone still liked each other.** This lesson is the two halves of that: what the CEO seat is for, and the four documents that make a founding team survivable — the CEO's charter, the CEO's half of the CEO–COO contract, the founders' agreement with vesting, and the decision-rights matrix.

## 1. What the company gets from a CEO that it cannot get anywhere else

[[01-what-is-a-ceo|Lesson 01]] defined the seat. This section says what it is _for_ — and specifically, what a company gets from a CEO that it could not get by hiring six good managers.

The spine is Fred Wilson's reduction, written in 2010 and still the most useful three sentences on the job: a CEO "sets the overall vision and strategy of the company and communicates it to all stakeholders", "recruits, hires, and retains the very best talent for the company", and "makes sure there is always enough cash in the bank" ([AVC — What A CEO Does](https://avc.com/2010/08/what-a-ceo-does/), 2010; Wilson expanded on the communication half a month later in [What A CEO Does (continued)](https://avc.com/2010/09/what-a-ceo-does-continued/), and the framing was widely picked up — e.g. [SKMurphy](https://www.skmurphy.com/blog/2011/02/01/fred-wilson-on-role-of-ceo-manage-vision-talent-cash/)). Six values, not three, because Wilson was writing for a venture-backed American CEO with a board and a general counsel. A founder-director of an Indian Private Limited company carries two more that cannot be delegated: **the final call** and **legal accountability under the Companies Act**.

Two things to notice about this list.

**None of the six is "does the work."** At three people the CEO will write code, answer a dealer's WhatsApp at 10 PM during the rate window, and drive to Bhilai. That is correct and temporary. But it is not the value. The value is what remains true about the company after the CEO stops doing any of it — which is the exact mirror of the COO course's line that "your product is the operating system, not the output" ([[03-the-coo-core-value-and-the-ceo-coo-contract|COO lesson 03]] §1).

**The six are not equally urgent at every stage.** For VSYST today, pre-revenue and bootstrapped, the live ones are #1 (a direction sharp enough to say no to a feature request) and #2 (a runway number that exists at all). #3 and #4 become live the month you can afford a hire. #6 is live from incorporation and is the one most likely to be quietly neglected because nothing bad happens for two years.

A caution on the time this actually takes. Porter and Nohria tracked 27 large-company CEOs 24/7 for 13 weeks — 60,000 hours of data — and found they worked **9.7 hours a weekday, 62.5 hours a week**, spent about **72% of work time in meetings**, and — the number that matters here — **36% of their time in reactive mode** ([HBR, July–August 2018](https://hbr.org/2018/07/how-ceos-manage-time); figures as reported by [MGMA](https://www.mgma.com/articles/test-of-time-harvard-study-reveals-how-ceos-spend-their-days-on-and-off-work)). Those are CEOs with chiefs of staff and gatekeepers. A founder-CEO who also ships code will run higher than 36% reactive unless the calendar is defended on purpose — which is why [[15-the-ceo-operating-cadence-and-calendar|lesson 15]] and [[C27-ceo-weekly-template-and-calendar-audit|C27]] exist, and why the six values above are worth memorising: they are the test you apply to every hour.

## 2. What "good" looks like at 6, 12 and 24 months

The COO's checkpoints run 6/12/18 months because an operating system can be finished. The CEO's run **6/12/24**, for a reason worth saying out loud: **a strategy is not falsifiable in under a year.** You cannot tell in month six whether "district by district, dealer-first, per GSTIN" was right. You can only tell whether you executed it honestly enough that the answer will mean something.

So the table below is written in **evidence a stranger could verify**, not adjectives. No row says "improved" or "stronger". Every row is a thing that either exists on a date or does not. Three cautions about reading this table.

**Do not set N in advance from a benchmark.** "N paying GSTINs by month 12" must come from your own unit economics — the number where the business becomes worth continuing at your cost base — not from a SaaS blog. Compute it in [[C12-unit-economics-and-business-model-calculator|C12]] and write it into the annual plan ([[C28-annual-plan-and-ceo-okr-sheet|C28]]) where it can be checked against.

**Month 6 has almost no revenue evidence in it, on purpose.** A founder-CEO who judges the seat on revenue at month 6 will make month-6 decisions that destroy month-24 — the classic being discounting the first five dealers into a price the market then anchors on. What you can honestly demand at month 6 is _honest evidence gathered_, not results.

**The people row is the one founders skip.** "One non-founder hire made, and still there" at month 12 is the single hardest row in the table for a bootstrapped family company, and the one most predictive. Wasserman's data on 10,000 founders is blunt about where founder-run companies break: by the third round of financing, **52% of founder-CEOs had been replaced**, and "nearly three-fourths of those" were board-driven rather than voluntary ([Noam Wasserman, CKGSB interview](https://english.ckgsb.edu.cn/knowledge/article/the-hypergrowth-conundrum-qa-with-noam-wasserman-start-up-statistician/); the full argument is [_The Founder's Dilemmas_](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691158303/the-founders-dilemmas), Princeton, 2012, and the underlying paper is [Founder-CEO Succession and the Paradox of Entrepreneurial Success](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=14658), _Organization Science_ 2003). VSYST has no investors and no outside board, so nobody will replace you. That is not the good news it sounds like: **it means the only person who can notice you have outgrown the seat is you**, which is what §3.4 is about.

## 3. How the CEO is measured — the CEO scorecard

### 3.1 Three different instruments, and why confusing them wrecks all three

By the end of Week 0 VSYST will have three measurement artefacts in play. They look alike and are not. Get this table right and half the arguments about "which number matters" disappear.

The rule that keeps them apart: **T05 answers "is the business healthy this week?" The COO scorecard answers "is the machine being built?" The CEO scorecard answers "is the company pointed at the right thing, funded, staffed and legal?"** A CEO who reads T05 daily has quietly become a COO — which is failure mode #1 in §4. A CEO who has no scorecard of their own has quietly become unaccountable, which is failure modes #5 through #8.

One honest note before the rows. There is a real body of practice on boards evaluating CEOs — five areas (financial performance, strategic execution, culture, stakeholder relationships, personal leadership), 6–10 KPIs, a backward-looking accountability half and a forward-looking capability half, a mid-year check-in so the annual review holds no surprises ([Ideals — CEO performance evaluation guide](https://idealsboard.com/blog/board-management/ceo-performance-evaluation-guide/)). Almost none of it survives contact with a pre-revenue three-director company, because it assumes an independent compensation committee. What follows is the version that survives.

Three rules, mirroring the COO's. **Numbers before narrative** — the review opens with the table, not with how the quarter felt. **You fill it; someone else reads it** — a scorecard only you ever see is a diary. **It is a contract, not a report card** — if a target was wrong, change it deliberately and log why in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]].

Row 7 deserves a defence, because it is the row founders delete. Every other row can be gamed by a CEO with good intentions: revenue by discounting, runway by deferring a hire you needed, bets by declaring victory early. Row 7 is the row you cannot game because you chose it while you were still honest. The best single candidate for VSYST's technical CEO is the calendar one — Porter and Nohria's 36%-reactive finding is a large-company floor, and a founder who also ships code will be far above it ([HBR, 2018](https://hbr.org/2018/07/how-ceos-manage-time)).

### 3.3 What this scorecard deliberately does not contain

Uptime. Support first-response time. Rate-confirmation rate. Invoices processed. DSO. Every one of those is real, and every one of them belongs to [[T05-kpi-scorecard|T05]] and the COO's weekly meeting. If they appear on the CEO scorecard, two things happen: the COO's ownership is undermined (failure mode #1 in the COO course's list — authority "formally promised and informally withdrawn", [Alder Koten](https://charliesolorzano.me/2026/03/17/coo-founder-led-company-failure/)), and the CEO's attention drains into the week. The CEO watches the business through **exceptions surfaced by the COO**, not through the raw dashboard. The full set of numbers a CEO does watch, and how they roll up, is [[17-the-numbers-a-ceo-watches|lesson 17]].

### 3.4 Who reviews it when there is no board — manufacturing accountability

VSYST's board is three founder-directors, all family, all of whom report to nobody. Statutorily that is a board; functionally it is not an accountability mechanism, because a board that cannot fire the CEO and has no interest in doing so cannot supply the pressure the seat needs. Investor-backed CEOs get this pressure for free — and, per Wasserman, sometimes get it terminally ([CKGSB](https://english.ckgsb.edu.cn/knowledge/article/the-hypergrowth-conundrum-qa-with-noam-wasserman-start-up-statistician/)). A bootstrapped founder-CEO has to build it deliberately. Four mechanisms, in increasing order of cost and effectiveness.

One person on the actual board — ideally not family — with the standing and the temperament to vote against you and survive the dinner afterwards. Indian family businesses are measurably weak here: PwC's 12th Family Business Survey found **over half** of Indian family businesses lack cross-industry board representation (29% globally) and **30% maintain family-only boards** ([Forbes India on PwC's 12th survey, Feb 2026](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/news/indian-family-businesses-high-on-ambition-low-on-governance/2991239/1); [PwC India](https://www.pwc.in/services/entrepreneurial-and-private-business/pwcs-12th-family-business-survey.html))              | A board seat, real information rights, and your ego | The decisions nobody else will contradict: a hire you should not make, a partnership you want too much, a pivot you are avoiding | The trigger is **any of**: first outside capital, first non-family employee above ₹X lakh cost, or the first time two founders deadlock twice on the same question |

### 6.3 The equity split — and the equal-split question, honestly

VSYST is three family founder-directors. The default that team will reach, unless something stops it, is a third each, agreed quickly, never revisited. Here is the evidence in both directions, because this is a case where the popular advice and the honest answer diverge.

| The case **for** an equal split                                                                                                | The case **against**                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| It is fast, and speed matters at the start when there is nothing to divide                                                     | Hellmann and Wasserman's study of founding teams found that **equal splitting is associated with lower pre-money valuations at first financing**, and the effect is more pronounced in teams that decided quickly — an estimated value at stake of roughly **10% of firm equity, 25% of the average founder stake, or ~\$450K in NPV** ([NBER Working Paper 16922, "The First Deal: The Division of Founder Equity in New Ventures"](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w16922/w16922.pdf)) |
| It signals genuine partnership and avoids a first conversation that could poison the team                                      | It usually means the conversation was **avoided**, not resolved — and avoided conversations return, with interest, at the worst moment                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| It removes a source of daily resentment about who contributed more this week                                                   | Contributions diverge. Two years in, one founder is full-time and one is not, and the split now describes a past that no longer exists                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| At a family company, an unequal split can read as a judgement on a person rather than a role — real social cost, not imaginary | The alternative to naming it is that everyone privately believes a different split is fair, and never says so ([Wasserman's broader finding](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691158303/the-founders-dilemmas): equity splits made as "quick handshakes" have a shelf life)                                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| Renegotiation is possible later — via ESOP grants, sweat equity or a secondary                                                 | Renegotiation is possible in theory. In a family firm it is close to impossible in practice, because it requires someone to say "you are worth less than you thought" to a relative                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       |

**The house call for VSYST.** An equal split among three family founders is defensible — but only if you do the three things an equal split is usually used to avoid:

1. **Write down why**, on the day, in one paragraph naming each founder's contribution: capital, domain, code, relationships, full-time-ness. Date it. This is the document that makes the split a decision instead of a default.
2. **Attach vesting to all three stakes, on identical terms** (§6.4). Vesting is what makes an equal split survive divergence, because it converts "who owns what" into "who has earned what so far" — and it is dramatically easier to agree when it applies equally to everyone including the CEO.
3. **Separate shareholding from employment and from salary** (§7). Most of the pain people attribute to an equal split is actually the pain of these three being welded together.

Where the split genuinely should not be equal: if one founder is part-time and intends to stay part-time, or if one contributed materially more capital, an equal split is not generosity — it is a deferred argument. Say it now, or write a mechanism (extra shares vesting on a full-time trigger) that says it for you later.

### 6.4 Vesting and cliffs — and how this actually works in an Indian Pvt Ltd

**The principle** is the same everywhere. Founders "earn" their equity over time by staying and working; if a founder leaves early, the shares they did not earn go back. The rationale is the free-rider problem stated plainly: without vesting, "one founder could just leave the company and keep his or her shares while the other founders are the ones working hard to build value in the business" ([WilmerHale Launch](https://launch.wilmerhale.com/explore/formation/founders/vesting-restrictions-on-shares-held-by-the-founders)). Note that this protects the **remaining founders** first and investors second — which is exactly the argument to make to a family co-founder who hears "vesting" as "you don't trust me."

**The standard shape**, as Indian practice states it: a vesting period of **three to five years** with a **one-year cliff** (nothing vests in year one; on the cliff, typically **25–33%** vests at once), and Treelife's caution that "any cliff longer than 1 year is aggressive" ([Treelife — Founder vesting in a shareholders' agreement](https://treelife.in/legal/founder-vesting-in-a-shareholders-agreement/)). Monthly or quarterly vesting after the cliff. **VERIFY LIVE** — norms move, and yours should be set by your lawyer against your actual facts, not by a table.

**The Indian structuring problem, stated honestly.** In the US, founder vesting is usually implemented as _restricted stock_: the founder holds the shares and the company holds a contractual right to repurchase the unvested ones at cost. India has no clean statutory equivalent, and the obvious substitute — a company buy-back under **Section 68** — is a poor instrument for routine founder vesting, for four separate reasons:

| Constraint under §68                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                    | Why it hurts founder vesting                                                            |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Buy-back must be **25% or less of the aggregate of paid-up capital and free reserves**, and the 10%-or-less route by board resolution alone is capped against paid-up equity capital and free reserves ([§68](https://ca2013.com/power-of-company-to-purchase-its-own-securities/); [EquityList](https://www.equitylist.co/blog-post/buy-back-of-shares-companies-act)) | A departing founder's unvested stake can easily exceed the cap                          |
| Funded only from **free reserves, securities premium, or the proceeds of a fresh issue** — not borrowings                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               | A pre-revenue company has no free reserves. There is literally nothing to buy back with |
| **No further buy-back offer within one year** of the closure of the preceding one                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       | Two founder departures in a year cannot both be handled this way                        |
| Post-buy-back debt must not exceed twice paid-up capital and free reserves                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              | Another constraint you may fail on a small balance sheet                                |

Add the tax layer: the treatment of buy-back proceeds in the shareholder's hands changed materially with the Finance (No. 2) Act 2024 ([Cyril Amarchand Mangaldas](https://corporate.cyrilamarchandblogs.com/2024/08/buy-back-of-shares-will-recent-changes-in-the-tax-laws-end-the-party/)) — **VERIFY LIVE with your CA before designing anything around a buy-back.**

**So what Indian practice actually does** is contractual **reverse vesting**: all shares are issued to the founder up front, and the SHA/founders' agreement subjects them to a reverse-vesting schedule under which, on departure, the unvested portion must be **transferred** — not bought back by the company — at a nominal price or a formula price. Treelife describes the mechanism as requiring "all the shares held by the founder to be subject to a virtual reverse vesting schedule", with the company holding "the right to impose/initiate the transfer of the unvested shares at a nominal price or at a predetermined percent of the fair market value" ([Treelife](https://treelife.in/legal/founder-vesting-in-a-shareholders-agreement/)). The transferee is typically the continuing founders, a nominee, a trust, or the ESOP pool.

Making that mechanism actually work needs machinery your CS and lawyer must build — **all of the following is VERIFY LIVE, and none of it is DIY**:

- a **call option** in favour of the company or the continuing founders, exercisable on defined leaver events, with a stated price formula;
- **transfer restrictions mirrored in the AoA**, because the SHA binds only signatories while the AoA binds all shareholders ([Sterling & Partners](https://sterlingpartners.law/shareholder-agreement-india-startup-sha-clauses/));
- executed but undated **share transfer forms (SH-4)** and/or a power of attorney held in escrow, so a departing founder's refusal to sign does not stall the transfer;
- **specific performance** and a named arbitration seat, so the remedy is the shares and not damages;
- a **valuation basis** for the transfer price — and here is the live trap: BharatPe's dispute with Ashneer Grover included a separate fight because "share transfers priced at face value rather than fair market value" created their own problem, and shares acquired even from a co-founder "remain subject to the SHA's restricted share mechanics once classified as founder shares" ([Treelife — the BharatPe/Grover SHA saga](https://treelife.in/legal/bharatpe-ashneer-grover-sha-saga/)). Ask your CA specifically about the income-tax consequences of a below-FMV transfer in both directions before you write "nominal price" anywhere.

**Good leaver / bad leaver, and who decides.** Define the categories in the agreement, not afterwards. And define **who determines "cause"** — the single most valuable lesson from BharatPe, where four SHA mechanisms (restricted shares with reverse vesting, a for-cause bad-leaver clawback, a drag, and an SIAC arbitration clause) were litigated across multiple forums for years: courts "give more deference to a cause determination made by an independent auditor or tribunal than solely by a board that benefits from the clawback", and founders should "push for an independent third-party determination before a bad leaver clawback is triggered" ([Treelife](https://treelife.in/legal/bharatpe-ashneer-grover-sha-saga/); background: [Inc42](https://inc42.com/features/bharatpe-vs-ashneer-grover-a-settlement-or-compromise/), [TechCrunch, Feb 2022](https://techcrunch.com/2022/02/28/ashneer-grover-shark-tank-bharatpe-board-resignation)). At a three-person family board, a board-only cause determination means two relatives voting on a third — name the auditor, the CA, or a named arbitrator instead, in the agreement, today.

**Acceleration.** Two clauses worth naming now because they cost nothing today and are expensive to add later: **single-trigger** acceleration (vesting accelerates on a change of control) and **double-trigger** (change of control **plus** the founder being terminated without cause within a window). Ask your lawyer for double-trigger as the default; single-trigger for the CEO only if you have a specific reason.

### 6.5 Roles, titles and decision rights inside the founding team

Titles among founders are not vanity — they are the map an outsider uses. The rules:

- **One CEO.** Not "co-CEO" among three family directors. If you genuinely cannot pick, that is a real disagreement about direction and it will not be solved by a title; it will be solved by §8.
- **Titles describe decision rights, not seniority or age.** Write for each founder: the decisions they make alone, the decisions they are consulted on, and the decisions they can veto. That list is C03 and it is more useful than the title.
- **"Founder" is permanent; "CEO" is a job.** Say this out loud once, early, while nobody is upset. It is the sentence that makes failure mode #7 survivable later.
- **Reserved matters need all three.** Cap-table changes, borrowing, a partnership that binds the company, changing the AoA, admitting a new shareholder, selling the company, and changing the founders' agreement itself.
- **Deadlock has a written route.** Two–one is a decision. One–one–one, or a genuine two-way split on a reserved matter, goes to the tiebreaker named in §7 — before it goes anywhere near a family gathering.

### 6.6 If a founder leaves, dies, divorces, or stops working

Four events, four clauses. Write them while all four are hypothetical.

| Event                                       | What must happen automatically                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 | The mechanism                                                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Leaves voluntarily**                      | Unvested shares transfer per §6.4. Vested shares: either retained subject to transfer restrictions, or subject to a buy-out right at a stated formula. Director resignation filed; bank mandates, cloud, store and repo access revoked the same week                                                                           | Good-leaver clause + the COO's offboarding checklist ([[T13-offboarding-checklist\|T13]])      |
| **Asked to leave for cause**                | Bad-leaver terms — but only after the **independent cause determination** named in §6.4, not a board vote of the two who benefit                                                                                                                                                                                               | Bad-leaver clause with a named third-party determiner                                          |
| **Dies or becomes permanently disabled**    | Shares pass to the estate (they are property) — so the agreement must say what the company or the continuing founders may do: a purchase right at a formula price, and a period to fund it. This is the clause that most often does not exist. Consider **key-person insurance** sized to fund it — quotes **VERIFY LIVE**     | Purchase right + funding mechanism + nomination on the shares                                  |
| **Divorce**                                 | Shares may become a matrimonial asset. The agreement cannot override matrimonial law, but transfer restrictions and a purchase right can keep an unconnected party off the cap table and out of the votes. **VERIFY LIVE with a family-law-aware lawyer** — this is genuinely jurisdiction- and personal-law-specific in India | Transfer restrictions in the AoA + purchase right                                              |
| **Stops working but stays** — the quiet one | This is the most likely of the five at VSYST and the one with no natural trigger. Define "materially ceasing to be full-time" with an **objective test** (hours, or presence, or a defined output), and attach a consequence: vesting stops, salary stops, and the seat is re-papered                                          | A "continued involvement" clause with a written test, reviewed at each quarterly board meeting |

### 6.7 Drag-along, tag-along, ROFR — and the layer where they actually bite

| Right                             | What it does                                                                                                        | Who it protects                                 | The Indian enforcement point                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **ROFR** (right of first refusal) | A shareholder wanting to sell must first offer the shares to the others on the terms of a genuine third-party offer | Everyone; keeps strangers off the cap table     | Contractually valid and enforceable in India **provided it is in the SHA and mirrored in the AoA** ([Vakilsearch](https://vakilsearch.com/article/drag-along-and-tag-along-rights/)). ROFO (right of first offer) is the weaker sibling — ROFR tied to actual third-party pricing gives remaining shareholders stronger protection |
| **Tag-along (co-sale)**           | If a majority holder sells, minorities may join on the same terms                                                   | The minority founder                            | Same: SHA + AoA. This is the clause that stops two of three family founders selling out and stranding the third                                                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| **Drag-along**                    | A defined majority who have agreed to sell can compel the minority to sell on the same terms                        | The majority; and any future buyer wanting 100% | Enforceable when clearly drafted and not oppressive, but a dragged minority can seek relief before the **NCLT under §§241–242** alleging oppression — price fairness, process transparency and good faith are what get tested ([Vakilsearch](https://vakilsearch.com/article/drag-along-and-tag-along-rights/)) — **VERIFY LIVE**  |

The general rule underneath all three, worth memorising: **a private contract binds the people who signed it; the Articles bind everyone.** Restrictions on transfer that live only in the SHA can be defeated by a shareholder who never signed it, and where SHA and AoA conflict, the AoA governs ([Sterling & Partners](https://sterlingpartners.law/shareholder-agreement-india-startup-sha-clauses/)). So: draft in the SHA, **mirror in the AoA**, and have the CS do it as a single exercise.

A useful caution about how far a "first refusal" can reach. In _Percept D'Mark (India) Pvt. Ltd. v. Zaheer Khan_ the Supreme Court struck down a right-of-first-refusal clause that bound a person's **future personal-service contracts after the agreement expired**, holding that it "contains a restrictive covenant in restraint of trade… and such a type of restriction extending beyond the tenure of the contract is clearly hit by Section 27 of the Contract Act and is void" ([Supreme Court, 22 March 2006](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/571375/)). That case is about restraining a _person_, not about a ROFR over _shares_ between shareholders — which is a restriction on dealing with property and stands on different ground. But it is the reason to have a lawyer draft the clause rather than copy one: the further a "first refusal" reaches into someone's future freedom to work, the closer it moves to §27.

### 6.8 Non-compete in India — get this right

This is the most commonly mis-drafted clause in Indian founders' agreements, because everyone copies an American template.

**Section 27 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872**: _"Every agreement by which any one is restrained from exercising a lawful profession, trade or business of any kind, is to that extent void."_ The only statutory exception in the section is the **sale of goodwill** — a seller of the goodwill of a business may agree to refrain from carrying on a similar business within specified local limits, so long as the buyer carries on a like business there and the court finds the limits reasonable ([Indian Contract Act §27, full text](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/1431516/)).

What follows from that:

| Restraint                                                                           | Enforceable in India?                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           | Authority                         |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| **During** employment or engagement — exclusivity, no moonlighting for a competitor | **Generally yes.** Treated "as a condition of exclusive service rather than a restraint of trade" ([Treelife](https://treelife.in/legal/enforceability-of-non-compete-clauses-in-india/))                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       | Long-settled                      |
| **After** employment ends — a post-employment non-compete                           | **Largely void, however narrow or short.** The Supreme Court in _Superintendence Company of India (P) Ltd v. Krishan Murgai_ held that "a service covenant extended beyond the termination of the service is void", rejecting the English "reasonableness" test as incompatible with the statute (1980 AIR 1717; (1981) 2 SCC 246) ([Supreme Court, 9 May 1980](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/1186410/)). Reaffirmed by the Delhi High Court in **Varun Tyagi v. Daffodil Software Pvt Ltd** (FAO 167/2025), holding the non-compete and non-solicitation clause void because it "extended beyond the term of employment" ([Mondaq, 2025](https://www.mondaq.com/india/employee-rights-labour-relations/1643996/section-27-of-the-indian-contract-act-varun-tyagi-judgment-and-delhi-high-courts-ruling-on-non-compete-clauses)) | Settled, and recently reaffirmed  |
| **Confidentiality / NDA** surviving departure                                       | **Yes**, where the employer can show it actually owns the information ([Treelife](https://treelife.in/legal/enforceability-of-non-compete-clauses-in-india/))                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   | The restraint that actually works |
| **Non-solicitation** of clients or staff                                            | **Sometimes** — enforced where narrowly drawn, identifying specific clients and time-bound ([Treelife](https://treelife.in/legal/enforceability-of-non-compete-clauses-in-india/)); but note Varun Tyagi struck down a combined non-solicit/non-compete that ran past employment. Draft it separately and narrowly, never bundled                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               | Mixed — draft carefully           |
| **Founder non-compete on a genuine sale of shares/goodwill**                        | **Better prospects.** Where founders sell equity and the buyer is genuinely paying for goodwill — reputation, client networks, brand — restraints of typically two to five years post-completion may hold under Exception 1, tested for proportionality in scope and duration; but the exception is read strictly, and a restraint designed to suppress competition while the promoters keep managerial control remains void ([Bhatt & Joshi Associates, 2026](https://bhattandjoshiassociates.com/non-compete-clauses-in-india-2026-enforceability-in-employment-contracts-vs-shareholder-agreements/))                                                                                                                                                                                                                        | Fact-specific — **VERIFY LIVE**   |

**The practical instruction for VSYST's founders' agreement.** Do not rely on a post-departure non-compete to protect the company; it will probably not be enforced. Rely instead on the stack that does work: **IP assignment** (so the code and the designs are the company's, not a founder's), **confidentiality** (so the dealer list and the pricing logic cannot walk), **a narrowly drawn non-solicit** naming client categories and a short window, **transfer restrictions and reverse vesting** (so leaving is economically unattractive), and — if a founder ever genuinely sells their stake — a goodwill-linked restraint drafted by a lawyer at that time. Write it this way and put a **VERIFY LIVE** note in C02 telling whoever fills it to have the clause reviewed, because the case law moves.

## 7. The family-founded company: the contract nobody writes

VSYST is three family founder-directors. That is a strength — trust, low coordination cost, shared risk tolerance — and it is a specific structural risk, because the company and the family share a decision-making surface. Tagiuri and Davis's **three-circle model**, developed at Harvard in the 1970s, is still the clearest way to see it: family, business and ownership are three overlapping circles, producing **seven distinct interest groups**, each with legitimate but different goals ([John Davis — the three-circle model](https://johndavis.com/three-circle-model-family-business-system/); [Family Business United](https://www.familybusinessunited.com/post/the-three-circle-model-central-to-family-business-understanding)). A brother who is a shareholder, an employee and a family member sits in the centre and holds three sets of interests that will not always agree — and none of the three is illegitimate.

The Indian evidence says this is where family firms actually lose. PwC's 12th Family Business Survey found Indian family businesses high on ambition and low on structure: **36% have no clear succession plan** (28% globally), **52% report "old guard" resistance** to next-generation leadership (29% globally), **21% have delayed succession outright** (10% globally), over half lack cross-industry board representation, and **30% have family-only boards** ([Forbes India on PwC's 12th survey, Feb 2026](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/news/indian-family-businesses-high-on-ambition-low-on-governance/2991239/1); [PwC India](https://www.pwc.in/services/entrepreneurial-and-private-business/pwcs-12th-family-business-survey.html); the pattern was already visible in [PwC's 10th survey](https://www.pwc.in/press-releases/2021/10th-global-family-business-survey-india-report.html)). Notably, **91% say they have clearly defined family values** — values are not the gap. Documents are.

So: the risks, and the specific clause or ritual that defuses each. These belong in the founders' agreement (§6) or in a short **family charter** that sits alongside it.

| The risk                                              | What it looks like at VSYST                                                                                                      | The clause or ritual that defuses it                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Business decided at family gatherings**             | Pricing, a hire, or a partner is half-decided over dinner; the founder who wasn't there hears about it as settled                | **The "no business at the table" rule, written and signed.** Any business question raised socially gets one sentence: _"put it on Monday's agenda."_ Paired with: a **real** minuted agenda every week, so the rule is not just a refusal but a redirection. If it is important enough to raise at dinner, it is important enough to minute                                                                                                  |
| **Salary conversations by family seniority**          | An older relative expects more because they are older; a younger one takes less to avoid a scene; nobody knows what anyone earns | **Role-based comp bands, written before anyone is hired** ([[C20-compensation-bands-and-esop-design\|C20]]). Salary attaches to the seat, not the surname. All founder salaries are visible to all founders. Changes go through the board with the interested director disclosing under §184 ([§184](https://ca2013.com/184-disclosure-of-interest-by-director/))                                                                            |
| **Nobody knows who may hire or fire a relative**      | A cousin joins on a Monday because a founder said yes at a wedding. Two years later nobody can end it                            | **A written rule with three parts**: (a) no relative is hired without the same scorecard and interview loop as anyone else ([[C19-executive-hiring-scorecard-and-loop\|C19]]); (b) hiring a relative requires **unanimous** founder agreement, minuted; (c) the authority to terminate a relative sits with the **CEO plus one non-related decider** — named in advance, in the document                                                     |
| **A family director cannot be performance-managed**   | There is no review, because a review would be an insult. Performance drifts and everyone works around it                         | **A written role charter per director** — outcomes, not effort — reviewed at the same quarterly board meeting for all three, in the same format, starting with the CEO. Order matters: the CEO's review goes first, every time. A review everyone gets is not an accusation                                                                                                                                                                  |
| **A family member underperforms and nothing happens** | The most damaging and the most common. Work quietly re-routes; resentment accumulates; the person is never told                  | **A defined consequence ladder, agreed in advance**: written expectations → a 90-day plan with named outcomes → change of role → separation of _employment_ from _shareholding_ (they remain a shareholder, they stop being an employee). The ladder must be written **before** it is needed and must apply to all three founders identically                                                                                                |
| **Shareholding and employment welded together**       | "I own a third, so I have a job and a salary and a vote on everything"                                                           | **Three separate documents**: shares (SHA/AoA), employment (a contract with a role and a notice period), and directorship (a board seat with duties under §166 — [§166](https://ca2013.com/166-duties-of-directors/)). One can end without the others. Say this explicitly in the founders' agreement: _ceasing to be an employee does not cease shareholding, and ceasing to be a shareholder does not automatically vacate the board seat_ |
| **Deadlock has no exit**                              | Two founders split on a reserved matter; the third is conflicted or absent; the question sits for months                         | **The outsider tiebreaker**, named by name in the agreement while everyone is calm: a specific person — a respected industry figure, the company's CA, a former employer, an advisory-board member — who is empowered to break a deadlock on a defined list of matters, whose decision the founders agree in advance to accept. Not a mediator; a decider. Choose someone whose "no" all three can absorb                                    |
| **The next generation, undiscussed**                  | A founder's child is assumed into the business, or assumed out of it, and nobody has said which                                  | **A written entry rule**: what qualification, what outside experience, what role, what process — agreed before any specific child is old enough for it to be about them. This is what PwC's 20%-with-a-documented-succession-plan gap actually is                                                                                                                                                                                            |

Two notes on doing this at VSYST specifically.

**Write it before there is money.** Every one of these clauses is easy to agree to today and nearly impossible to agree to once there is revenue to fight over. That is not a rhetorical point; it is the whole reason this lesson sits in Week 0.

**Make the CEO the first subject of every rule.** Whoever proposes the family charter should propose that their own review happens first, their own salary is banded first, and their own removal process is written first. It is the only way the document reads as governance rather than as a manoeuvre.

## 8. The disagreement protocol

One page, agreed once, used every time. It is deliberately identical in substance to [[03-the-coo-core-value-and-the-ceo-coo-contract|COO lesson 03]] §5.5 — **the same words must appear in both files**, because a protocol that differs by whose chair you are sitting in is not a protocol. Amazon's phrase for the discipline is "disagree and commit", and Bezos's framing is worth quoting exactly because it is not a demand for compliance but an offer: _"Look, I know we disagree on this but will you gamble with me on it? Disagree and commit?"_ ([Amazon 2016 shareholder letter](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2016-letter-to-shareholders)).

```
VSYST DISAGREEMENT PROTOCOL                            v1 · agreed __________

1. ARGUE IN PRIVATE, IN WRITING FIRST.
   The person raising it writes one page before the conversation:
   issue -> options -> recommendation -> what would change my mind.
   ("Writing vs talking" — Mochary, The Great CEO Within.)
   No disagreement is aired in front of a third person before this page exists.

2. DECIDE BY THE NAMED DECIDER.
   Look up the decision in C03 / T01 / T22 and use the level it says:
     decide alone   -> the owner decides now and informs
     consult        -> the owner decides after hearing the other, and may overrule
     joint          -> both must agree; if still split after TWO conversations,
                       the pre-agreed tie-break applies (choose one, now):
                         [ ] the CEO decides and the others commit
                         [ ] the third director decides
                         [ ] the named outsider tiebreaker decides   (see s.7)
     escalate       -> board of three directors, minuted; or the outside adviser
   Reversible (two-way door): decide fast, log, set a review date.
   Irreversible (one-way door): slow, joint, board, and a written pre-mortem.

3. BOTH PRESENT IT AS OURS.
   Outside the room there is one position. Nobody says "I was against this."
   The person who lost the argument explains the decision at least once,
   in their own words, to the team or the dealer. That is the test that it landed.

4. LOG IT THE SAME DAY.
   C29 entry: date · decision · decider · options rejected · why ·
   one-way or two-way · what would make us revisit · REVIEW DATE.
   No log entry = the decision did not happen.

5. REVISIT ONLY ON NEW EVIDENCE, AT A NAMED TRIGGER.
   Write the trigger when you decide, not later. Examples:
     "revisit if churn > X by 31 Dec"   "revisit if IOCL responds in writing"
     "revisit at the Q2 review"          "revisit if the third dealer says the same thing"
   Re-opening a decision without hitting its trigger is itself a decision,
   and needs its own C29 entry saying why the rule was broken.

6. THE FAMILY CLAUSE.
   No step of this protocol happens at a family gathering, on a family group,
   or in front of a relative who is not a director.
   Anything raised there gets exactly one reply: "put it on Monday's agenda."
```

Two things make this work that look like details and are not. **The tie-break is chosen in advance**, when the question is abstract — choosing it during a live argument is choosing a winner, and everyone knows it. And **step 3 is a behaviour, not a feeling**: making the person who lost explain the decision is uncomfortable, and it is the single most reliable way to find out whether "disagree and commit" actually happened or was merely said.

## 9. At VSYST — applying this now

- **Write the CEO charter before the strategy.** [[C01-ceo-charter-and-90-day-plan|C01]] takes an hour and settles four things that otherwise get settled by accident: which of the six values you personally own vs share, the seven scorecard rows with first targets, the rich-or-king answer, and the written trigger for adding an outside director. Do it before [[05-strategy-i-diagnosis-and-the-strategy-kernel|lesson 05]], because a strategy written by an undefined seat gets re-litigated by whoever is unhappy with it.
- **Sign the founders' agreement this month, imperfectly.** A signed 80% document beats a perfect unsigned one, with one exception: the **IP assignment** and **vesting** clauses must be right the first time, because they are the two that cannot be retrofitted after a dispute starts. Have the CS and a startup lawyer review those two specifically; take the rest from [[C02-founders-agreement-and-vesting-checklist|C02]]. Between family founders a signature feels absurd — the [[startup-operations-plan|Startup Operations Plan]] already makes the case: co-founder conflict kills more startups than competition does, and the founder agreement "is the mechanism that lets you disagree safely."
- **Ask, and record, the question that makes the COO seat real.** "Which operational decisions will you stop making?" ([Alder Koten](https://charliesolorzano.me/2026/03/17/coo-founder-led-company-failure/)). Verbatim into [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] and into [[T01-ceo-coo-operating-agreement|T01]] §B, with a transfer date per decision. If the honest answer today is "none", log that with a review date — an empty list that is written down is a fact you can act on; an empty list you never wrote is a story you tell yourself.
- **Start the free accountability now.** The monthly written update ([[C23-stakeholder-and-investor-update|C23]]) costs an hour and is the only item in §3.4 with no prerequisites. Send the first one at the end of this month, to your co-founders and your CA, including the parts that went badly.
- **Name the outsider tiebreaker while nothing is wrong.** One name, in the founders' agreement, for deadlock. This is a fifteen-minute conversation today and an impossible one in eighteen months.
- **Failure modes to watch, in order of likelihood here:** #1 (a technical CEO staying an engineer — the most comfortable failure), #8 (the dinner-table/boardroom collapse — the most structural), #6 (being the only one who knows things — the easiest to fix and the most neglected), and #5 (running out of cash while believing you had time — the one with no warning shot at a bootstrapped company).

Put it on the next board agenda for ratification. **Artefact:** a first-cut C03 and a line on the next [[C17-board-pack-agenda-and-minutes|C17]] agenda.

**10.4 — The decisions the CEO will stop making this quarter (20 min, written).** Not a discussion — a list. Write every recurring operational decision that currently routes through you: vendor choices, tool spend, support wording, refunds, contractor engagement, meeting cadence, policy wording, dealer promises inside SLA. For each, write: **who becomes the decider**, **the date it transfers**, and **the limit it transfers with**. Start with the least emotionally loaded (vendors, finance operations, compliance) and leave product and key relationships for a later quarter. Paste the list verbatim into [[T01-ceo-coo-operating-agreement|T01]] §B and open one [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] entry per decision with a day-90 review date. **Artefact:** the list, in T01 and in C29 — and a calendar reminder on day 90 to check which transfers actually happened.

**10.5 — Agree the disagreement protocol and the family clause (25 min, all three founders).** Copy the §8 block into the founders' working folder. Fill the blanks: which tie-break applies to "joint" decisions, and — by name — who the outsider tiebreaker is. Then walk it backwards through **one real disagreement you have already had**: which step would have caught it, and where it actually went instead. Confirm the identical text sits in [[T01-ceo-coo-operating-agreement|T01]] §E so the COO course and this course cannot drift. **Artefact:** an agreed protocol with the tie-break and the tiebreaker named, signed by all three, and a C29 entry recording the worked example.
