## Explain-it-like-I'm-5

There is a spare-parts shop in Raipur's Transport Nagar. Ramesh runs it. He sells filters, belts, brake linings and bulbs to the truck operators whose vehicles park in the same three lanes. He knows every mechanic by name. When a driver needs a part at nine at night, Ramesh opens the shutter.

One day a company's area sales manager walks in and mentions, half in passing, that they are appointing an authorised dealer for the district. Ramesh cannot sleep. An authorised signboard would change everything — the big fleets buy only from authorised dealers, the credit terms are better, and his father would finally see the family name under a national brand. So he starts going to Bhilai for meetings. He fills forms. He gets a bank guarantee quoted. He waits for calls. Nine months pass, and in those nine months two things happen: the signboard does not arrive, and his shelves quietly empty, because the man who used to open the shutter at nine at night has been on a bus to Bhilai.

Here is the part that matters. The signboard was a real prize and Ramesh was not a fool to want it. His mistake was in what he stopped doing while he chased it — and in never writing down, on paper, before he started, the date on which he would give up. His second mistake was smaller and more common: he thought the meetings were the relationship. They were not. The relationship was the area manager's belief that Ramesh could move stock, and the only evidence for that belief was sitting on the shelves he was letting empty.

Every partnership chapter in a founder's life is that story. The prize is real. The cost is invisible, it is paid in the founder's own hours, and it is paid out of the one thing that would have made the partnership happen anyway.

## 1. Which relationships the CEO must hold — and which the CEO must stop holding

A founder-CEO is structurally the company's most expensive employee and its most credible one. Both facts have to appear in the same sentence, because the whole art here is spending credibility without wasting hours.

Start by separating two words that founders use interchangeably and should not.

> **Holding** a relationship means you are the person the other side's senior person thinks of, trusts, and calls. **Running** a relationship means you send the follow-ups, keep the tracker row current, book the meetings and chase the document. The CEO holds. Somebody else runs. When the CEO is doing both, either the relationship is genuinely new or the CEO has quietly taken a job.

The COO course is unambiguous about the running half: one named owner per partnership, a tracker row with a stage, a last-touch date and a next step with a date, and a 30-day verdict of push-or-park at the ops meeting ([[11-revenue-operations-and-partnerships|COO 11]] §9). That machinery is not repeated here. This lesson is about the other half — which relationships belong on the CEO's own desk, what the CEO does with them that nobody else can, and how to tell the difference between a relationship and a habit.

### 1.1 The four-question test

Ask these four questions about any counterparty. **Two or more yeses and the CEO holds it.** Fewer than two and the CEO is doing someone else's job — and, worse, is teaching the counterparty that they get the CEO whenever they want.

| #   | Question                                                              | Why it discriminates                                                                                                                                                                             |
| --- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1   | **Does the other side send someone senior?**                          | Seniority is answered with seniority. A DGM will not build a working relationship with a coordinator, and a dealer's father will not sign because a field executive was polite.                  |
| 2   | **Could this change the shape of the company — not just add a task?** | Anything that changes who the customer is, what the product is, who owns the data, or where the revenue comes from is a strategy decision wearing a partnership costume.                         |
| 3   | **Does it run on personal trust that does not transfer?**             | If the counterparty's confidence is in _a person_, handing it over is not delegation, it is a downgrade the other side will feel and resent.                                                     |
| 4   | **Is the cost of a wrong sentence irreversible?**                     | Some rooms let you promise something you cannot deliver, or concede something you cannot take back — exclusivity, a data right, a price. Those rooms need the person who can say no on the spot. |

The test has an unfashionable corollary. **Most relationships fail this test**, including many that feel important. A cloud vendor's account manager, a SaaS renewal, an ordinary supplier, a recruiter, a conference organiser — none of these produce two yeses, and every hour spent on them is an hour not spent on the ones that do. Those belong to the COO's vendor register and renewal calendar ([[14-vendors-procurement-and-cost-control|COO 14]] §1–§4), and the CEO should be visibly absent from them.

Two things to notice about this table before you fill your own version.

**Row 8 is the point of the exercise.** Cloud and tooling vendors score zero and should therefore never appear on the CEO's calendar except at a renewal decision above the delegation limit. If your version of this table puts the CEO on a call with a hosting account manager, the table has just told you where a week is going.

**The backup column is mostly empty, and that is the finding.** VSYST's fuel-domain relationships live almost entirely in one director's three decades of standing in the trade. That is a genuine cornered resource ([[06-strategy-ii-moats-positioning-and-the-business-model|lesson 06]]) and a single point of failure at the same time. The cheapest fix is not a hire; it is a written list — every named relationship, the person's role, where they sit, the history, the last conversation — kept where two directors can read it. Do that before anything in this lesson gets ambitious.

### 1.3 What the CEO actually does with a held relationship

Holding is not attending. Four things only, and they are the four nobody else can do:

1. **Say the strategic sentence.** "We will not build a bespoke module for one divisional office." "We will not hand you our dealers' customer data." "We can start with two pumps in 30 days." Those sentences commit the company, so they need the person who is allowed to commit it.
2. **Absorb a no without damage.** A founder can be told no and keep the room warm. An employee usually cannot, because a no lands on them as a personal failure and they start negotiating against their own company to avoid it.
3. **Change the shape of the deal in the room.** The whole value of seniority in an Indian negotiating room is the ability to restructure on the spot — smaller scope, different phase, a different counterparty inside the same organisation.
4. **Decide to stop.** §10. Nobody below the founder will ever kill a partnership the founder started, and everybody knows it.

Everything else — minutes, decks, follow-ups, documents, scheduling, chasing — is running, and belongs to the tracker.

## 2. The Indian fuel-distribution ecosystem, mapped properly

You cannot judge a partnership in an industry you have only met through your own customers. This section is the map. Read it once as geography, then use it as a checklist: for each node, VSYST is either a **channel** (they can bring you customers), a **gate** (they can stop you), or **noise** (interesting, irrelevant this year).

The population at the bottom of that chain is large and countable. India crossed **1,00,266 retail outlets** by late 2025, the world's third-largest network, with IOCL at roughly **41,664 outlets (41.5%)**, BPCL at **24,605 (24.5%)** and HPCL at **24,418 (24.3%)** — the three PSU marketers running about **90.5%** of it, with Nayara, Jio-bp and Shell holding most of the private balance ([Energy Watch — India's petrol pump count crosses 1 lakh](https://www.energywatch.in/oil-and-gas/indias-petrol-pump-count-crosses-1-lakh-nearing-us-china-in-network-scale), Dec 2025; corroborated in the vault's own [[03_Market_Analysis|market analysis]]). State-wise counts, including Chhattisgarh's, come from PPAC's downloadable state-wise retail-outlets file — **VERIFY LIVE** at [PPAC — Retail Outlets](https://ppac.gov.in/infrastructure/retail-outlets) before any number of yours goes into a partner document, because that file is refreshed and your counterparty knows the current figure.

### 2.2 Inside an OMC — the org chart you will actually meet

An oil marketing company is not one counterparty. It is at least four, and they have different budgets, different incentives and different words for yes.

The field structure is public, because it is the structure through which dealerships are advertised and awarded: the OMC dealer-selection portal lists field offices as **Divisional Offices for IOC, Territory Offices for BPC and Regional Offices for HPC**, each with an address, a phone number and a state ([Petrol Pump Dealer Chayan — Area Offices](https://www.petrolpumpdealerchayan.in/area-offices)). Above them sit State Offices and the Marketing Division head office; below them sit the field officers who visit the forecourt.

| Layer                                                                  | Typical title you will meet                    | What they genuinely control                                                                                  | What they cannot do                              | Your realistic ask                                                    |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Field officer / Sales officer / Territory manager**                  | Sales Officer, Territory Manager               | Monthly outlet visits, inspections, the dealer's day-to-day; their opinion carries authority with the dealer | Commit the company to anything; sign; spend      | An introduction, a hearing, permission to be present at a dealer meet |
| **Area / Divisional / Territory / Regional Office**                    | Chief Divisional Retail Sales Manager and team | The district's outlets; local pilots; who gets attention                                                     | A national programme; procurement                | **A named, written pilot at N dealers inside one division**           |
| **State Office**                                                       | State Head (Retail)                            | State-level programmes and priorities                                                                        | Corporate IT, procurement, IT security clearance | Sponsorship of the divisional pilot upward                            |
| **Marketing Division HO / IS (Information Systems) / Retail Business** | GM / ED level                                  | National programmes, integrations, systems                                                                   | Move fast                                        | An evaluation, a PoC slot, a place in a roadmap                       |
| **Materials / Procurement / Contracts**                                | Purchase, Contracts, e-tender cell             | Vendor registration, EOIs, tenders, purchase orders                                                          | Decide that they want your product               | Empanelment in the right category                                     |
| **Start-up / innovation cell**                                         | Start-up Fund team                             | A funded proof-of-concept                                                                                    | Buy your product                                 | Selection in an open-innovation challenge                             |

**The rule that this table exists to teach:** _the officer who can say yes to a pilot is never the officer who can say yes to a contract, and the officer who can say yes to a contract has never met your product._ Every OMC conversation must therefore be run as two threads at once — a field thread that produces evidence, and a corporate thread that produces a procurement route — and the field thread must come first, because the corporate thread has no idea what to do with a company that has nothing to show.

One more structural fact you must know before you speak in any OMC room. The relationship between an OMC and its dealer is governed by the **Marketing Discipline Guidelines (MDG)**, a rulebook with inspections, penalties and termination clauses — and it is contested, not merely accepted. In _All India Petroleum Dealers Association v. Union of India_ the Delhi High Court struck down parts of the October 2017 MDG amendments, including penalty provisions and mandated obligations that went beyond statutory requirements, while upholding quality standards on delivery ([Indian Kanoon — AIPDA v. Union of India, 18 March 2020](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/62133942/)) — **VERIFY LIVE** the current MDG text and any subsequent appeal before relying on it. What matters for you: your customer lives under a penal rulebook administered by the counterparty you are courting. Anything your software records is, in the dealer's mind, potentially evidence. That single sentence explains more dealer objections than any feature list.

**The Chhattisgarh gap is itself a finding.** Open sources do not readily yield a named, currently-active Chhattisgarh state petroleum dealers' body with a public presence — which does not mean one does not exist; it means the district and state bodies here are organised through people rather than websites. **VERIFY LIVE with the domain-expert director**, who will know the office-bearers by name, and write the names into the relationship map in §1.2. Treat the absence of a website as good news: a channel your competitors cannot Google is a channel your competitors cannot use.

The discipline that keeps association work from becoming theatre: **never buy a stage before you have five referenceable dealers to put on it.** Sponsoring an association meet with nothing to demonstrate purchases one afternoon of goodwill at the price of a month of runway. Bringing five of their own members to say something true purchases a district.

### 2.4 The customer's side — transport and fleet bodies

The dealer's B2B customers have their own apex structure, and it is older and larger than the dealers'. The **All India Motor Transport Congress (AIMTC)**, founded in **1936**, is a non-political apex body of more than **3,500 taluka, district and state-level federations and transport associations**, representing roughly **95 lakh truckers and 50 lakh bus and tourist operators** ([AIMTC — About Us](https://www.aimtc.org/about-us/)). Underneath it sit district truck-operators' unions and transport-nagar associations, which is where a fleet owner in Raipur actually belongs.

For VSYST this side is strategically important and tactically premature. The transporter is the **hard side of the network** — no incentive to onboard, lower digital literacy, infrequent interaction ([[11_EDUCATION_GAP|education-gap analysis]]) — so a transport association is not a route to revenue this year. It is a route to _credibility with dealers_, which is different and cheaper: a district transport body publicly saying "we want quantity assurance and one ledger" is the strongest push force a dealer can hear, and it comes from the customer, not from you.

Two conclusions come out of this table and they should shape the next quarter.

**First: fleet cards are the ceiling of the current digital layer, and they stop short of the ledger.** XTRAPOWER solves cashless purchase and rewards; it does not maintain the dealer-and-customer common ledger, bill-to-bill settlement, or credit limits by amount and period. **Second: the highest-value under-used channel is the service engineer, not the association.** He is inside every pump quarterly, has no software to sell, is trusted on technical matters, and is invisible to every competitor because he does not appear in any acquisition funnel. That is a channel worth three founder-hours a month for a quarter, as an experiment with a written outcome.

## 3. The OMC relationship — the biggest prize and the most dangerous distraction

### 3.1 There are three doors, not one

| Door                     | What it is                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               | Who opens it                                    | What it produces                                      | Realistic elapsed time                                                                                                 |

The form is not the relationship. **The field door is the only one VSYST can open this quarter, and evidence produced behind it is the only thing that makes the other two answerable.**

### 3.2 What empanelment unlocks — and what it demands

Read the last three rows together. **Empanelment is not blocked by IOCL's opinion of DZZLO. It is blocked by VSYST's balance sheet and its security paperwork** — both of which are fixable on a schedule, neither of which is fixed by another meeting. That reframing is the single most useful thing this section can do for the CEO's calendar.

### 3.3 Why it is simultaneously the biggest prize

Be honest about the upside, because a distraction you have not respected will keep pulling at you. An OMC relationship is the only route VSYST has to three things it cannot manufacture:

1. **Distribution at a scale no district-by-district motion reaches** — 41,664 outlets under one brand.
2. **A cornered resource**: authority. Dealers are conditioned to accept systems that arrive from above ([[07-customers-markets-and-founder-led-sales|lesson 07]] §2.3). The same product carries a different weight when the divisional office has blessed it.
3. **A data position** nobody else can occupy: the credit book that never reaches the OMC. The vault's own framing — roughly 60% of sale on credit while only about 20% flows through the loyalty rails, leaving the rest in ledgers the OMC never sees — is the most interesting sentence VSYST has ever written to IOCL ([[discussion-document|the IOCL discussion document]] §3.6). **VERIFY LIVE** those percentages with the domain-expert director before they are quoted to anyone; they are field estimates, not published statistics, and they must be labelled as such in any document that leaves the building.

### 3.4 And why it is the most dangerous distraction

| The trap                        | How it feels from inside                                                        | The tell                                                                                |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Altitude drift**              | Each meeting is with someone more senior, which feels like progress             | Nobody in the chain has a budget line for you                                           |
| **The document treadmill**      | You are asked for another deck, another note, another comparison                | The asks grow, the commitments don't. Count documents produced vs commitments received  |
| **Free consulting**             | Your analysis of their problem is genuinely good, and they are learning from it | You have described their gaps in more detail than they have described your scope        |
| **The roadmap hostage**         | "If you could also do X, we could take this further"                            | You are building against an unfunded hypothetical for a customer who has signed nothing |
| **The stopped clock**           | Six months pass warmly                                                          | There is no dated next step in writing, only a good feeling                             |
| **Opportunity cost, invisible** | You did not notice what you stopped doing                                       | Districts not visited, pilots not run, losses not read                                  |

The strategic guardrail is already written in the COO course and it is worth repeating verbatim here because it settles arguments: _if the district plan only works when IOCL says yes, it is not a plan_ ([[11-revenue-operations-and-partnerships|COO 11]] §9). Partnerships accelerate a machine that already sells. They do not substitute for one.

### 3.5 The written test before you spend another quarter

Run this every quarter on any partnership above a certain size, and specifically on the IOCL thread. Write the answers down, dated, in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]]. **All seven must pass. Six out of seven is a fail, not a near-miss.**

```
THE SEVEN-QUESTION QUARTERLY TEST                    Partner: ____________  Date: ______

1. NAMED PERSON      Who, by name and designation, wants this to happen inside their
                     organisation? ______________________________
                     Is it their job to want it?              [ ] yes  [ ] no

2. BUDGET OR AUTHORITY  Can that person authorise a pilot, a PoC or a purchase without
                     a committee we have never met?           [ ] yes  [ ] no

3. WRITTEN NEXT STEP  Is there a next step in THEIR words, with a date, in writing
                     (email, minutes they acknowledged, a letter)?   [ ] yes  [ ] no
                     Paste it here: ______________________________

4. ARTEFACT          Do we hold one document with their name or letterhead on it —
                     an email confirming scope, an EOI, a challenge acceptance?  [ ] yes [ ] no

5. THE NO TEST       If they said a clean no tomorrow, what in our plan changes?
                     ______________________________
                     If the honest answer is "nothing", the relationship is optionality,
                     not strategy — cap it at 4 senior hours a month.
                     If the honest answer is "everything", the plan is broken (§3.4).

6. THE PRICE         Senior hours spent last quarter: ______ .  Cap for next quarter: ______ .
                     What did NOT happen because of those hours? ______________________

7. THE LINE          Written walk-away: "If, by ____________ [date], we do not have
                     ____________________ [a specific artefact], we park this and
                     revisit on ____________ [date]."
```

Question 5 is the one that does the work. It splits every partnership into **optionality** (nice, cap it, keep it warm cheaply) and **dependency** (dangerous, fix the plan). Most founders discover their biggest partnership is optionality that they have been resourcing like dependency.

### 3.6 The smallest real ask

When the field door opens, do not ask for an MoU. Ask for the smallest thing that is genuinely real:

> **A named pilot: N dealers under one divisional office, a written scope of what DZZLO will and will not do, a start date and a review date, one named officer who will look at the output, and a one-page note on what data is shared and what is not.**

That ask is answerable by a person who exists, inside one financial year, without procurement. It produces the only currency that moves the other two doors: a divisional officer who can say, internally, "I have seen it working at four of my outlets." An MoU produces a PDF.

### 3.7 The risk nobody warns a founder about: you already hold their dealers

This is the part of the OMC relationship that is genuinely a one-way door, and it needs the CEO in the room.

VSYST's dealers are IOCL's dealers. The data those dealers put into DZZLO includes their B2B customers, their credit terms, their discounts and their off-take — precisely the information the vault's own analysis says the OMC cannot see today. So the moment the relationship becomes real, some version of this sentence will be said: _share the data._

Three positions, and you must choose before you are asked:

| Position                           | What it means                                                                                                             | Consequence                                                                                                                                                                          |
| ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Dealer-owned, dealer-consented** | The dealer's data is the dealer's. Anything that flows to the OMC flows because that dealer switched it on, with a record | Slower, defensible, consistent with DPDP consent duties and with what you promise dealers in the sales conversation ([[07-customers-markets-and-founder-led-sales\|lesson 07]] §6.4) |
| **Aggregate-only**                 | Only de-identified, aggregated views leave the tenant                                                                     | Useful to an OMC; safe; sometimes not what they want                                                                                                                                 |
| **Platform-to-OMC feed**           | You pipe tenant data upward as a product feature                                                                          | You have changed who the customer is, and the first dealer who finds out will tell the district                                                                                      |

**The house call for VSYST: position one, written down, before the next IOCL meeting, and said out loud in that meeting.** It costs nothing to say early and it is very expensive to concede late. It is also, in a market where trust is the entire moat, a differentiator: you are the vendor who told the OMC no in front of the dealer.

The second half of the same risk: an OMC relationship can quietly redefine the product from _the dealer's system_ into _the OMC's system for dealers_. Those are different companies with different pricing, different buyers and different moats. Deciding to become the second one is legitimate. Drifting into it is not. That decision belongs in [[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager|C04]] and [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]], not in a meeting room.

## 4. Partnership types — which are real, and which are rewards

Founders talk about "partnerships" as one thing. They are eight things with different economics, different failure modes and different prerequisites. The last column is the one to read first.

| Type                        | What it actually is                                                                                      | What it costs you                                                   | Classic failure                                                                     | Fit at VSYST today                                                                                                                                                                  |
| --------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Referral**                | Someone sends you a name; you pay for outcomes                                                           | Small, variable, only on success                                    | Paying on signature rather than activation; unpaid referrers who talk               | **Yes — now.** The dealer-to-dealer loop is designed and owned operationally by [[11-revenue-operations-and-partnerships\|COO 11]] §10                                              |
| **Channel / reseller**      | A third party sells and sometimes supports; they own the customer conversation                           | Margin (20–40% typical), training, conflict with your own selling   | Signing resellers before you can sell it yourself, so nobody can                    | **No — trigger:** 20 direct paying GSTINs and a stable script ([[07-customers-markets-and-founder-led-sales\|lesson 07]] §9.1)                                                      |
| **Tech integration**        | Your product plugs into theirs — gateway, IRP, e-way bill, banking, Tally/ERPNext                        | Engineering weeks, permanent maintenance                            | Building an integration nobody asked for, to make a partner page look full          | **Selectively yes.** Payments is the one that unblocks the product loop (§11)                                                                                                       |
| **Co-marketing**            | Joint webinars, case studies, logos, events                                                              | Founder time, little money                                          | Both parties wait for the other's audience                                          | **No.** VSYST has no audience to trade                                                                                                                                              |
| **OEM / white-label**       | They sell your product under their name                                                                  | Your brand, your customer relationship, your pricing power          | You become a supplier to a company that can replace you                             | **No, and it is on the disqualification list** ([[07-customers-markets-and-founder-led-sales\|lesson 07]] §5.3)                                                                     |
| **Association partnership** | A dealers' or transporters' body endorses, hosts, or circulates                                          | Membership, sponsorship, sometimes a fee                            | Paying for a stage with nothing to show on it                                       | **District: yes. State/national: not yet** (§2.3)                                                                                                                                   |
| **Enterprise / OMC**        | A large counterparty adopts or integrates                                                                | Senior hours, years, security paperwork                             | §3.4, all six rows                                                                  | **Field door only** (§3.6)                                                                                                                                                          |
| **Embedded finance / data** | Your ledger becomes the basis for credit — invoice-backed lending, receivable factoring, an NBFC partner | A regulated counterparty, a data-rights decision, real product work | Selling a lender's product to your own customers before you have earned their trust | **No — trigger:** twelve months of clean ledger history across ≥50 tenants. It is written as an ambition in [[discussion-document\|the IOCL discussion document]] §6; keep it there |

### 4.1 The rule that sorts them

> **A partnership that only works once your product is popular is not a partnership. It is a reward.** Co-marketing, white-label, national associations and embedded finance are all rewards. Referral, one integration, and one field pilot are partnerships. Do the second list; write the first list on the no-list and stop discussing it.

### 4.2 The one-at-a-time rule

At three people, with one of them writing the code, the honest capacity is:

> **One partnership in "build" state at a time. Everything else is in "warm" state — a quarterly message, a tracker row, no engineering, no travel, no documents.**

Build state means engineering hours, travel, or documents drafted for a counterparty. Warm state costs about twenty minutes a month. The failure mode this rule prevents is the one VSYST is closest to right now: two partnerships in build state (IOCL and the gateway) plus a founder-led sales motion that is supposed to be the strategy.

## 5. Evaluating a partnership before you say yes

### 5.1 The scored screen

Score any proposed partnership out of 100 before the second meeting. The rubric lives in [[C26-partnership-evaluation-and-mou-checklist|C26]]; the discipline is the same as the ICP rubric in [[07-customers-markets-and-founder-led-sales|lesson 07]] §5.2 — you are buying yourself permission to decline without guilt.

| 2   | **Named champion with authority**         | nobody                         | interested individual | named, no budget           | named, can authorise                                             | ×15    | §3.5 question 1–2                                                                                    |

| 4   | **Who does the work**                     | all us                         | mostly us             | shared                     | mostly them                                                      | ×10    | The side doing the work is the side that cares                                                       |
| 5   | **Reversibility**                         | one-way door                   | hard to unwind        | 12-month exit              | exit any time, data ours                                         | ×10    | [[02-how-a-ceo-thinks\|Lesson 02]]'s door test applies to partners                                   |

**Bands.** ≥70: run it, one at a time (§4.2). 45–69: warm state only — a quarterly message and a tracker row. <45: decline in writing, warmly, with the trigger that would change your answer.

### 5.2 Partnership as procrastination — the diagnostic

Business development is the most comfortable form of avoidance available to a founder, because it produces meetings that feel like progress and cannot be falsified for months. Three questions catch it:

1. **Would you rather have this meeting than visit a pump on Tuesday?** If yes, notice why. Partner meetings are air-conditioned, flattering and free of rejection. Pump visits produce truth and occasional humiliation. Your calendar will drift toward the first without your permission.
2. **In the last 60 days, has this partnership produced a single artefact with the counterparty's name on it?** Not your notes — theirs. If not, you have a correspondence, not a partnership.
3. **If a competitor got this exact partnership tomorrow, what would break for you?** If the honest answer is "not much", the partnership is not a moat, and the hours should go to the district.

Paul Graham's rule about doing things that don't scale applies here in reverse: recruiting users manually is unscalable and correct; recruiting _partners_ manually is unscalable and usually a way to avoid recruiting users ([Paul Graham — Do Things that Don't Scale](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html), 2013). The check on both is the same — which activity produced a fact this week.

### 5.3 The kill criterion, written before the first meeting

Every partnership above 45 points gets a written kill criterion **before** work starts, not after it stalls. One sentence, dated, in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]]:

> _"If by **[date]** we do not have **[the specific artefact — a signed pilot scope, a dealer live on the gateway, an EOI response, a named officer's written confirmation]**, we park this, tell them so in writing, and revisit on **[date]**."_

Two properties make it work. It names an **artefact**, not a feeling. And it names a **revisit date**, so parking is not a funeral — which is what lets you actually do it in a market where you will meet these people again.

## 6. Running a partner relationship

### 6.1 The first meeting, run to a script

Partner meetings fail for the opposite reason sales meetings fail. In a sales meeting the founder talks too much about the product; in a partner meeting the founder talks too much about the _possibilities_. The counterparty leaves impressed and uncommitted.

**Run-of-show — an OMC divisional / area office meeting (30 minutes, assume 20).**

| Min     | What you do                                                                                                                                                                               | What you must not do                                         |
| ------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 0–2     | Who you are, where the office is, whose dealer network you already sit inside, and the fact that the family has run a retail outlet for decades                                           | Open with the product, or with a deck                        |
| 2–6     | **One problem, in their vocabulary, from their side of the table.** Their off-take visibility, their loyalty-programme conversion, their dealer complaints — not the dealer's ledger pain | Describe six problems. Six problems reads as a lecture       |
| 6–10    | **Three minutes of live product on a phone**, using a real dealer's real (consented) screen — the shared ledger and the OTP-bound delivery                                                | Show architecture, roadmap, or the word "platform"           |
| 10–14   | **The ask**, small and specific: a pilot at N outlets in this division, scope written down, a review date                                                                                 | Ask for an MoU, an integration, or a meeting with their boss |
| 14–18   | **The data sentence** (§3.7), unprompted: what is the dealer's, what can be shared, what needs consent                                                                                    | Wait to be asked. Volunteering it is the whole point         |
| 18–20   | Written next step, in their words, agreed out loud, before you stand up                                                                                                                   | Leave with "we'll be in touch"                               |
| +24 hrs | A short email that repeats the next step and the date, and nothing else                                                                                                                   | Attach a 30-page document                                    |

**Run-of-show — a channel partner (CA, Tally partner, service engineer, association office-bearer).** Same shape, three differences: lead with _what their customer says to them_ rather than what you do; put the commercial on the table in the first meeting, because ambiguity about money poisons these relationships fast; and end with a **single named test case** — one dealer they will introduce you to this month — rather than a programme.

### 6.2 The pilot is the only honest instrument

Everything true of customer pilots ([[07-customers-markets-and-founder-led-sales|lesson 07]] §7) is true of partner pilots, plus one rule of its own.

| Rule                                      | Partner version                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| ----------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Dated at both ends**                    | Start date and review date agreed before day zero, in writing                                                                                                                                         |
| **Numeric success, agreed first**         | "Four outlets, 30 days, ≥20 credit customers on the ledger, ≥100 OTP-verified deliveries, one officer's written read-out"                                                                             |
| **Named owner on their side**             | A person, with a phone number. A pilot owned by "the division" is owned by nobody                                                                                                                     |
| **Written exit**                          | What happens to data and access if it stops. Say it out loud, early                                                                                                                                   |
| **New: no product work inside the pilot** | The pilot tests the product that exists. If it needs a build to start, it is not a pilot, it is a project — and it needs a charter ([[T18-project-charter\|T18]]) and a decision memo, not enthusiasm |

### 6.3 MoU, and what it is not

An MoU is a statement of intent. It is not a contract, not revenue, not a customer and not a fact ([[11-revenue-operations-and-partnerships|COO 11]] §9). It has exactly two legitimate uses: giving a mid-level champion inside a large organisation a piece of paper to show upward, and forcing both sides to write down what they actually mean. It has one illegitimate use, which is making a founder feel that a quarter was productive.

If you sign one, three clauses earn their place: it is **non-binding except** for confidentiality and any cost-bearing; it is **non-exclusive**; and it has an **expiry date**. Everything else in an MoU is decoration.

### 6.4 The commercial terms that actually matter

In descending order of how much damage they do when wrong:

| Term                                | Why it matters more than price                       | The default to hold                                                                                                  |
| ----------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Who owns the customer**           | Decides whether you have a business or a subcontract | VSYST holds the contract, the tenant and the support relationship                                                    |
| **Data rights**                     | The one-way door (§3.7)                              | Tenant data belongs to the tenant; sharing is consented and logged                                                   |
| **Exclusivity**                     | Cheap to give, impossible to take back               | None. If unavoidable: narrow field, small territory, short term, revenue-conditioned                                 |
| **Term and termination**            | Decides whether a mistake is a quarter or a decade   | Twelve months, terminable for convenience with notice                                                                |
| **What "partner" may say publicly** | Your credibility in one district                     | Nothing about your customers or numbers without written approval                                                     |
| **Support obligations**             | Where a three-person company dies                    | Explicit hours, explicit channel, explicit exclusions                                                                |
| **IP**                              | You built it                                         | You own everything you built, including anything built during the pilot                                              |
| **Commercials**                     | Real, but recoverable                                | Referral fee on activation and collection, not on signature ([[11-revenue-operations-and-partnerships\|COO 11]] §10) |

### 6.5 Cadence, and who owns the verdict

| Beat                                   | Who          | What happens                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| -------------------------------------- | ------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Within 24 hours of any partner meeting | Runner       | Note into the tracker; next step and date confirmed to the counterparty in writing                                                                                                                                            |
| Monthly                                | Runner       | Tracker refresh: stage, last touch, next step                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| **Monthly, at the ops meeting**        | COO function | Any partnership with no movement in 30 days gets **push or park** — never "stalled but hopeful" ([[11-revenue-operations-and-partnerships\|COO 11]] §9)                                                                       |
| **Quarterly**                          | **CEO**      | The seven-question test (§3.5) on every partnership above 45 points; the kill criteria re-read; hours capped for next quarter                                                                                                 |
| On any request for real commitment     | **CEO**      | A decision memo — engineering time, exclusivity, revenue share or data access are never verbal ([[T09-decision-log-and-adr\|T09]] operationally; [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log\|C29]] for the CEO's own record) |

The split, stated once so it never has to be argued: **the COO's machinery keeps partnerships moving; the CEO's quarterly test decides whether they should be.**

### 6.6 The crossover — when an MoU has to become a real agreement

An MoU and a commercial agreement are not two grades of the same document. They answer different questions: the MoU says _we intend_, the agreement says _who owes what to whom, and what happens when it breaks_. There is a precise moment when you must stop using the first and start using the second, and it has nothing to do with how the meetings feel.

| Crossover trigger                              | Why the MoU stops being enough                                     |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Money moves in either direction                | An intent document cannot govern a payment, a refund or a set-off  |
| Personal data crosses the boundary             | §7.3 — the DPDP Act requires a contract, not an intent             |
| Either side builds something                   | Someone has to own the IP before it exists, not after              |
| A customer is exposed to the partner           | Your dealer now has two vendors and one contract; the gap is yours |
| Anyone makes a public claim                    | Logo, press line, "in partnership with" — all need written limits  |
| Uptime, response times or volumes are promised | An SLA that lives in an MoU is a promise with no remedy            |

Everything above the line is a pilot scope email and, at most, a mutual NDA. Everything below it is [[C26-partnership-evaluation-and-mou-checklist|C26]] and §7.

## 7. The paperwork

You will be shown documents by people who have lawyers, and you will be asked to sign them in rooms where saying "I need a week" feels like weakness. It is not. **"Our counsel reviews everything; you'll have it back inside a week" is a complete sentence and no competent counterparty has ever thought less of a founder for saying it.**

### 7.1 The five documents, and what each actually does

| Document                        | What it really is                                                                                       | Binding?                                              | Right moment                                                                   | VSYST default                                                                                     |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **NDA** (mutual)                | A promise not to leak, with a term and a definition of what counts                                      | Yes — usually the only binding thing early            | Before _you_ see their data, roadmap or dealer list — not before your own demo | **Mutual, 2–3 years, narrow definition, no non-solicit of your team, no exclusivity smuggled in** |
| **LOI** (letter of intent)      | A one-side letter saying "we intend to buy / to pilot", used internally by a champion                   | Normally non-binding except confidentiality and costs | When their process needs paper to move a step                                  | Accept if offered; never ask for one                                                              |
| **MoU**                         | A mutual statement of intent (§6.3)                                                                     | Same                                                  | Only when a mid-level champion needs it upward                                 | Non-binding except confidentiality; non-exclusive; expiry date                                    |
| **MSA + order form / SOW**      | The actual contract: scope, price, term, liability, IP, data, termination                               | **Yes, entirely**                                     | The moment money, data, build or a customer is involved (§6.6)                 | One MSA on VSYST paper; commercials in a short order form so the MSA never reopens                |
| **DPA / data-processing terms** | The annexure that says who is the Data Fiduciary, what may be processed, for what purpose, for how long | Yes                                                   | Whenever personal data crosses the boundary                                    | Annexure to the MSA, never a separate negotiation                                                 |

The document nobody lists and everybody should use most: **the one-page pilot scope email**, sent by you, confirmed by them in a reply. It is free, it takes twenty minutes, it is admissible, and at VSYST's stage it settles nine disputes out of ten before they exist.

### 7.2 Data-processing terms under the DPDP Act 2023

This is the clause set a technical CEO must read personally, because it is the one where a signature quietly changes what the product is allowed to be.

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 requires that a Data Fiduciary may involve a Data Processor **"only under a valid contract"** — there is no informal route and no implied one ([DPDP Act 2023 §8(2), MeitY](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2024/06/2bf1f0e9f04e6fb4f8fef35e82c42aa5.pdf); [§8 text](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/186118625/)). The operational rules arrived with the **Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025**, notified in November 2025 with a staggered commencement — the Data Protection Board provisions first and the substantive fiduciary obligations phased in over the following eighteen months ([S.S. Rana & Co — MeitY notifies final DPDP Rules 2025](https://ssrana.in/articles/meity-notifies-final-digital-personal-data-protection-rules-2025/)). **VERIFY LIVE** the exact commencement dates and the current text of the Rules with your CS or counsel before relying on any date in a contract; this schedule is the single most likely thing in this lesson to have moved.

Two structural facts drive every partner conversation:

1. **Indian law does not split liability the way the GDPR does.** The Act loads the duties — notice, consent, security safeguards, breach reporting, erasure, grievance redressal — onto the **Data Fiduciary**. A processing contract disciplines your processor; it does not move your dealer's exposure off your dealer. So the honest sales line is not "we're compliant" but "here is how our contract keeps you compliant".
2. **Who is the Fiduciary in DZZLO is a real question, not a rhetorical one.** For the dealer's B2B customer records, the dealer decides purpose and means, so the dealer looks like the Fiduciary and VSYST like its Processor. For the dealer's own account, VSYST looks like the Fiduciary. **VERIFY LIVE** this characterisation with counsel and write the answer into the MSA — a partner's draft will otherwise write it for you, in their favour.

Six clauses to insist on in any partner paper that touches tenant data:

| Clause                    | The line to hold                                                                                      |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Purpose limitation**    | Named purposes only; anything else needs fresh written instruction                                    |
| **No onward transfer**    | No sub-processor without prior written notice and the same terms flowing down                         |
| **Security and locality** | Stated controls; India hosting; no copies outside the agreed systems                                  |
| **Breach notification**   | Upward to you on a **stated clock in hours**, with enough detail to make your own report              |
| **Deletion and return**   | On termination, a dated certificate of deletion — not "we'll take care of it"                         |
| **Audit**                 | A right to ask, answered in writing; do not accept an unlimited right _into_ your systems in exchange |

Read this section alongside §3.7 (the OMC will ask for the dealers' data) and [[certification-and-standards-roadmap|the Certification and Standards Roadmap]], which owns the ISO 27001 and CERT-In sequencing that makes these promises credible rather than aspirational.

### 7.3 What a founder may sign alone, and what needs a board resolution

VSYST has three founder-directors and no delegation instrument, which means the true current answer to "may I sign this?" is _unclear_ — and unclear is the worst of the three possible answers, because it produces both over-caution and accidental over-reach. Fix it once, at the next board meeting, with a **standing authorisation resolution**: a minuted delegation naming who may sign what, up to what value, with everything above the line reserved. That is a [[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties|lesson 09]] artefact, drafted with [[C17-board-pack-agenda-and-minutes|C17]] and recorded in [[C03-decision-rights-matrix|C03]].

| Instrument                                                                             | Who may sign at VSYST                                                                                                                                                                                               | Why                                                                                   |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Mutual NDA, pilot scope email, LOI acknowledgement                                     | **CEO alone**, under the standing resolution                                                                                                                                                                        | Low value, reversible, time-critical                                                  |
| Non-binding MoU with an expiry                                                         | **CEO alone**, circulated to co-directors the same day                                                                                                                                                              | Non-binding, but reputationally board business                                        |
| Customer/partner MSA within the standing value limit                                   | **CEO alone**, logged                                                                                                                                                                                               | Ordinary course                                                                       |
| Anything with **exclusivity, revenue share, or a multi-year term**                     | **Board resolution**                                                                                                                                                                                                | Restricts the company's future — the definition of a one-way door                     |
| **Borrowing, investing company funds, issuing securities, giving guarantees**          | **Board resolution at a meeting** — these are s.179(3) powers, exercisable only by resolution ([Companies Act 2013 s.179](https://ca2013.com/179-powers-of-board/))                                                 | Statutory, not preference. Includes the bank guarantee a PSU tender will ask for (§8) |
| Any contract with a **related party** — a director's other business, a relative's firm | **Board resolution**, with the interested director not participating, and the register maintained ([Companies Act 2013 s.188](https://ibclaw.in/section-188-of-the-companies-act-2013-related-party-transactions/)) | Family-founded company; this row will come up                                         |
| **IP assignment or exclusive licence of DZZLO**                                        | **Board resolution**, and read §7.4 first                                                                                                                                                                           | It is the company                                                                     |

One doctrine that surprises founders: internal limits protect the company **internally**. A third party dealing in good faith is generally entitled to assume the company's internal procedures were followed — the indoor-management rule — so a signature given beyond an internal cap can still bind VSYST while leaving the signing director answerable to the board. The limit is a discipline, not a shield. Treat it that way.

Stamp duty and execution are the boring half: stamp duty on agreements is **state-specific** and e-stamping is the normal route in Chhattisgarh, and electronic execution is valid for most commercial contracts but not for every instrument. **VERIFY LIVE** the applicable stamp rate and whether the specific document may be e-signed with your CS _before_ signature, not after — an unstamped agreement is a problem you only discover when you need to enforce it.

### 7.4 The never-sign list

Print this. It is short on purpose.

| Never sign                             | Why it is fatal here, specifically                                                                                                                                                                                    | What you sign instead                                                                                                                    |
| -------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Unlimited liability**                | VSYST's entire equity is smaller than one mid-size claim. An uncapped clause converts a software bug into the end of the company, and the Pvt Ltd's limited liability does not help — the company is the one liable   | **Cap at fees paid in the preceding 12 months.** If the counterparty insists on carve-outs, cap the carve-outs too, at a stated multiple |
| **Uncapped indemnity**                 | An indemnity is a promise to _bear another's loss_ under the Contract Act ([s.124](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/1810320/)) — uncapped, it is a blank cheque signed by a three-person company                          | Narrow indemnity for IP infringement and wilful breach only, capped, with the right to control the defence                               |
| **Perpetual or broad exclusivity**     | It sells your future for a present that has not been paid for; and a restraint drawn too wide can be void in India while still having cost you the years ([Contract Act s.27](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/1431516/)) | Narrow field, one district, twelve months, auto-lapsing unless a stated revenue number is met                                            |
| **IP assignment of the core product**  | You would be handing over the only asset the company has, in exchange for a pilot                                                                                                                                     | A limited, revocable, non-exclusive licence to _use_ — never to own, modify or sublicense                                                |
| **A founder's personal guarantee**     | It reverses the one thing incorporation bought you and puts the family home behind a partner's tender                                                                                                                 | The company's covenant, and a smaller deal                                                                                               |
| **Unrestricted access to tenant data** | §3.7. One dealer finds out and the district knows by Friday                                                                                                                                                           | Consented, logged, per-tenant switches; aggregate-only by default                                                                        |
| **MFN pricing across all customers**   | You cannot price a market you have not sold yet                                                                                                                                                                       | Nothing. There is no version of this worth having at VSYST's stage                                                                       |
| **Auto-renewal with no exit**          | Turns a mistake into a subscription                                                                                                                                                                                   | Twelve months, terminable for convenience on notice                                                                                      |

The general rule underneath all eight rows: **at VSYST's size, no partnership is worth a clause that can end the company.** A partner who cannot accept a liability cap proportionate to what they are paying you is not offering a partnership; they are offering to transfer their risk onto a smaller balance sheet. The polite refusal — "we can't carry a cap larger than the company" — has never lost a deal that was real.

### 7.5 The three sentences that end a clause negotiation

You will not out-lawyer a PSU's contracts cell or a payment company's legal team, and you should stop trying. What a very small company has instead is the ability to be **plainly, unembarrassedly honest about its size**, which large counterparties find far easier to work with than a three-person company pretending to be an enterprise vendor. Three sentences do most of the work.

1. **"We can't carry a cap larger than the company."** Use it on liability, indemnity and penalty clauses. It is a fact, not a position, and the person across the table has heard it before from every small vendor they have ever onboarded. It usually produces a counter-offer rather than a walk-out — and when it produces a walk-out, the deal would have killed you anyway.
2. **"Our counsel reviews everything; you'll have it back inside a week."** Use it whenever a document appears in a meeting. Nobody has ever lost a real deal to a one-week review, and the founders who sign in the room are the ones who later discover an auto-renewal, an exclusivity or an IP clause they never read.
3. **"Let's start with the smallest version of this that is real."** Use it when the paperwork is growing faster than the commitment. It converts an MSA negotiation back into a pilot scope email, which is where a pre-revenue company should be living anyway (§3.6, §6.2).

The tell that you are in a bad negotiation is not a hard clause. It is **asymmetry of effort**: you are producing documents, they are producing requests. Count the last sixty days. If the count is 5–0 in documents and 0–5 in commitments, the clause you are arguing about is not the problem — §10 is.

## 8. Government and quasi-government paths

Every Indian founder eventually hears the same sentence at a family dinner: _"Government mein ek order mil gaya toh life set hai."_ It is not wrong. It is just incomplete in a way that has killed a great many small companies, because it describes the revenue and omits the working capital, the calendar and the sunk senior hours.

This section exists so that the CEO can hold both halves at once: government is a genuine market with real money in it, **and** it is the single most reliable way for a bootstrapped company to run out of cash while winning.

### 8.1 Four different things wearing one word

"Government" is four unrelated counterparties with four different economics. Confusing them is the first mistake.

| Path                                                                 | What it actually is                                                                                                                 | Who the buyer is                                                       | What it demands                                                                                                 | Fit at VSYST today                                                                                            |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **GeM — Government e-Marketplace**                                   | A transactional marketplace; buyers place orders against catalogue listings, bids and reverse auctions ([GeM](https://gem.gov.in/)) | A specific officer with a budget head in a specific department or PSU  | Registration, a category that fits, a listed offering, and — crucially — a buyer who already wants it           | **Register, don't chase.** Free, a weekend's work, and it makes you _findable_. It does not make you _bought_ |
| **PSU procurement** (IOCL, BPCL, HPCL, transport corporations)       | Vendor registration, EOIs and tenders, evaluated at the PSU's sole discretion (§3.1, §3.2)                                          | Materials / Contracts, on the say-so of a user department              | Category match, turnover and past-performance thresholds, EMD and performance bank guarantee, security posture  | **Not yet.** Blocked by the balance sheet and the security paperwork, not by anyone's opinion of DZZLO (§3.2) |
| **State departments** (Transport, Food & Civil Supplies, Industries) | Departmental procurement, sometimes via GeM, sometimes via the state e-procurement portal, sometimes via a scheme                   | A director or commissioner, moved by a scheme or a minister's priority | A relationship inside the department, patience across financial years, and often a local implementation partner | **No.** No fit that VSYST's product serves today; revisit only if a named officer asks                        |
| **Government as an API supplier** (DigiLocker, NIC, UIDAI, GSTN)     | Not a customer at all — a **rail you consume**, mostly at ₹0 in government fees                                                     | Nobody. You are the applicant                                          | Paperwork, audits, a DSC, static IPs, patience in weeks not years                                               | **Yes — this is the good one** (§8.6)                                                                         |

Read the last row against the other three. **The most valuable government relationship available to VSYST this year is one in which the government is the supplier and VSYST is the applicant, not one in which the government is the buyer.** That inversion is the single most useful reframe in this section, and it is worth saying out loud at a board meeting, because "we are talking to the government" sounds identical in both cases and means opposite things for cash.

### 8.2 GeM, honestly

GeM is genuinely easier than classical tendering, and the start-up provisions are real. DPIIT-recognised start-ups can list through the **Startup Runway**, which exists precisely so that a never-seen-before product can reach government buyers without the usual past-performance wall, and which routinely carries exemptions from **prior turnover, prior experience and EMD** ([GeM — Startup Runway](https://gem.gov.in/Startup_Runway); [Startup India — Public Procurement](https://www.startupindia.gov.in/content/sih/en/public_procurement.html); [Invest India — Easing public procurement for start-ups](https://www.investindia.gov.in/team-india-blogs/easing-public-procurement-startups)). Underneath it sits the **Public Procurement Policy for Micro and Small Enterprises Order, 2012**, which sets a minimum **25% annual procurement target from MSEs** for central ministries, departments and PSUs, with sub-targets of 4% for SC/ST-owned and 3% for women-owned MSEs ([Ministry of MSME — Public Procurement Policy](https://msme.gov.in/public-procrument-policy); [DC-MSME — Policy FAQs, 2022](https://www.dcmsme.gov.in/FAQs-PPP_25032022.pdf)). **VERIFY LIVE** the current exemption terms, the recognition validity window and the Udyam/DPIIT eligibility conditions with your CS before you rely on any of it — these are amended often, and the Startup Runway exemption in particular is time-bound to the recognition, not to the listing.

Now the part the promotional material omits.

| What GeM fixes                                                                    | What GeM does not fix                                                                                                                                               |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Discoverability — a buyer searching a category can find you                       | **Demand.** No listing has ever generated a government buyer who did not already have the problem, the budget head and the intent                                   |
| Entry barriers — turnover and experience walls fall away for recognised start-ups | **Category fit.** A multi-tenant OMS for fuel dealers maps awkwardly onto GeM's service categories; getting the category right is a real, unglamorous piece of work |
| Contracting friction — standard terms, standard order flow                        | **Who your customer is.** DZZLO's paying tenant is a _private dealer_, not a government department. GeM does not sell to your ICP                                   |
| Payment discipline — GeM's process is materially faster than manual bill-passing  | The **float** between delivery and credit, which a company with no working capital feels regardless of how disciplined the process is                               |

The honest verdict, stated as an instruction: **spend one weekend registering on GeM if DPIIT recognition is in hand, list one offering, then stop.** It is a cheap, permanent option — a listing does not decay and costs nothing to hold — and it converts "we should look at government" from a recurring agenda item into a done row. What it must never become is a project. A GeM listing that produces monthly discussion and no order is §5.2 wearing a government uniform.

### 8.3 State transport and fuel departments

The temptation here is specific and worth naming because it looks so plausible: VSYST's product tracks fuel movement, deliveries and vehicles, so surely the state Transport Department, the Food & Civil Supplies Department (which administers PDS kerosene), or a state transport corporation with its own fleet and internal pumps would want it.

They might. But work through what such a sale actually is, because it is not the business VSYST is in:

| Reality                                                                        | Consequence for VSYST                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| The buyer is a department, not a dealer                                        | Different product, different pricing, different security requirements, different support expectation — a **second company**, in the sense of [[06-strategy-ii-moats-positioning-and-the-business-model\|lesson 06]] §9 |
| Purchases follow schemes and financial years                                   | Your sales cycle stops being a function of your effort and becomes a function of their calendar                                                                                                                        |
| Implementation usually needs a local partner already empanelled with the state | You become a subcontractor to a systems integrator — the OEM/white-label row on §4's disqualification list                                                                                                             |
| Requirements are written before you are consulted                              | If a tender's specification was not written with you in the room, you are pricing to lose ([[07-customers-markets-and-founder-led-sales\|lesson 07]] §8)                                                               |
| Payment runs through treasury processes                                        | Months, not weeks — and no MSMED remedy makes a treasury faster in practice                                                                                                                                            |

**The house call: no state-department pursuit at VSYST until there is a named officer who has asked, in writing, for something specific.** Inbound interest from a department is a real event and should be answered within a day. Outbound pursuit of departments, at three people and pre-revenue, is the most expensive form of hope available. Chhattisgarh's own start-up machinery — [Startup Chhattisgarh](https://www.invest.cg.gov.in/startup) under the Department of Commerce & Industries, and the state's start-up policy as summarised on the national portal ([Startup India — Chhattisgarh state policy](https://www.startupindia.gov.in/content/sih/en/state-startup-policies/Chhattisgarh-state-policy.html)) — is a far better use of the same relationship: it offers recognition, incubation and credit support rather than a procurement cycle. **VERIFY LIVE** the current incentive terms and corpus figures with the department before quoting any of them.

### 8.4 The money reality: what a tender actually costs a company with no working capital

This is the table to read before any conversation about a government order, and it is the one nobody at the family dinner has ever seen.

| Cash event                         | When it happens   | What it costs                                                                    | Why it is fatal at VSYST's size                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| ---------------------------------- | ----------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **EMD / bid security**             | At bid submission | Typically a percentage of estimated value, blocked or furnished as an instrument | Cash out before any chance of cash in. Start-up and MSE exemptions can waive it — **VERIFY LIVE** per tender, never assume                                                                                                                                                 |
| **Performance bank guarantee**     | On award          | A percentage of contract value, held for the contract term plus a claim period   | A bank issues a BG against **margin money or collateral**. A pre-revenue company with no fixed deposits gets it against 100% margin — i.e. the cash is simply gone from your account for the contract's life. This is the row that ends most small-vendor tender ambitions |
| **Delivery and deployment**        | Before invoice    | Your engineering, your travel, your support                                      | Paid out of a runway that has no revenue behind it                                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| **Invoice → acceptance → payment** | After delivery    | The float                                                                        | The real number. Budget in **months**, and assume the first invoice of any relationship takes longest because your vendor master, bank details and tax particulars are being created for the first time                                                                    |
| **Retention / warranty holdback**  | After payment     | A slice held back                                                                | Cash you have earned, cannot spend, and must still fund payroll around                                                                                                                                                                                                     |

The legal protections exist and you should know them precisely, because knowing them is what lets you decide _not_ to rely on them. Under the **MSMED Act, 2006**, a buyer must pay a registered micro or small supplier within the agreed period or, absent an agreement, within **15 days — and in no case beyond 45 days** from acceptance, with interest on delay; disputes go to the Micro and Small Enterprise Facilitation Council through the **MSME Samadhaan** portal ([India.gov.in — MSME Samadhaan, Delayed Payment Monitoring System](https://www.india.gov.in/micro-small-and-medium-enterprises-msme-samadhaan-delayed-payment-monitoring-system)). The Finance Act 2023 added real teeth from the buyer's side: **section 43B(h) of the Income-tax Act** disallows the deduction of any amount payable to a micro or small enterprise unless it is actually paid within the MSMED time limit, which turns a slow payment into a tax cost for the buyer ([ClearTax — Section 43B(h)](https://cleartax.in/s/section-43bh-of-income-tax-act); [IndiaFilings — the 45-day rule](https://www.indiafilings.com/learn/section-43bh-new-msme-45-days-payment-rule)). Both protections require the supplier to hold a valid **Udyam registration** ([Udyam Registration portal](https://udyamregistration.gov.in/)) — which VSYST should therefore obtain regardless of whether it ever bids for anything. **VERIFY LIVE** the current thresholds, the 43B(h) applicability boundaries and Udyam eligibility with your CA; these have been amended since introduction and are amended most Budgets.

And then the honest sentence underneath all of it: **a remedy is not a cash flow.** Filing at the Facilitation Council against the very PSU you want a second order from is a decision most small vendors will never take, and everybody on both sides of that table knows it. Price the float as though the remedy does not exist, because operationally it does not.

### 8.5 When it is a business, and when it is a trap

Same opportunity, opposite verdicts, separated by facts you can check before spending anything.

| It is a business when                                                                           | It is a trap when                                                                       |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| A **named officer with a budget head** has described the requirement to you unprompted          | You found the opportunity on a portal and nobody inside knows you                       |
| The specification describes **what you already sell**, not what you would build                 | Winning requires a build, and the build only pays off if you win                        |
| **EMD and PBG are exempt or trivially small**, confirmed in the tender document                 | The BG would lock cash you need for payroll                                             |
| The order is **repeatable** — a first order creates a category and a vendor code you can reuse  | It is a one-off with a bespoke scope and no second order behind it                      |
| You can survive the float **at your worst realistic case**, computed from your own bank balance | You are counting on being paid on time to make the next month work                      |
| Your **existing customers are the same people** the department serves, so the work compounds    | The government product diverges from the dealer product and you are now maintaining two |
| Losing costs you **a week**                                                                     | Losing costs you a quarter, and you will have to lose several times before you win once |

If any left-hand cell is false, the correct posture is not "no" — it is **"registered and reachable, not resourced"**: hold the GeM listing, keep watching supplier notices for twenty minutes a week (§3.2), and put the senior hours into the district.

### 8.6 The government path that is actually open: government as your API supplier

Here is the row that justifies this section's place in a partnerships lesson. VSYST's own research — `docs/tasks/tasks_15_govt_apis` — established that the entire KYC and compliance wishlist can be served **directly by government gateways at ₹0 in government fees**, with no aggregator taking a per-call cut. That is a partnership in every meaningful sense: an application, an approval committee, a contract of adhesion, an audit obligation and an ongoing relationship. It just has none of the tender pathologies, because there is no bid, no EMD, no bank guarantee and no float.

| Rail                                                                                                                     | What it unlocks for DZZLO                                                                                                                                                                              | Government fee                                                                                          | Realistic elapsed time            | The real cost                                                                                                                                                                           |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **DigiLocker Requester**, via the API Setu partners portal ([partners.apisetu.gov.in](https://partners.apisetu.gov.in/)) | Consent-based in-app pulls of **PAN, e-Aadhaar, Driving Licence and Vehicle RC**, signed by the issuing department — one onboarding covers all four                                                    | **₹0**                                                                                                  | ~2–8 weeks, weekly committee      | Official-domain email, organisation DSC, a public website, a nodal officer, **STQC audit of the app and annual CERT-In-empanelled audits**, India-only hosting, quarterly usage reports |
| **UIDAI OVSE registration** ([UIDAI — OVSE](https://uidai.gov.in/en/ovse-registration.html))                             | QR-scan / Aadhaar-app verification with face match — proof-of-presence driver checks at the nozzle                                                                                                     | **₹0** today, fee schedule reserved — **VERIFY LIVE**                                                   | Weeks to ~2 months                | Callback URL, public certificate, app IDs, and the discipline of never storing the Aadhaar number                                                                                       |
| **NIC "registered ERP"** ([NIC e-invoice API sandbox](https://einv-apisandbox.nic.in/))                                  | **E-way bill + e-invoice** APIs for every dealer GSTIN under one credential set, each dealer self-linking in minutes — and, with it, the Get-GSTIN-details call that closes the GSTIN-verification gap | **₹0**                                                                                                  | **3–6 weeks** to production       | A sandbox test campaign (≥50 success and ≥50 failure cases per API), whitelisted static IPs, and the rule that the provider must not store request/response payloads                    |
| **GSTN / GSP** ([GSTN](https://www.gstn.org.in/))                                                                        | GST **return filing** inside the product                                                                                                                                                               | **₹0** application fee, but a **₹50 lakh turnover bar** and an application window that opens in batches | 4–9 months _after_ a window opens | The only genuinely closed door today. Ship guided portal filing with offline JSON instead, and watch for a window                                                                       |

Three CEO-level judgements follow, and none of them is an engineering call:

1. **This is the cheapest strategic moat purchase available to VSYST.** Direct rails mean no per-verification fee forever, which is a permanent unit-economics advantage over any competitor paying ₹1.5–4 per check to an aggregator ([[C12-unit-economics-and-business-model-calculator|C12]]). It also produces a sentence no competitor of VSYST's size can say to an OMC or a bank: _our KYC comes signed by the issuing department, not from a data broker._
2. **The audit obligations are the sequencing problem, and they are shared with everything else.** DigiLocker, the MoRTH NTR route and GSP all require **CERT-In-empanelled** audits, and one engagement can serve several programmes at once. That is the same engagement the OMC security question needs (§3.2) and the same one that sits at the front of [[certification-and-standards-roadmap|the Certification and Standards Roadmap]]. Sequence it once, deliberately, rather than three times reactively.
3. **Do not apply before you are ready to integrate.** The research is explicit that an unverified DigiLocker Requester account is auto-deleted after three months. An application filed to feel productive is worse than no application, because it burns the goodwill of a committee you will need again. **The trigger to apply is a dated engineering slot, not a free afternoon.**

One domain quirk that keeps this honest and belongs in every partner conversation: petrol, HSD and natural gas sit **outside GST**, so there is **no e-way bill and no IRN on fuel movement — ever**. What crosses the e-invoice threshold is the dealer's **lubricant and GST B2B lines**, while fuel turnover still counts toward the aggregate turnover that triggers the mandate. Anyone who promises a fuel dealer "e-invoicing for your sales" has not understood the customer, and saying this correctly in a room is worth more credibility than a deck. **VERIFY LIVE** the current e-invoice turnover thresholds and exemption rules with your CA before quoting them — they move nearly every year.

## 9. Advisors and the informal ecosystem

A Bengaluru founder is handed, free and by accident, a set of things a Raipur founder has to manufacture on purpose. Naming them precisely is the first step, because the usual framing — "we don't have an ecosystem here" — is self-pitying, imprecise and therefore useless.

### 9.1 What proximity actually gives, itemised

| What a Koramangala founder gets for free                                                      | What it is really worth                                                     | How a Raipur founder manufactures it                                                                                                                 |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Ambient pattern-matching** — overhearing what worked and what did not, twenty times a month | Avoided mistakes. The most valuable and least visible item on this list     | Deliberate, scheduled conversations with three or four founders who are 12–24 months ahead. Online is fine; frequency is what matters, not proximity |
| **Warm introductions on tap**                                                                 | A meeting that starts at trust instead of at suspicion                      | A written, maintained list of who knows whom, and the willingness to ask — see §9.5's ask ladder                                                     |
| **Peer benchmarks** — what a salary, a conversion rate, a churn number should be              | Calibration. Without it you cannot tell whether 3% is good or catastrophic  | Two peer founders in Indian B2B SaaS at similar stage who will trade real numbers under a mutual NDA                                                 |
| **Investor visibility before you raise**                                                      | A raise that starts warm ([[08-capital-runway-and-fundraising\|lesson 08]]) | Monthly written updates to a small list, started long before you need money ([[C23-stakeholder-and-investor-update\|C23]])                           |
| **Talent density** — engineers who have seen scale                                            | Hiring speed                                                                | Remote hiring, and the honest acceptance that this is where Raipur costs you most ([[10-building-the-team-hiring-equity-and-firing\|lesson 10]])     |
| **A sense that this is normal**                                                               | Morale, which is a real input at three people                               | Peer founders, again. This is not soft; founder isolation is a documented failure mode                                                               |

And now the column nobody writes down: **what Raipur gives that Koramangala cannot.** Three decades of standing in the fuel trade in one director's name. A district where you can reach any pump in forty minutes. A cost base a funded competitor cannot match. Customers who are physically reachable, which is the precondition for founder-led sales. These are not consolations; they are the actual moat ([[06-strategy-ii-moats-positioning-and-the-business-model|lesson 06]]). The correct posture is to buy the six missing items above as cheaply as possible **without** giving up the four you have — which, concretely, means not moving the company to Bengaluru because a deck would read better.

The rule that governs all six: **choose depth over breadth.** A founder who is genuinely known by fifteen people in Raipur and four peer founders online has a better network than one who has attended forty events. Networking that produces business cards is a hobby; networking that produces a named person who will take your call at 9 pm is infrastructure.

### 9.3 The one relationship worth more than the other five combined

Everything above is general. This is specific, and it is the recommendation this section exists to make.

> **Find one fuel-industry insider, outside the family, who is not a customer, and buy their judgement with attention rather than equity.**

The profile, in order of usefulness:

| Profile                                                | What only they can tell you                                                                                                                                                                                     | Where they are                                                                                      |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **A retired or serving OMC divisional / area officer** | How a proposal actually travels inside an OMC, which desk kills things, what the annual calendar looks like, what a divisional manager is measured on, and which of your three doors (§3.1) is open _this year_ | Retirement is common in the mid-50s; a retired DGM is reachable, has time, and owes nobody anything |
| **A multi-outlet dealer from another state**           | What your product looks like to someone with no loyalty to your family, and what the objection sounds like in a market you have not sold to                                                                     | Association meetings; other dealers' introductions                                                  |
| **A former fleet-card / loyalty programme manager**    | Why XTRAPOWER-class programmes convert the way they do, and where the credit book really sits (§3.3)                                                                                                            | OMC alumni, fintech alumni                                                                          |
| **A petroleum-sector consultant or trade journalist**  | Who is doing what, and the map of everybody already selling software into forecourts (§2.6)                                                                                                                     | Trade publications; industry events                                                                 |

Row one is the one to pursue first, and the reason is arithmetic. VSYST's plan currently allocates senior hours to an OMC relationship on the basis of guesses about how OMCs behave. **One retired divisional officer converts those guesses into facts in a single afternoon**, and facts are what the §3.5 seven-question test needs to produce a real answer instead of a hopeful one. Measured against a quarter of misdirected founder time, that afternoon may be the highest-ROI hour in this entire lesson.

Two warnings, because this relationship has a characteristic failure mode. **Do not hire them as a "consultant to open doors" on a retainer.** Door-opening retainers are the classic way a small company converts cash into meetings, and the incentive is exactly wrong — the retainer is paid whether or not anything happens. And **never let a former officer's involvement look like influence-peddling to the OMC**; in a PSU context that is not merely distasteful, it is disqualifying. What you want is counsel, disclosed and ordinary: a person who tells you how the machine works, not a person who claims to move it.

### 9.4 The CA/CS network, which you are already paying for

Your CA and CS are not just compliance vendors. In a district economy they are, structurally, three things at once:

1. **A judgement source you have already retained.** They see the inside of dozens of local businesses, including businesses that failed. Asking "what do you see going wrong at companies our size?" is free and is almost never asked.
2. **The most credible referral channel to your own ICP.** A CA who does the books for four petrol pumps is closer to your customer than any advertisement will ever be, and their recommendation carries the weight of someone with nothing to sell. §2.7 puts this channel _after_ ten paying dealers — because a referral from a CA whose client then has a bad experience costs you the CA, not just the dealer — but the relationship should be built long before it is used.
3. **The people who make §7 real.** The standing authorisation resolution (§7.3), the stamp-duty and e-execution question (§7.3), the DPDP Fiduciary characterisation (§7.2) and the Udyam registration (§8.4) all resolve in one thirty-minute call with a CS who knows you. Founders postpone that call for months because it feels like admin. It is not admin; it is the difference between signing safely and signing hopefully.

The honest caveat: a small-town CA is usually excellent on statutory compliance and rarely experienced in SaaS contracts, DPDP processing terms or IP clauses. Use them for what they are expert in and retain a **second, specialist counsel by the hour** for the four or five documents in your life that actually matter. Two relationships, different jobs. Trying to make one person do both is how founders end up with an MSA drafted from a template.

### 9.5 The ask ladder — what to ask for, and what to offer

Founders ask badly in two directions at once: too big of strangers, too small of friends.

| Rung | The ask                                                                    | Who you may ask                                      | Why it works                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| ---- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1    | **"Can I ask you one question?"** — and then genuinely one                 | Anyone, including cold                               | Bounded, flattering, answerable on a phone in four minutes                                                                                                                                                                            |
| 2    | **"Thirty minutes on one specific thing"** — named in the request          | Someone one degree away                              | The specificity is what gets the yes; "pick your brain" gets ignored                                                                                                                                                                  |
| 3    | **"Would you look at this and tell me what's wrong with it?"**             | Someone who has already given you rung 2             | People enjoy critiquing far more than advising. This is the highest-yield ask on the ladder                                                                                                                                           |
| 4    | **"Would you introduce me to X?"** — with a forwardable paragraph attached | Someone who has met you twice and knows what you do  | The forwardable paragraph is the whole trick: you are asking for a _forward_, not for effort                                                                                                                                          |
| 5    | **"Would you be an advisor?"**                                             | Someone who has said yes to rungs 2–4 more than once | Only after the relationship has already been doing the work informally. The instrument, the equity question and the conflict check belong to [[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties\|lesson 09]] §7 and are not repeated here |

**Never skip to rung 5.** An advisory agreement signed with someone who has never actually helped you is a piece of paper that buys nothing and costs equity.

Now the half founders forget. A pre-revenue three-person company has less to trade than it thinks in money and **more than it thinks in everything else**:

| What VSYST can genuinely offer                                                                                                                                   | To whom                                                                                                                                                                             |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Ground truth about a market nobody else can see** — what fuel dealers actually do at 10 pm, how credit really works on a forecourt, what the ledger looks like | Investors, journalists, fintech operators, other founders. This is rare and valuable, and giving it away generously is how a small company becomes known                            |
| **Careful, unpaid technical help** — a script, a review, an honest read of an architecture                                                                       | Peer founders. It is the currency technical founders actually trade in                                                                                                              |
| **Introductions inside the trade**                                                                                                                               | The domain-expert director's network is a real asset to others too; spending it deliberately for someone else is how reciprocity starts                                             |
| **Being an easy, prompt, honest counterparty**                                                                                                                   | Everyone. In a district economy this reputation compounds faster than anything else in this lesson                                                                                  |
| **Attention and follow-through**                                                                                                                                 | Advisors. The thing that makes people keep helping you is evidence that the last advice was acted on. Report back — it is the cheapest and most neglected act in founder networking |

### 9.6 Making it survive contact with a busy quarter

Networks decay silently. Two mechanisms, both cheap, both belonging to the CEO personally rather than to any tracker:

- **The relationship ledger.** One vault page: every named person outside the company who matters, their role, where they sit, how you met, the last conversation and its date, and what you owe them. This is the same fix §1.2 prescribed for the domain-expert director's trade relationships, extended to the CEO's own. It takes an hour to start and ten minutes a month to maintain, and it is the difference between a network and a memory.
- **Two calls a month, blocked.** Not "when there is time" — a recurring slot in the CEO's calendar, one call with a peer founder and one with an advisor or insider, in the same week each month ([[C27-ceo-weekly-template-and-calendar-audit|C27]], and the cadence in [[15-the-ceo-operating-cadence-and-calendar|lesson 15]]). Twenty-four conversations a year, kept for two years, is a network. There is no faster version of this and pretending otherwise is how founders end up making the biggest call of their company's life without one person to check it against.

## 10. When to walk away

Almost nothing in a founder's education prepares them for this section. The literature, the mentors and the culture all reward persistence, and persistence is genuinely a founder's most useful trait — right up to the point where it becomes the mechanism by which the company dies. Annie Duke's argument is the correct corrective and it is worth taking literally: quitting is a skill, it is the twin of grit rather than its opposite, and the way to make it usable is to set **kill criteria — a state and a date — before you start**, then hold a review whose only job is to check them ([Annie Duke, _Quit: The Power of Knowing When to Walk Away_, 2022](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/60097435-quit); [Next Big Idea Club — Quit](https://nextbigideaclub.com/magazine/quit-power-knowing-walk-away-bookbite/36452/)). §5.3 already made you write one. This section is about actually using it.

### 10.1 The signals a partnership is dead

Dead partnerships almost never announce themselves. They keep producing warmth, which is precisely what makes them dangerous — a hostile counterparty is easy to leave. Eight signals, in rough order of how early they appear:

| #   | Signal                                                              | What it looks like from inside                                        | The honest read                                                                                                                                           |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | **The champion changed, or went quiet**                             | New person, "let me get up to speed", a slower reply cadence          | In a large organisation a transfer is a reset, not a delay. Restart the §3.5 test from question 1                                                         |
| 2   | **The asks grow and the commitments do not**                        | Another deck, another note, another comparison sheet                  | §3.4's document treadmill. Count documents produced against commitments received over sixty days                                                          |
| 3   | **Every next step is yours**                                        | You are always the one sending, chasing, proposing                    | Criterion 4 of the scored screen (§5.1). The side doing the work is the side that cares — and it is not them                                              |
| 4   | **Nothing exists with their name on it**                            | You have detailed notes; they have produced nothing                   | You have a correspondence, not a partnership (§5.2)                                                                                                       |
| 5   | **The scope keeps moving toward a build**                           | "If you could also do X…"                                             | The roadmap hostage. It is now a project for a customer who has signed nothing                                                                            |
| 6   | **Your own kill date passed and you renegotiated it with yourself** | "It's moving, just slowly"                                            | **The single most diagnostic item on this list.** A kill date you silently extended is the partnership telling you the answer while you refuse to hear it |
| 7   | **You have begun explaining it defensively**                        | To the other directors, in a board meeting, to yourself in the shower | Defensive explanation is the sound of sunk cost ([Escalation of commitment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escalation_of_commitment))                      |
| 8   | **The strategy no longer needs it**                                 | The reason you started is no longer the plan                          | The most legitimate death of all, and the easiest to miss because nothing went wrong                                                                      |

Signal 6 deserves its own rule, because it is the one that costs quarters:

> **Extending a kill date is a decision. It gets written down, with a reason, in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]], and it may be done once.** A second extension is not an extension; it is a decision to keep the partnership indefinitely, and it should be made explicitly with that language, or not at all.

### 10.2 Why founders do not kill, and what each excuse actually is

| The thought                                | What it really is                                                                                                      | The counter                                                                                                            |
| ------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "We've put nine months into this"          | Textbook [sunk cost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunk_cost). The nine months are gone in every future you can choose | Ask only: _starting from today, with what I now know, would I begin this?_                                             |
| "It's about to break open"                 | The stopped clock (§3.4). Imminence has been six weeks away for two quarters                                           | Point at the artefact. If there is none, imminence is a feeling                                                        |
| "If we stop, we look unserious"            | Identity, not strategy. You are protecting a self-image                                                                | Large organisations park initiatives constantly and think nothing of it. Nobody is watching you as closely as you fear |
| "The relationship is too valuable to lose" | A confusion between the _partnership_ and the _relationship_ — which is exactly the confusion §10.4 dissolves          | You are ending a workstream, not a friendship. Say so, and it is true                                                  |
| "The other directors expect it"            | An audience problem, usually invented                                                                                  | Put it on the board agenda as a decision item and find out. Ninety per cent of the time the relief is mutual           |
| "One more meeting will tell us"            | The cheapest possible deferral, and the most repeatable                                                                | You have had that meeting. Six times                                                                                   |

### 10.3 The cost of not killing it, priced

Founders resist killing because the cost of continuing is invisible. Make it visible and the decision usually makes itself. Do this arithmetic in writing, with your own numbers:

| Cost                     | How to price it                                                                                                                                                         | Why it is worse than it looks                                                                                           |
| ------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Senior hours**         | Hours per month × months remaining, at the founder-day rate you already computed for CAC ([[06-strategy-ii-moats-positioning-and-the-business-model\|lesson 06]] §11.4) | These are the _only_ hours that can sell, ship or decide. They are not fungible with anyone else's                      |
| **The counterfactual**   | Name the specific thing not done: districts not visited, the tenant-isolation fix not shipped, the ten dealers not signed                                               | This is the real cost and it never appears on any tracker, so it must be written down explicitly or it will not be felt |
| **Roadmap distortion**   | The features built or deferred because of this partner                                                                                                                  | Compounding. A distorted roadmap costs you after the partnership is dead                                                |
| **Attention and morale** | The recurring agenda item, the founder's mood, the meeting nobody wants to have                                                                                         | A stalled partnership occupying a monthly slot is a slow tax on the whole company's sense that things move              |
| **Optionality lost**     | Any exclusivity, informal or written, and any competitor you did not talk to because of it                                                                              | The one that shows up as a surprise                                                                                     |

State the total in one sentence at the board meeting — _"this has cost roughly N senior hours a month, and the thing that did not happen instead is X"_ — and stop arguing about whether the partner is nice.

### 10.4 Three exits, not one

The reason founders cannot kill partnerships is that they think the only options are _continue_ and _destroy_. There are three, and choosing precisely is what makes ending one survivable in a market where you will meet these people again — at an association meeting, at a wedding, at a divisional office, next year.

| Exit          | What it means                                                                                                                     | When to use it                                                                                       | What it costs                                                                |
| ------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Downgrade** | Move from build state to warm state (§4.2): a quarterly message, a tracker row, zero engineering, zero travel, zero documents     | The partnership is real but early, or the timing is wrong and you can name what would change it      | ~20 minutes a month. This is the correct exit for **most** dead partnerships |
| **Park**      | Say explicitly, in writing, that you are stopping work and name a revisit date                                                    | Your kill criterion fired and the counterparty is a person you respect and expect to deal with again | One awkward message, and it buys back the whole cost column of §10.3         |
| **End**       | Terminate formally: notice under the agreement, data returned or deleted with a certificate, access revoked, announcements agreed | There is a contract, money, data or a customer exposed to the partner (§6.6)                         | Real work, and it must be done properly — see §7.2's deletion clause         |

**The default is downgrade, not end.** A founder who says "we killed it" when they mean "we stopped working on it" invents drama and burns a relationship for no gain. Reserve _end_ for things with paper.

### 10.5 The conversation, and the message that follows it

Do it by phone or in person, then confirm in writing the same day. Never only in writing, and never through the runner — this is one of the four things only a holder can do (§1.3).

Four rules for the conversation:

1. **Own the reason on your side of the table.** "We're a three-person company and we've taken on more than we can carry" is true, non-accusatory, and unarguable. "You never gave us a next step" may also be true and will cost you the relationship for nothing.
2. **Be specific about what stops.** Vagueness leaves the other side expecting things, which is how a parked partnership becomes an awkward encounter eight months later.
3. **Name what would restart it, and mean it.** "If a divisional pilot opens up, you're the first call I make." This is the sentence that converts an ending into a pause.
4. **Thank them concretely.** One specific thing they taught you or did for you. Generic gratitude reads as a formality; a specific line is remembered.

The written note, in full — it should be this short:

> _"Ravi ji, thank you for the time over these months. We're a three-person company and I've had to cut what we're carrying down to two things, so we're stopping active work on this for now — no meetings, no documents from our side. What we learned from you about how the divisional calendar works has already changed our plan, and I'm grateful for it. If a pilot opportunity opens at your end, or if our position changes, you'll be the first person I call. I'll check in around [date] either way."_

That message costs nothing, ends the ambiguity, and leaves the door open — which matters far more in a district market than in an anonymous one.

### 10.6 The same week, or it did not happen

Killing a partnership creates a vacuum, and vacuums refill with whatever is nearest — usually another partnership. Three things happen in the same week, or the decision will quietly reverse:

- **Reallocate the hours explicitly.** Write down where the recovered senior hours go — a district week, the tenant-isolation fix, ten dealer calls — and put them in the calendar before the week ends ([[19-planning-okrs-and-capital-allocation|lesson 19]] on reallocation as the actual act of strategy).
- **Log it in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]]**: what you believed at the start, what actually happened, the criterion that fired, and — the field founders skip — **what you would look for earlier next time.** A killed partnership that produces a better screening question has paid for part of itself.
- **Tell the other directors, in the words you used to the counterparty.** In a family-founded company, an ending that the CEO explains one way internally and another way externally becomes a grievance later ([[11-culture-and-values-as-a-ceo-instrument|lesson 11]]).

## 11. At VSYST — applying this now

### 11.2 Easebuzz / UBI — what "good" looks like by end-November 2026

**What the CEO should stop doing on Easebuzz.** Stop producing documents. The document phase is over and the temptation now is to polish — another read-through, another export, a better .docx. That is §5.2's avoidance in its purest form, because polishing is pleasant and uploading five HTML files to a domain is not. Second, stop treating the privacy policy as a legal errand to be delegated later; it is a CEO signature item under §7.3 and it gates the whole application. Third, stop letting this thread be run by whoever is free — the map (§1.2) assigns it CEO-held, domain-expert-director-run, and it needs the runner's tracker row and a weekly next step with a date, not the CEO's inbox.

### 11.3 IOCL — what "good" looks like by end-November 2026

There is a genuinely strong story here and it deserves to be said plainly: **the credit-book insight is the most interesting sentence VSYST has ever written to an OMC** (§3.3). Roughly 60% of forecourt sale on credit while only about 20% flows through the loyalty rails means the majority of a dealer's commercial reality is invisible to the corporation that governs him under a penal rulebook (§2.2). Nobody with a fleet card can see that. VSYST can, because the dealers put it there themselves.

That insight is worth an enormous amount **and it is not worth a quarter of the CEO's calendar in its current state**, because there is no named officer who can authorise anything and no artefact with IOCL's name on it. Both of those are §3.5 failures, and six out of seven is a fail.

So: warm state, with three exceptions that are cheap and high-yield.

| 1   | **The data position is written down and said out loud** — position one from §3.7, dealer-owned and dealer-consented, in one paragraph, agreed by all three directors, logged in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log\|C29]], and stated unprompted in the next IOCL conversation whenever it happens | One hour. That is roughly **four senior hours a month**, which is exactly what §3.5's question 5 prescribes for a relationship that is optionality rather than dependency. And the test that keeps it honest is the one from that question: _if IOCL said a clean no tomorrow, what in the district plan changes?_ If the answer is "nothing", four hours a month is correct and generous. If the answer is "everything", the plan is broken and that — not the partnership — is the thing to fix this quarter.

Alongside it, two cheap standing options: **twenty minutes a week watching supplier notices** (§3.2), and **the GeM registration weekend** (§8.2) if DPIIT recognition is in hand. Both are options you hold, not projects you run.

**What the CEO should stop doing on IOCL.** Stop producing analysis of IOCL's problems — the discussion document and the design-philosophy note are already better than what most vendors ever produce, and the next one is free consulting (§3.4). Stop pursuing altitude; a warmer meeting with someone more senior and no budget line is not progress. Stop building anything against an IOCL hypothetical: no module, no report, no data feed, until a signed pilot scope exists. And stop letting the corporate and start-up-fund route occupy any recurring thought at all — score 35, decline in writing to yourself, and set the trigger: _revisit only when a relevant open-innovation challenge is published, or when a divisional pilot has produced a written read-out._

### 11.4 The consolidated stop list

Nine things to stop, this month, stated as instructions because that is what makes them actionable:

1. **Stop running two partnerships in build state.** Easebuzz is the one. IOCL is warm at four hours a month.
2. **Stop producing documents for counterparties who have produced none.** Count the last sixty days both ways before drafting the next one.
3. **Stop having partner meetings without a written next step in their words.** Twenty minutes at the end of a meeting is worth more than the meeting.
4. **Stop treating "policy pages generated" as "policies published".** Live URL or it does not exist.
5. **Stop postponing the CS call.** One thirty-minute conversation settles the standing authorisation resolution (§7.3), the DPDP Fiduciary characterisation (§7.2), the stamp-duty question and the Udyam registration (§8.4).
6. **Stop signing anything with an uncapped anything.** §7.4 is one page. Print it and keep it where documents get signed.
7. **Stop pursuing government as a buyer.** Register on GeM, watch notices for twenty minutes a week, and put the senior hours into the district — while starting the rails in §8.6, which is the government relationship that is actually open.
8. **Stop extending kill dates silently.** Once, in writing, with a reason. Never twice.
9. **Stop confusing the relationship with the workstream.** Downgrading a partnership does not end a relationship, and saying so plainly is what lets you actually do it.

And one thing to start, because a stop list with no start list is just guilt: **find the fuel-industry insider.** Everything else in this section is bookkeeping. That one relationship changes what VSYST knows.

## 12. Exercises

**12.1 — Draw the relationship map, with the backup column filled honestly (40 min, alone, then 20 min with the other two directors).** Reproduce §1.2's table for VSYST as it actually is today, not as it should be: every counterparty that has taken more than two hours of anyone's time this quarter, plus the ones that should have. For each row fill **what we want / what they want / holder / runner / backup**, and score it against §1.1's four questions. Then do the two things that make it an artefact rather than a diagram. First, **circle every row scoring under 2/4 that has the CEO as holder** — that is your calendar leak, and it should move to the COO's vendor register ([[14-vendors-procurement-and-cost-control|COO 14]]) or to nobody. Second, **fill the backup column for the fuel-domain rows**, which will mean starting the written list §1.2 prescribes: every named trade relationship, the person's role, where they sit, the history, the last conversation, kept where two directors can read it. **Artefact:** a filled relationship map in the vault, plus the first ten rows of the trade-relationship list. **Success test:** at least one row moves off the CEO's name during the 20-minute review, and at least one row that nobody had written down turns out to be held by exactly one person.

**12.2 — Score the OMC path in C26, and run the seven-question test for real (45 min, alone, with the correspondence open).** Open [[C26-partnership-evaluation-and-mou-checklist|C26]] and score **all three IOCL paths separately** — field door, corporate/start-up route, procurement door — using §5.1's ten criteria. Do not average them into one "IOCL" row; the whole point of §3.1 is that they are three different partnerships with three different timelines. Compare your scores against §11.1's and **write a sentence wherever you disagree with this lesson**, because that sentence is your actual judgement and it is worth more than the table. Then run §3.5's seven-question test on the highest-scoring path, in full, in writing, dated, into [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] — including question 5 (_if they said no tomorrow, what changes?_) and question 6 (_what did not happen because of last quarter's hours?_). **Artefact:** a scored C26 with three columns and a band verdict for each, plus a dated seven-question test in C29 with a senior-hours cap written for next quarter. **Success test:** question 6's second blank — what did not happen — names something specific and uncomfortable. If it is blank or vague, the test has not been run honestly.

**12.3 — Build the first-meeting prep pack, once, so it is never built again (45 min).** Assemble one folder in the vault that any partner meeting can be run from cold. Five items, all of which already exist in fragments in this lesson: (a) the **run-of-show** from §6.1, edited into your own words and timed for 20 minutes; (b) the **one problem, in their vocabulary** — written out in the counterparty's language, not yours, and no more than three sentences; (c) the **three-minute live demo path** on a phone, written as an actual click sequence on a real consented dealer's screen, so it never has to be improvised; (d) the **data sentence** from §3.7 — the dealer-owned, dealer-consented paragraph, ready to be said unprompted; (e) the **one-page pilot scope** template from §3.6, with N, the dates, the named officer and the review date left blank. Print §7.4's never-sign list and put it in the same folder. **Artefact:** a `partner-prep/` folder in the vault with five reusable documents and one printed page. **Success test:** hand the folder to the domain-expert director and ask him to run the first six minutes from it. If he cannot, item (b) is written in your vocabulary, not the customer's — which is the most common defect in this pack and the one worth fixing before any meeting.

**12.4 — Write the kill criterion for the largest open partnership, and put the date in the calendar (20 min, tonight).** Take whichever partnership currently consumes the most senior hours. Write §5.3's sentence — _"If by [date] we do not have [specific artefact], we park this, tell them so in writing, and revisit on [date]"_ — with a real artefact and two real dates, into [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]]. Then do the two steps founders skip. **Create a calendar event on the kill date itself**, titled with the criterion, so that it arrives whether or not anyone remembers it. And **pre-write the parking message** from §10.5 now, while you are unattached to the outcome, and paste it into the same C29 entry — a message written in advance is a message you can actually send, and a message written on the day is a message that gets postponed. **Artefact:** a dated C29 kill-criterion entry containing a named artefact, two dates and a pre-written parking message, plus a calendar event on the kill date. **Success test:** the artefact named is something the _counterparty_ produces, not something you produce. A criterion that depends on your own output is a to-do, not a kill criterion.

**12.5 — Find the insider, and send one message today (30 min).** Per §9.3, write a shortlist of **three real names** who could be the one fuel-industry insider — ideally at least one retired or serving OMC divisional/area officer — with, for each: how you reach them (the warm path through the domain-expert director's network, not a cold email), and **the single question you would ask them**, written out in full. Rank them by how much of VSYST's IOCL plan currently rests on a guess that person could convert into a fact. Then send the rung-2 ask from §9.5 to the first name today: thirty minutes, on one named thing, no agenda beyond it. Start the **relationship ledger** from §9.6 in the same sitting with these three rows in it, and block the two monthly calls in the calendar before you close the laptop. **Artefact:** a three-name shortlist with warm paths and written first questions, one message sent with today's date, a relationship ledger page, and two recurring calendar blocks. **Success test:** your first question is one that neither you nor either co-director can currently answer, and answering it would change how you spend next quarter. If it would not change anything, you have written a networking question rather than a strategic one — rewrite it.
