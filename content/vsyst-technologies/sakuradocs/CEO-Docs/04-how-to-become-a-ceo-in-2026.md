# 04 — How to Become a CEO in 2026

_Phase 1 · Understand the Seat · Week 0. After this lesson you can name the four routes into the CEO chair and the specific blindness each one buys; score yourself honestly against fourteen CEO competencies using evidence rather than optimism; say out loud which of your engineering instincts help you in this seat and which are actively working against you; run an eight-quarter competence plan in which every quarter ends in an artefact; choose between a coach, a mentor, a peer group and a therapist at a price a pre-revenue bootstrapped founder can actually pay; read the canon in the order that fixes your worst problem first; present a three-person company honestly to a dealer and to an oil marketing company; and finish ninety days with a signed [[C01-ceo-charter-and-90-day-plan|C01]] and a written strategy kernel in [[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager|C04]]._

## Explain-it-like-I'm-5

A family runs a cloth shop on a busy Raipur street. For thirty years the father has stood at the counter. He knows which wholesaler will extend credit in a bad month, which customer pays late and must be watched, and roughly how many metres of a new print will move before Diwali. He has never written any of it down.

The son studied computers. He built the shop a billing program. It is a genuinely good program — bills that took four minutes take forty seconds, and the stock register stopped lying. One morning the father puts the keys on the counter and says: _you run it now._

The son knows the program cold. He does not know how to choose next season's stock, how to talk the wholesaler into thirty more days, how to tell a cousin on the payroll that there is no work for him, or what to do when a bigger shop opens across the road with a bank behind it. None of that came with the keys. **He has the title. He does not yet have the job.**

That is where you are. This lesson is about how the son learns the shop — deliberately, on a schedule, with evidence — instead of standing behind the counter improving the billing program, because improving the billing program is the one thing he already knows how to do well and it feels, hour by hour, exactly like work.

## 1. Four routes into the chair — and the two you are on

Most writing about "becoming a CEO" is about **getting** the seat: the career ladder, the search firm, the board interview. You already have the seat. Your problem is the other one — becoming **competent in it** — and the four routes still matter, because each route hands you a different starting kit of strengths and a different, specific blindness. Knowing which kit you were issued tells you what you must deliberately go and buy.

| #   | Route                            | How the seat arrives                                            | What it makes you good at                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            | What it makes you blind to                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 | What you must deliberately buy                                                                                  |
| --- | -------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | **Founder**                      | You started the company; the title is a description of the fact | Conviction, product judgment, speed, first-principles thinking, total context, and the moral authority to reverse a decision you yourself made. Ben Horowitz's case for founding CEOs is exactly this: founders hold "comprehensive knowledge" that is "nearly impossible to replicate", and it is easier for the person who invented the original model to abandon it ([a16z, 2010](https://a16z.com/why-we-prefer-founding-ceos/)) | The outside view. No boss has ever told you that you were wrong and been able to make it stick — no board, no investor, no manager. Your judgment has been tested against exactly one market, which you chose, and which has not yet paid you. You also cannot tell the difference between "I built this" and "I can run this"                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             | Benchmarks, peers, honest feedback, and one person whose "no" you have agreed in advance to take seriously (§5) |
| 2   | **Internal promotion**           | You ran a function or a P&L; the board moved you up             | Institutional knowledge, credibility with the staff, knowing exactly where the bodies are buried, relationships that survive the promotion                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           | The company as it _could_ be. Promotion rewards the person who ran the current model well, which trains incrementalism; and you carry loyalty debts to the people who backed you                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           | An outsider's diagnosis, and permission to break the thing you were promoted for running                        |
| 3   | **External hire**                | A search process; the board bought you for a pattern            | Pattern library, outside view, willingness to cut, no sentimental attachment to any existing decision                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                | Context and culture. Everything true-but-unwritten about the company is invisible for a year, and "we tried that in 2023" is an argument you cannot evaluate                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               | Time, listening, and one internal ally who will tell you what nobody says in meetings                           |
| 4   | **Succession / family business** | The previous generation hands it over                           | Long time horizon, inherited relationships, patient capital, a balance sheet, and a customer base that trusts a surname                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              | Legitimacy and authority. PwC's 12th Family Business Survey found **52% of Indian family businesses cite resistance from the senior generation** as the biggest barrier to next-generation leadership — against 29% globally — and 21% have delayed succession outright versus 10% globally; the next generation ends up with "high aspirations but little authority" ([Forbes India on PwC's 12th Family Business Survey, Feb 2026](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/news/indian-family-businesses-high-on-ambition-low-on-governance/2991239/1); [PwC India](https://www.pwc.in/services/entrepreneurial-and-private-business/pwcs-12th-family-business-survey.html)) | Written decision rights, a real board process, and at least one voice at the table who is not related to you    |

**You are route 1 with a route 4 overlay, and you have both blind spots at once.** Name them plainly, because everything else in this lesson is downstream of them.

**Blind spot A — the founder's untested judgment.** Nobody in your life has authority over your decisions. There is no board beyond the three founder-directors, no investor with a veto, no manager writing your appraisal. That is genuine freedom and it is also an unmeasured instrument. Every call you make is scored by you. The one honest scorer available — a customer paying money — has not yet been engaged at scale, because VSYST is pre-revenue. Until it is, you are flying on self-report.

**Blind spot B — the family company's authority gap.** VSYST is three founder-directors, family-founded, with a domain-expert director who has spent a working lifetime inside fuel distribution. That expertise is the company's single largest non-code asset and the [[COO-Docs/index|COO course]] is right that converting it into written mechanisms is job one. But the same relationship makes two things unnaturally hard: overruling him on a call inside his own domain, and being overruled by him on a call inside yours. The PwC number above is not about big industrial houses; it is about exactly this dynamic, and it says the senior generation's resistance — not the junior generation's ability — is the usual binding constraint. Deloitte's 2025 India survey found **96% of Indian family enterprises are still led by family members and 51% are second-generation** ([Deloitte India, Dec 2025](https://www.deloitte.com/in/en/about/press-room/india-s-family-enterprises-drive-economic-power.html)); the professionalisation problem is the norm here, not an edge case.

The counter to both is structural, not emotional, and it is the same counter: **write the decision rights down, before the disagreement.** That is [[C03-decision-rights-matrix|C03]] and the founder contracts in [[03-the-ceo-core-value-and-the-founder-contracts|lesson 03]]. A family argument about who decides pricing is unwinnable. A one-page matrix signed in a calm week, and minuted, is a different kind of object: you can point at it.

One more framing worth carrying, from Paul Graham's September 2024 essay: founders are routinely handed management advice designed for someone who did not found the company — "how to run a company you hadn't founded, how to run a company if you're merely a professional manager" ([Paul Graham, 2024](https://paulgraham.com/foundermode.html)). Read this lesson as a syllabus, not as an instruction to become a professional manager. Route 1 has real advantages. It just does not come with the skills in §2, and it actively conceals which ones you are missing.

## 2. The honest skills audit

The audit below is the most useful twenty minutes in Phase 1, and the easiest to do dishonestly. Three rules make it real.

**Rule 1 — rate today, not on your best day.** The question is not "could I do this if I concentrated?" It is "did I do this, recently, without being forced to?"

**Rule 2 — no evidence, subtract a point.** Evidence means an artefact or an incident a stranger could inspect: a signed document, a recording, a number that moved, a person who was hired or exited, a deal that closed. "I've read about it" is a 1.

**Rule 3 — a low score is information, not a verdict.** The CEO Genome Project assessed more than 17,000 C-suite executives, 2,000 of them CEOs, and found that charisma, confidence and pedigree had little bearing on CEO success; **only 7% of high performers had an Ivy League undergraduate degree and 8% had not graduated from college at all**. What separated the strong ones was four behaviours: deciding with speed and conviction, engaging for impact, adapting proactively, and delivering reliably ([Botelho, Powell, Kincaid & Wang, HBR, May 2017](https://hbr.org/2017/05/what-sets-successful-ceos-apart); [ghSMART CEO Genome](https://ceogenome.com/)). Every one of those four is a habit. Habits are scoreable and buildable, which is the entire premise of §4.

### 2.1 The rubric

| Score | Meaning                                      | The test                                                                                                |
| ----- | -------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **1** | Never done it                                | You would not know where to start. You have possibly read about it.                                     |
| **2** | Done it once, badly, with help               | It happened, someone else carried it, and you could not repeat it alone.                                |
| **3** | Can do it when it is on the calendar         | Output is acceptable. It does not happen unless scheduled, and you feel relief when it is cancelled.    |
| **4** | Do it by default, to an inspectable standard | It happens without anyone reminding you, and there is an artefact from the last 90 days that proves it. |
| **5** | Others learn it from you                     | You have taught it to someone else and improved their version of it.                                    |

A **3 is the honest ceiling for most founders on most rows in year one**, and that is fine. The point of the audit is not to reach fours everywhere; it is to find the two rows where a 1 or 2 is currently costing the company money, and to put those two into [[C01-ceo-charter-and-90-day-plan|C01]] as named 90-day development goals with a mechanism attached.

### 2.3 How to read your own score

Do not average it. The average hides the thing you need to see. Read it three ways:

| Read          | What to look for                                                                                                                      | What to do about it                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| ------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **The floor** | Your two lowest rows                                                                                                                  | These go into [[C01-ceo-charter-and-90-day-plan\|C01]] as named 90-day development goals, each with a mechanism (a booked rep, not an intention)                                                                                                 |
| **The shape** | Rows 1–6 are the **outward** job (strategy, customers, money); rows 7–14 are the **inward** job (people, comms, governance, yourself) | A founder who is 4s outward and 1s inward will build a company nobody can work in. A founder who is the reverse will build a lovely company that dies of no revenue. Phase 2 and Phase 3 of this course exist to correct whichever half is lower |
| **The lie**   | Any row where you gave yourself a 4 and could not name the artefact within ten seconds                                                | Subtract the point. Then decide whether the honest fix is to build the artefact or to stop pretending the row matters this year                                                                                                                  |

**Where the score lives.** Not in your head, and not in a note you never reopen. The audit block sits inside [[C01-ceo-charter-and-90-day-plan|C01]] with a date on it, and you re-score it at day 90 and then every second quarter alongside the §4 plan. A skills audit scored once is a mood; scored on a schedule it is a trend line, and a trend line is the only thing that will tell you whether the eight-quarter plan is working.

## 3. The technical founder's specific gap list

This section is about you. Read it slowly and argue with it in writing, not in your head.

Shipping code for years taught you a set of habits so deep you no longer experience them as choices. Some of them are the best equipment anyone brings to this seat. Some of them are actively wrong here, and the dangerous property of the wrong ones is that they still _feel_ like competence — they produce the same sensation of good work that they produced when you were writing software, right up until the quarter ends and there is no revenue.

### 3.2 What it mis-trains — the five that will cost you

| The instinct                                          | Why it is right in code                                                                                    | Why it is wrong in the seat                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       | The tell — how you catch yourself                                                                                                                                                                            | The counter-mechanism                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |

| **Comfort in the IDE as avoidance behaviour**         | Deep work in code is real work, and for years it was your highest-value hour                               | It is now, sometimes, the most expensive hour of your week — and it is the one that feels safest on a day when a dealer has gone quiet or a co-founder conversation is overdue. ### 3.3 The two things nobody will tell you

**You will get better feedback from the market than from your co-founders, and you will get it later.** Family co-founders protect you. Employees you do not yet have would protect themselves. The market is the only honest reviewer in the system, and it does not respond until you ship to it commercially. This is a structural argument for the §4 plan front-loading customers and revenue: not because revenue is the goal in quarter one, but because **revenue is the only unbiased feedback channel you own.**

**Founder mode is not permission to skip §2.** Graham's essay is frequently read as licence for founders to ignore management practice entirely. Read what it actually says: the advice founders are given is designed for professional managers running companies they did not found ([Paul Graham, 2024](https://paulgraham.com/foundermode.html)). That is an argument about which advice applies, not an argument that no skill applies. A founder who cannot fire, cannot price and cannot read a cash flow is not in founder mode. They are in the first blind spot from §1, with a slogan.

## 5. How CEOs actually learn

Five channels, in descending order of how much they teach per hour and ascending order of how comfortable they are. The discomfort ordering is not a coincidence: **reading is the cheapest, most seductive and least effective of the five**, and it is the one a technical founder will over-invest in, because it resembles study. A founder who has read thirty books and made three sales calls has optimised the wrong channel with great discipline.

| Channel                 | What it teaches that nothing else can                     | Cost at VSYST today            | The failure mode                                                |
| ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Doing**               | Judgment under real consequence                           | Free; costs courage            | Doing only the parts you already like                           |
| **Peers**               | Calibration — whether your problem is normal              | Low to moderate; some are free | Networking that never becomes a real conversation               |
| **Coaching**            | Your own blind spots, named by someone with no stake      | ₹0 to very high; see §5.4      | Buying a friend, or buying a consultant and calling it coaching |
| **Structured feedback** | What people actually think of working with you            | Free; costs ego                | Asking in a way that makes honesty socially expensive           |
| **Reading**             | Vocabulary, frameworks, other people's expensive mistakes | ₹0–₹15,000 for the whole canon | Substituting reading for the other four                         |

### 5.5 Structured feedback — a 360 with three people

A CEO's feedback problem is structural: the higher the authority, the more expensive honesty becomes for the person delivering it. In a company of three, two of whom are family, it is worse — the cost of honesty is paid at dinner as well as at work.

You cannot run a conventional 360 with three people; you can run something better, because you can run it in person and you can close the loop within a week.

**The three-person CEO 360, quarterly, 40 minutes total:**

1. **Ask three named people.** Both co-founder-directors, plus one outsider who has watched you work — a customer you are close to, a contractor, a mentor, a peer from §5.3. The outsider is what stops it becoming a family conversation.
2. **Ask in writing, and ask narrow questions.** Broad questions get polite answers. Use exactly these four:
   - _What should I keep doing?_
   - _What one thing should I stop doing?_ (one, not "some things" — forcing a single answer defeats politeness)
   - _What decision of mine in the last quarter would you have made differently, and why?_
   - _What do you think I believe about this company that isn't true?_
3. **Take it in writing first, then talk.** Writing lets the other person edit toward honesty rather than toward the room's mood.
4. **Say nothing except thank you** in the session itself. No defending, no context-setting, no "well, the reason for that was". Explaining is how a founder teaches everyone that feedback is expensive.
5. **Close the loop within seven days.** Write back: here is what I heard, here is the one thing I am changing, here is what I am not changing and why. Not closing the loop is the single fastest way to ensure the next round is worthless.
6. **Log it in [[C01-ceo-charter-and-90-day-plan|C01]]** alongside the §2 re-score, so the audit and the 360 are read together. Your self-score and other people's score of you diverging on the same row is the most useful signal in this lesson.

**Getting honest input from a family co-founder.** This is the hard one, and generic advice fails at it. Four things that work:

- **Separate the hats explicitly, out loud, at the start.** "I am asking as CEO, not as your son / brother / cousin." The [[COO-Docs/index|COO course]] uses the same device for shared roles; it works because it gives the other person permission to answer in a register they would otherwise avoid.
- **Do it at the office, on a calendar invite, in working hours.** Location and formality carry more of the signal than the words do. A conversation held at the dining table will be answered by the family, not by the director.
- **Ask about the decision, not about you.** "Would you have priced it differently?" is answerable. "How am I doing?" is not — it forces the other person to choose between honesty and kindness, and family chooses kindness.
- **Use the outsider as the pressure valve.** When you genuinely cannot get a straight answer from inside the family, put the question to the third respondent and bring the answer back as a third party's view. It is easier to disagree with an absent stranger than with a present relative, and the disagreement is the data you wanted.

If you get nothing usable after two honest attempts, that is itself a finding — record it in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] and treat "no reliable internal feedback channel" as a named company risk in [[C30-pre-mortem-crisis-playbook-and-succession|C30]], because it is one.

## 7. The first 90 days as a founder-CEO formalising the seat

The standard new-CEO 90-day arc is _listen → analyse → implement_, and it is built for someone who does not know the company. **You know the company.** That removes about half the work and adds a problem the outside hire does not have: there is no forcing function. Nobody is watching your first ninety days, no board is expecting a plan in week twelve, and the seat will drift back into "founder who codes and sometimes sells" unless you manufacture the forcing function yourself.

So the compressed arc is: **name the seat → write the direction → sign it and start the cadence.** And the forcing function is a date, a signature and a minute in the company's own records.

**What this 90 days does _not_ include, deliberately.** The operating system — cadence below board level, SOPs, the scorecard, the compliance calendar, the vendor register, the hiring process, the RACI and delegation-of-authority matrix — belongs to the [[COO-Docs/index|COO course]] and is built on the COO's own timeline. Where the two meet, the split is: **the COO owns the weekly ops meeting and the operational scorecard; the CEO owns the direction those feed, the decision rights above them, and the board-level record.** [[03-the-ceo-core-value-and-the-founder-contracts|Lesson 03]] draws that line properly; do not redraw it here.

**Three ninety-day failure modes specific to a founder formalising the seat:**

1. **The charter that nobody countersigns.** A document you wrote about yourself, filed in your own folder, is a diary. It becomes a charter when two other directors sign it and the signature is minuted. Companies Act mechanics for that live in [[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties|lesson 09]].
2. **The strategy that never leaves the vault.** [[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager|C04]] must be read aloud to the other two directors and disagreed with. An unopposed strategy has not been tested; it has been tolerated.
3. **The cadence that survives four weeks and dies in week six.** Book it as a recurring calendar event now, with a named agenda, and put "did the CEO weekly happen?" on the founder meeting agenda as a standing line. The [[COO-Docs/index|COO course]]'s line applies exactly: mechanisms, not intentions.

## 8. What to do about the title in public

You hold a real title with real legal weight, at a company small enough that saying it out loud can work against you. Both halves of that sentence matter.

### 8.1 The title has legal meaning in India — know what it is

"Chief Executive Officer" is not a marketing word here. Under the Companies Act 2013, **section 2(18) defines a CEO as "an officer of a company, who has been designated as such by it"**, and a CEO so designated falls within the definition of **Key Managerial Personnel** ([ClearTax on KMP](https://cleartax.in/s/key-managerial-personnel-kmp-under-companies-act-2013); [section 203 text](https://ca2013.com/203-appointment-of-key-managerial-personnel/)). Three practical consequences:

| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Mandatory KMP appointment under s.203 does not apply to private companies**  | Section 203 was "intended to apply to only specific classes of companies, with private companies being excluded from its ambit"; separately, private companies with paid-up share capital of ₹10 crore or more must appoint a whole-time company secretary under the Companies (Appointment and Remuneration of Managerial Personnel) Rules, 2014 ([Cyril Amarchand Mangaldas, Oct 2022](https://corporate.cyrilamarchandblogs.com/2022/10/key-managerial-personnel-appointments-applicability-of-section-203-of-the-companies-act-2013-to-private-companies-does-the-nclat-order-cast-the-net-too-wide/)) | VSYST is nowhere near ₹10 crore paid-up capital. **VERIFY LIVE with the CS** — thresholds and rule numbers change                                                               |
| **But designating one voluntarily can pull you into the regime**               | An NCLAT order of 7 September 2022 held that where a private company that is otherwise exempt _chooses_ to appoint a KMP such as a CFO, it must then comply with section 203 for that appointment (same source, which questions whether the net was cast too wide)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         | If VSYST formally designates a CEO by board resolution, ask the CS what follows — appointment by board resolution, terms recorded, and any filing consequences. **VERIFY LIVE** |

## 9. The CEO market in 2026 — for context, not envy

This section exists for three reasons, none of which is your ego. **One:** the day VSYST hires an executive, or you negotiate your own arrangement with your co-directors, these are the numbers you budget from. **Two:** knowing the base rates tells you what the seat's failure modes look like from outside. **Three:** at some point someone — a customer, a family member, an investor — will ask why a founder is the CEO, and you should be able to answer with data rather than defensiveness.

Everything below is **directional and VERIFY LIVE** before any real decision. Compensation data is self-reported or payroll-derived, changes annually, and Indian figures in particular come from recruiting sites rather than audited surveys.

### 9.1 Founder-CEO versus professional CEO

The founder-replacement base rate is the most quoted number in startup governance, and it comes from Noam Wasserman's study of 212 American start-ups founded in the late 1990s and early 2000s ([Wasserman, HBR, Feb 2008](https://hbr.org/2008/02/the-founders-dilemma); expanded in [_The Founder's Dilemmas_, Princeton University Press, 2013](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691158303/the-founders-dilemmas)). The headline finding is that most founders do not remain CEO — a majority had lost the seat within a few years, and only a minority led their companies to an IPO, with most departures being involuntary rather than chosen. **VERIFY LIVE** the exact percentages against the source before quoting them anywhere that matters; the study is now over fifteen years old and describes venture-funded American companies, not bootstrapped Indian ones.

Two things follow for VSYST, and they point in opposite directions:

- **The mechanism does not apply to you.** Founders are usually replaced by a board acting for investors. VSYST has no outside capital and no board beyond the three founder-directors ([[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties|lesson 09]]). Nobody can remove you. That is genuine safety, and it is also blind spot A from §1 restated as a fact about the cap table: **the correction mechanism that catches other founders does not exist here, so you have to build it yourself** — which is what §5.5's 360 and [[C03-decision-rights-matrix|C03]]'s written decision rights are for.
- **The case for founder-CEOs is real and specific.** Horowitz's argument is that founding CEOs are better at _finding_ new product cycles while professional CEOs are better at _maximising_ existing ones, for three reasons: comprehensive knowledge that is nearly impossible to replicate, the moral authority to abandon assumptions you yourself created, and a time horizon measured in a career rather than a vesting schedule ([a16z, April 2010](https://a16z.com/why-we-prefer-founding-ceos/)). VSYST is squarely in the "finding" phase — pre-revenue, pre-product-market-fit, one district at a time. This is the phase founder-CEOs are good at.

### 9.2 Indian founder compensation at pre-revenue and seed stage

The honest summary: **there is no authoritative published survey of Indian pre-revenue founder salaries**, and anyone quoting a precise figure is extrapolating. What can be said with confidence:

| **Founder pay at pre-revenue is a runway decision, not a market decision** | Every rupee of founder salary is a rupee off runway, and at zero revenue runway is founder savings. ### 9.3 What a hired CEO costs, and when a founder should step aside

You will not hire a CEO. You may one day hire executives, and the same logic applies: **a hired executive costs market cash plus a small equity grant, while a founder holds founder equity and takes below-market cash.** They are different species of compensation and comparing them directly is a category error. The [[COO-Docs/index|COO course]]'s lesson 04 §6 sets out the Indian executive-pay benchmarks and the rule that matters — express equity in absolute rupee value, not percentage — and this course does not repeat them. Use [[C20-compensation-bands-and-esop-design|C20]] when the time comes.

**When should a founder step aside?** The literature gives you a test, not a rule. Three honest triggers:

1. **The company's binding constraint is a skill you have decided not to build.** Not "cannot" — _decided not to_. If row 3 (selling) or row 5 (finance literacy) of §2 is a 1 in year three and you have made no attempt at the reps, the seat is being under-served by choice.
2. **You are consistently the bottleneck and have refused every mechanism that would fix it.** [[16-decisions-delegation-and-not-being-the-bottleneck|Lesson 16]] gives you the mechanisms. Refusing all of them is a decision about the company, whatever it feels like.
3. **You no longer want it.** The most common real reason and the least often stated. Wanting to build rather than run is legitimate — the honest version is to change roles deliberately, not to hold the title and do the other job (§3.2's last row).

At VSYST, the realistic near-term version of "stepping aside" is **not** replacing the CEO. It is narrowing the seat: handing operations to the COO seat the [[COO-Docs/index|COO course]] defines, and — later — hiring a sales leader so that founder-led selling stops being founder-only selling. [[C30-pre-mortem-crisis-playbook-and-succession|C30]] is where you write down what happens if the CEO is unavailable, which is a question a three-person family company must answer on paper long before it is urgent.

### 9.4 The failure base rates worth carrying

Not envy, but calibration. CB Insights' March 2026 analysis of 431 companies that shut down since 2023 found the ranked causes: **ran out of capital (70%), poor product-market fit (43%), bad timing or macro conditions (29%), unsustainable unit economics (19%)** ([CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/research/report/startup-failure-reasons-top/)). Read it as a job description. Two of the top four are Fred Wilson's third job (cash), one is his first (strategy and direction), and the fourth is a pricing-and-unit-economics problem you own. **Nothing on that list is an engineering failure.** That is the whole argument of §3 in one dataset.

## 10. At VSYST — applying this now

- **You are route 1 with a route 4 overlay, and the overlay is the under-managed half.** The founder blind spot is well documented and you will read about it constantly. The family blind spot is not — and PwC's finding that 52% of Indian family businesses face senior-generation resistance to leadership transition, against 29% globally, is describing a three-person company in Raipur as much as a listed house ([Forbes India / PwC, Feb 2026](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/news/indian-family-businesses-high-on-ambition-low-on-governance/2991239/1)). The mitigation is a signed [[C03-decision-rights-matrix|C03]], written in a calm week, minuted. Do it before the first real pricing disagreement, not after.
- **Score §2 this week, with evidence, and expect it to be ugly.** A technical founder at a pre-revenue company will typically score 3–4 on rows 11 and 1, and 1–2 on rows 3, 6, 8 and 10. That is the normal starting shape. It becomes a problem only if it is still that shape in eight quarters.
- **Your single highest-leverage behaviour change is the weekly customer-conversation floor.** Not a target — a floor, booked before the week starts, with the number visible to your co-founders. Everything in §3.2 conspires against it, which is exactly why it needs a mechanism rather than an intention.
- **You cannot stop coding, so make the rule precise.** VSYST has one engineer and it is you; "stop coding" is not available advice. The workable version is a written rule with two clauses: **code inside a declared block, never in a gap**, and **never in the hour after an avoided conversation.** Put the rule in [[C01-ceo-charter-and-90-day-plan|C01]] where a co-founder can see it, and let exercise 11.5 tell you whether you are keeping it.
- **Start the peer group; do not wait for a paid one.** Four to six founders, monthly, fixed format, ₹0. [36Inc](https://36inc.in/) in Raipur is the nearest concentration of candidates and the [[COO-Docs/index|COO course]] already treats the local ecosystem as infrastructure rather than socialising. The paid CEO organisations have eligibility thresholds VSYST does not meet yet; note the trigger and move on.
- **Buy a therapist before you buy a coach.** A retained executive coach is a post-revenue purchase — the anchor at the top of that market is US\$10,000/month ([Mochary Method](https://www.mocharymethod.com/)), which settles the question for this year. Peer coaching, one paid session a quarter, and a formalised mentor cover most of what a coach's cadence provides. Mental-health support is a different budget line and should not wait for revenue.
- **On the title: be a Founder to dealers, a Director on paper, and a CEO to candidates and investors.** All three are true. What actually opens the IOCL door is DPIIT recognition, vendor registration and documents — not seniority ([Startup India](https://www.startupindia.gov.in/content/sih/en/public_procurement.html)).
- **Ninety days ends with two signatures, or it did not happen.** [[C01-ceo-charter-and-90-day-plan|C01]] signed and minuted, and [[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager|C04]] dated and disagreed with by both co-directors. There is no external forcing function in a founder-run company; the board minute is the one you manufacture.
- **Do not let the reading list become the work.** Two books in Q1 — Rumelt and Martin — plus one Mochary chapter a fortnight. That is the whole quarter's reading. The rest of the hours belong to §5.1's three reps.

## 11. Exercises

**11.1 — Score the fourteen (30 min).** Reproduce the §2.2 table in [[C01-ceo-charter-and-90-day-plan|C01]] with the two right-hand columns filled: a 1–5 score against the §2.1 rubric, and one line of evidence per row — an artefact, an incident, a number, a person. Apply the rule: no evidence, subtract a point. Then write three sentences: your two lowest rows, why each is currently costing the company something specific, and what the first rep would be. Date it. **Output: a dated fourteen-row skills audit inside C01, with two named 90-day development goals.**

**11.2 — Write, circulate and sign C01 (45 min).** Fill [[C01-ceo-charter-and-90-day-plan|C01]] end to end: what the CEO seat at VSYST exists to do, in one paragraph; what you own and — harder — what you do not; the three Wilson jobs translated into VSYST terms; the §11.1 audit; the 90-day plan from §7 with real dates; and the coding rule from §10. Circulate it to both co-directors for comment, take at least one change from each, then sign it and get it minuted at the next directors' meeting using [[C17-board-pack-agenda-and-minutes|C17]]. **Output: a signed CEO charter with a board minute referencing it.**

**11.3 — Build the eight-quarter plan (30 min).** Copy the §4 table into the `Annual Plan & OKRs` tab of [vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx](toolkit/vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx) and make it yours: replace the generic themes where VSYST's timeline demands it, put a real end-date on each quarter, name the artefact and the one number for each, and adjust the reading in the "outside input" column to start with whichever §6 tier fixes your two lowest §2 rows. Then book Q1: two recurring study blocks a week for thirteen weeks, each invite named for its artefact. **Output: eight dated quarters with artefacts and numbers, plus twenty-six calendar blocks that exist.**

**11.4 — Book one real conversation (20 min).** Two messages, sent today, not drafted. First: invite three to five founders you actually know to a monthly peer group, with the format from §5.3 stated in the message — one number each, one problem each, one commitment each — and a first date. Second: approach one person to be a formalised mentor, asking for one hour a month for six months with a written agenda each time, and name the first agenda in the message. If you cannot think of five founders, the first message goes to [36Inc](https://36inc.in/) instead. **Output: a calendar invite with at least one other human on it, and a sent mentor request.**

**11.5 — The avoidance audit (20 min).** Pull two numbers for the last thirty days: how many commits you made, and how many conversations you had with a real or prospective dealer. Then pull a third: for each of the five largest coding sessions, what was on your calendar that day or the day before that you did not do? Write the pattern down in one paragraph in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] and convert it into one written rule with a trigger you can actually notice. Re-run this at day 90 alongside the §2 re-score. **Output: a decision-journal entry naming your avoidance pattern, and one rule in C01 that a co-founder can hold you to.**
