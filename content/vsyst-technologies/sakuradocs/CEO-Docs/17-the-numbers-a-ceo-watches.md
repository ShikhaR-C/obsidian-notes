# 17 — The Numbers a CEO Watches

_Phase 4 · Running the Company and Yourself · Months 6–24. After this lesson you can state the difference between the COO's weekly scorecard and the CEO's monthly dashboard and refuse to run both, say five numbers — cash, burn, runway, paying GSTINs, net new — from memory and be right to the rupee, decompose paying GSTINs into a metric tree that separates the results you can only watch from the inputs a person can move this week, fill and read the eighteen-row `CEO Dashboard` tab with a definition, a source, an owner, a direction, a goal and a red line on every row, read a P&L, balance sheet and cash-flow statement the way a CEO reads them and ask your CA the five questions that matter, write down VSYST's binding definitions of MRR, churn, NRR, activation, CAC payback and LTV so nobody argues about them in year three, measure honestly when your N is six, spot the six specific ways your own numbers will lie to you, and run a monthly business review that ends in decisions and feeds the board pack._

## Explain-it-like-I'm-5

A family in Raipur owns a marriage garden — a lawn with a shamiana, a kitchen, a generator and eighty parking spaces. The manager who runs it walks the property every morning with a diary. Did the water tanker come? How many chairs are broken? Is the generator serviced? Did yesterday's caterer clear his mess? He writes seven or eight things down every day, and if any of them is wrong, he fixes it before lunch. That diary is how the place stays running.

The owner does not read the manager's diary. Once a month he sits with a cup of tea and asks four completely different questions. **How much money is actually in the account today?** **How many Saturdays are booked for the season, and how many did we book this month?** **Is the new air-conditioned banquet hall on the highway taking our weddings?** And: **did we pay the electricity board, and is the fire licence renewed?** Four questions, once a month. If the answers are good, he does not touch the manager. If the answer to the third one is bad, no amount of fixing broken chairs will save the business, and he needs to think about what the garden should become.

Both people are watching numbers. They are not the same numbers, they are not on the same clock, and — this is the part almost everyone gets wrong — **the owner does not get his four answers by reading the manager's diary harder**. The diary is a good diary. It just does not contain the question "should we still be in this business."

This lesson builds the owner's four questions into eighteen rows, on a monthly beat, with a source and a red line on each. The manager's diary already exists: it is the COO's weekly scorecard, and this course does not touch it.

## 1. Two instruments, one company

The COO course owns the **weekly KPI scorecard**. That is settled, and it is not a courtesy hand-off — it is the correct division. [[16-metrics-dashboards-and-scorecards|COO lesson 16]] builds the KPI tree, sets the RAG rules, names the source query behind every row, and installs the Monday-10:00 fill; [[T05-kpi-scorecard|T05]] is the template; the `KPI Scorecard` tab of `vsyst-coo-workbook.xlsx` is the live sheet. Its shape comes from EOS — **five to fifteen weekly numbers that give an absolute pulse on the business**, reported on-track or off-track in a five-minute segment ([EOS — Level 10 Meeting](https://www.eosworldwide.com/level-10-meeting)) — and its discipline comes from Amazon's Weekly Business Review, where you are _not_ allowed to discuss output metrics except in a reporting sense, because the way to hit an output is to drive the controllable inputs beneath it ([Commoncog — the Amazon WBR](https://commoncog.com/the-amazon-weekly-business-review/)).

The CEO's instrument is a different one. It answers a different question, on a different clock, for a different audience.

|                                 | **COO weekly scorecard** ([[T05-kpi-scorecard\|T05]])            | **CEO monthly dashboard** (`CEO Dashboard` tab, this lesson)                                                                                             |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **The question it answers**     | Is the machine healthy **this week**?                            | Is the strategy working, and **are we going to survive**?                                                                                                |
| **Horizon**                     | 13 weeks trailing, one week ahead                                | 12 months trailing, 12–24 months ahead                                                                                                                   |
| **Rows**                        | 5–15 weekly, from a ~24-row tree                                 | **18**, fixed, grouped in five blocks                                                                                                                    |
| **Beat**                        | Filled Monday 10:00, read in the ops meeting                     | Filled by the **5th of the month**, read in the monthly business review (§9)                                                                             |
| **Audience**                    | The operating team; the COO reads it first                       | The CEO, the other two directors, and — unchanged — the board pack ([[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties\|lesson 09]]) and any future investor |
| **Who fills it**                | The metric's owner, personally, by Monday                        | The **CEO**, personally, by the 5th — including opening the bank statement with their own hands                                                          |
| **What triggers action**        | Red → issues list this Monday; red twice → automatic top-3 issue | Red → a named decision at the MBR; **red two months running → an automatic board agenda item, without a vote**                                           |
| **What a bad week/month means** | Fix the mechanism                                                | Possibly change the plan, the price, the market or the burn                                                                                              |
| **Top row**                     | The most controllable input of the quarter                       | **Cash in bank**                                                                                                                                         |
| **Lifespan of a row**           | Retired if it drives no decision in a quarter                    | Deleted if it drives no decision in **two quarters**                                                                                                     |

Two things make this a real distinction rather than a rebrand.

**The clock changes what a number can mean.** A weekly number is noise plus a little signal; you read it to catch a mechanism drifting. A monthly number is signal plus a little noise; you read it to catch a _strategy_ drifting. Rate-confirmation rate falling for two weeks is an operations problem the COO solves on Monday. Paying GSTINs flat for three months is not an operations problem at all — it is a question about whether the district motion works, and no Monday meeting can answer it. Andy Grove's rule that indicators should be paired and read as trends, not points, is the same insight from the other end ([Nat Eliason — notes on _High Output Management_](https://www.nateliason.com/notes/high-output-management-andy-grove)).

**The audience changes what a number must survive.** The scorecard is read by people who already know the context. The dashboard is read, eventually, by a bank, a partner's finance team, or an investor who will assume that a number you state is a number you can defend. Every row on the dashboard therefore carries a written definition and a source, and the definition does not change quietly (§4, §10).

### 1.1 The failure mode: the CEO who reads the COO's sheet

Here is what actually happens at a three-person company, and it happens by accident, not by laziness. The COO's scorecard exists and is good. The CEO opens it every Monday, gets interested, starts asking about the rate-confirmation dip, sends a WhatsApp about first-response time, and by month four the CEO has become a second COO with a worse view of operations and no view of the company.

Name the trap before naming the technique. **A CEO reading the operating scorecard as their primary instrument produces four specific harms:**

1. **The company loses its only strategy reader.** Nobody else in a three-person company is looking twelve months out. The COO cannot: their job is this week. If the CEO is also looking at this week, the twelve-month question goes unread until it is a crisis ([[15-the-ceo-operating-cadence-and-calendar|lesson 15]]).
2. **The COO loses ownership.** A number with two readers who both comment has one real owner and one heckler. The COO's authority over the operating system is the whole basis of the CEO–COO contract in [[03-the-ceo-core-value-and-the-founder-contracts|lesson 03]] and [[C03-decision-rights-matrix|C03]].
3. **The CEO starts optimising proxies.** Bezos's warning in the 2016 letter — _"Good process serves you so you can serve customers. But if you're not watchful, the process can become the thing"_ ([Amazon — 2016 letter to shareholders](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2016-letter-to-shareholders)) — applies exactly here. The scorecard is a proxy for the company's health. It is a very good proxy for the _operating_ half. It is close to useless as a proxy for "is this the right market."
4. **Cash stops being anybody's daily object.** Cash sits at row 19 of a 24-row operating tree. It sits at row 1 of the dashboard. Fred Wilson's third irreducible CEO job is _making sure there is always enough cash in the bank_ ([AVC — What A CEO Does](https://avc.com/2010/08/what-a-ceo-does/), 2010), and a CEO who reads cash as the twentieth number of the week is not doing it.

The counter-move is a rule, not a resolution:

> **The CEO reads the weekly scorecard once a month, in the MBR, as history — and never sends a message about a weekly row outside that meeting.** If a weekly row is so bad that the CEO must intervene between MBRs, that intervention goes to the COO as an issue, not to the number's owner as a comment.

And the mirror-image failure, which is worse and quieter: **two instruments, two definitions.** If the dashboard's "paying GSTINs" is counted differently from the scorecard's "paying dealer tenants," the company holds two truths, and every review begins with a reconciliation instead of a decision. The guard is §10's rule — **one metric-sources note for the whole company** (the COO owns the file; the CEO adds the rows only the dashboard carries), and one definition per metric no matter how many sheets display it.

## 2. The five numbers a CEO says from memory

There are five numbers you should be able to say, correctly, standing in a dealer's office with no phone, six months from now, at 9 PM, when someone asks.

```
   1.  Cash in bank                 ₹ ______        (today, all accounts, net of cheques issued)
   2.  Net monthly burn             ₹ ______        (trailing 3-month average)
   3.  Runway                       ______ months   (1 ÷ 2, floored at today's plan)
   4.  Paying customers             ______ GSTINs   (billed and collected, not signed)
   5.  Net new this month           ______ GSTINs   (added − lost, this calendar month)
```

That is the whole list. Not ten. Five.

### 2.1 Why these five

They are the five with the shortest causal path to the company ceasing to exist, and the shortest causal path to it not ceasing to exist. Nothing else on the dashboard has both properties.

Numbers 1, 2 and 3 are one fact stated three ways, and the reason to hold all three is that each one fails differently. Cash alone hides a burn that just doubled. Burn alone hides a cash balance that includes a customer advance you will have to deliver against. Runway alone hides both, and hides the fact that runway is a _quotient_ — it moves when either term moves, and founders reliably misattribute which. The literature on why startups die is unambiguous about the destination: running out of cash / failing to raise new capital is the most-cited reason in the post-mortem sample ([CB Insights — top reasons startups fail](https://www.cbinsights.com/research/report/startup-failure-reasons-top/)). Paul Graham's reduction is the one to carry: a company is **default alive** if it reaches profitability on the money it has left at its current growth rate, and **default dead** otherwise — and the trap he names, _"default dead + slow growth + not enough time to fix it,"_ is the fatal pinch ([Paul Graham — Default Alive or Default Dead?](https://paulgraham.com/aord.html), 2015). You cannot know which side of that line you are on without 1, 2 and 3 in your head at the same time. Compute it properly in [[C13-runway-burn-and-scenario-planner|C13]] and [[finance/07-phase-7-bootstrapping-runway-burn|Finance Phase 7]]; carry the answer in your head.

Numbers 4 and 5 are the only two that answer "is anyone actually buying this." Number 4 is a _level_ — it is the North Star, restated in the unit VSYST actually bills in ([[app-store-economics/08-dzzlo-subscription-strategy|the subscription decision]]: dealer companies pay per GSTIN, users are unlimited and free, billing is web-only). Number 5 is the _derivative_ — and it is the one that predicts. A level of twelve with a net new of +3 and a level of twelve with a net new of 0 are the same photograph of two completely different companies.

Note what is deliberately **not** on the list. Not MRR — because at VSYST's stage MRR is GSTINs × a price you set, so it adds no information and invites rounding. Not app downloads, not registered users, not GMV flowing through DZZLO, not uptime. Those are real numbers with real homes (§4, §8), but they are not the five you must be able to say cold.

### 2.2 The exact definitions, and the trap in each

The point of memorising is defeated if you memorise a soft number. Each of the five has a precise form and a specific way founders fudge it.

| #   | Number                 | The binding definition at VSYST                                                                                                                                                                                                                   | The trap                                                                                                                                                                   |
| --- | ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | **Cash in bank**       | Sum of all bank balances **today**, minus cheques issued but not cleared, minus any amount already committed and invoiced to you. Not the app balance, not including the FD you would break only in an emergency unless you say so.               | Counting an approved-but-undisbursed loan, a promised director infusion, or a customer's advance as if it were free cash. It is cash you owe work against.                 |
| 2   | **Net monthly burn**   | (Cash at start of month − cash at end of month), averaged over the **trailing three months**. Net, not gross: revenue already subtracted ([a16z — 16 Startup Metrics](https://a16z.com/16-startup-metrics/) defines gross vs net burn precisely). | Quoting last month because it was a good month. One quarter-end of statutory payments makes a single month meaningless; three months is the minimum honest window.         |
| 3   | **Runway**             | Cash (row 1) ÷ net burn (row 2), in months, at **today's** plan — no assumed revenue growth, no assumed cost cuts.                                                                                                                                | The "but we'll cut costs" runway. That is a _scenario_, and it belongs in [[C13-runway-burn-and-scenario-planner\|C13]]'s Bear column, not in the number you say out loud. |
| 4   | **Paying GSTINs**      | Distinct GSTINs on an active paid subscription where **an invoice has been raised and the money has been received**. Not signed. Not in trial. Not "committed verbally."                                                                          | Counting a dealer firm as one when it has three GSTINs, or counting three when only one is billed. The unit is the GSTIN because that is the billing unit.                 |
| 5   | **Net new this month** | GSTINs that started paying this calendar month **minus** GSTINs that stopped. Signed, gross-of-churn additions are a different number and are not this one.                                                                                       | Reporting gross adds and mentioning churn separately, or not at all. Net new is one number with a sign, and the sign is the information.                                   |

### 2.3 The discipline of not opening the laptop

Knowing these from memory is not a party trick; it is three separate mechanisms in one habit.

**It forces monthly contact with the primary source.** You cannot hold cash in your head unless you look at the bank statement yourself. Row 1 of the dashboard says "Bank statement, personally checked" for exactly this reason — not "from the CA," not "from the workbook." A CEO who has not personally opened the bank account in eight weeks is a CEO who will learn about a problem eight weeks late.

**It creates an error signal.** Say the number, then check it. The gap between what you said and what is true is the most useful diagnostic on this entire dashboard, and it is free. A gap under 5% means your instrument panel is connected. A gap over 20% on any of the five means you are running the company on a mental model that has drifted from the company. Exercise 12.2 makes this a dated, written artefact rather than a feeling.

**It buys you the right to be believed about everything else.** This is the part founders underrate.

### 2.4 What it signals when you cannot say them

A board member, a banker, a prospective partner's finance head, an OMC's commercial team, or an investor asks "what's your runway?" and you say "let me check." Here is what the listener concludes, in order, and none of it is about the number:

| The listener's inference                   | Why it is a reasonable inference                                                                                                                                         |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **You are not the one managing cash.**     | People know the numbers they personally manage. Nobody forgets the balance of an account they check weekly.                                                              |
| **There may be no monthly close at all.**  | If the number were produced every month by a mechanism, you would have seen it four days ago.                                                                            |
| **Your other numbers are also estimates.** | If the easiest number to know is not known, the harder ones are decorative. This is the inference that kills the meeting.                                                |
| **Diligence will be slow and expensive.**  | A data room built by a founder who does not track it themselves is a data room built after the term sheet, badly ([[C14-fundraise-readiness-and-data-room-index\|C14]]). |
| **Fred Wilson's third job is unattended.** | Vision, talent, cash. Two of the three cannot be checked in a meeting. This one can ([AVC — What A CEO Does](https://avc.com/2010/08/what-a-ceo-does/)).                 |

The corollary is the useful part: **a CEO who says all five instantly, with the definitions attached, buys credibility on the unverifiable claims** — the market read, the roadmap, the team. Elad Gil's framing in [_High Growth Handbook_](https://press.stripe.com/high-growth-handbook) is that the CEO's job at every stage includes being the company's most reliable narrator; reliability is demonstrated on the numbers that can be checked. Getting the five right is the cheapest credibility VSYST will ever buy, and it costs one hour a month.

One honesty note for VSYST today: at pre-revenue, rows 4 and 5 are **0** and **0**. Say them anyway, and say them without flinching. "Zero paying GSTINs, zero net new, ₹X in the bank, ₹Y a month net burn, so N months of runway, and the first paid conversion is the only thing I'm working on" is a _strong_ answer. "About twenty-something dealers are using it" is a weak one, because it answers a question nobody asked with a number that isn't on the list.

## 3. The metric tree

A **metric tree** is the answer to "why is this number on my dashboard?" Every row must trace up to the one output that defines success, and down to something a human being can move on a Tuesday. Without the tree you accumulate rows; with it you can delete rows, which is harder and more valuable.

The COO course builds the operating tree — a North Star (paying, active dealer tenants) with five branches, twenty-four rows, mostly leading, filled weekly ([[16-metrics-dashboards-and-scorecards|COO 16]] §2–§3). **This section builds the same tree from the CEO's end and for a different purpose: not to pick weekly rows, but to know which nodes are results you can only watch and which are inputs you can actually buy.** The tree is shared; the reading is not.

### 3.1 Output metrics and controllable input metrics

Amazon's vocabulary is the one worth adopting because it is the sharpest. **Output metrics** are what you care about — revenue, free cash flow, engaged users. **Controllable input metrics** are the specific activities that, when driven, move the outputs — "add 500 new products in the amazon.fr Musical Instruments category," "run at least 20 newsletter ads per month." The WBR's rule is that outputs may be _reported_ but not _discussed_; the discussion is entirely about inputs ([Commoncog — the Amazon WBR](https://commoncog.com/the-amazon-weekly-business-review/); the mechanism is documented at book length in [_Working Backwards_](https://www.workingbackwards.com/) by Bryar and Carr, whose "Input Metrics" material is the fullest public treatment).

The test for whether something is a controllable input has three parts, and all three must pass:

1. **Can a named person move it this month by doing more or less of something specific?** ("Founder conversations" passes. "Brand awareness" does not.)
2. **If it moves, does the output move — with a lag you can name?** (Conversations → demos → pilots, roughly 3–8 weeks at VSYST. If you cannot name the lag, you have not established the link.)
3. **Is it hard to game without also doing the real work?** (This is the Goodhart guard; see §8.)

Grove's version of the same idea is that you should measure the activity, not just the result, because by the time the result arrives the opportunity to influence it is gone ([Nat Eliason — _High Output Management_ notes](https://www.nateliason.com/notes/high-output-management-andy-grove)). 4DX's second discipline says it in five words — act on the lead measures ([4DX — Perdoo](https://www.perdoo.com/resources/online-guides/4dx)).

### 3.2 VSYST's tree, branch one: the funnel to a paying GSTIN

```
                          PAYING GSTINs  (output — the North Star, in billing units)
                                   ▲
                      ┌────────────┴────────────┐
              RETAINED GSTINs               NET NEW GSTINs
              (output, lagging)             (output, monthly derivative)
                      ▲                             ▲
                      │                             │
            ACTIVATED TENANTS            ─── the acquisition funnel ───
            (input, controllable)                   ▲
                      ▲                             │
                      │                    PAID CONVERSIONS  (output of the funnel)
        ┌─────────────┴──────────┐                  ▲
   days-to-first-        ≥1 customer                │
   invoice (input)       linked (input)      PILOTS RUNNING  (input — you choose how many)
                                                    ▲
                                             DEMOS DELIVERED  (input — you book them)
                                                    ▲
                                          FOUNDER CONVERSATIONS  (input — the root)
                                                    ▲
                                        DISTRICT COVERAGE / NAMED LIST
                                             (input — you build the list)
```

Read it bottom-up, because that is the direction causation runs. The **root controllable input is founder conversations** — the number of real conversations with a named dealer about their ledger this month. It is fully controllable (it is a calendar decision), it has a nameable lag to paid conversion, and it is expensive to fake because a fake conversation produces no notes. It is why the dashboard carries "Founder customer conversations this month" as a row (§4) and why [[07-customers-markets-and-founder-led-sales|lesson 07]] insists on the script: the input is only useful if each unit is the same size. Paul Graham's _"the most common unscalable thing founders have to do at the start is to recruit users manually"_ is the strategic justification for spending the CEO's scarcest resource here ([Paul Graham — Do Things That Don't Scale](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html), 2013).

**Activation is the hinge.** It sits between the two output halves — it is the last input you control before retention becomes a thing that merely happens to you. The COO course defines it and owns the mechanism: **≥1 customer linked and ≥1 invoice raised within 14 days of signup** ([[10-customer-operations-support-and-success|COO 10]] §7). The CEO does not redefine it. The CEO watches it because activation is the earliest honest predictor of churn (§6.5), and because "days to first invoice" is a time-based measure that works even when N is six (§7).

### 3.3 VSYST's tree, branch two: the value chain inside a tenant

The funnel gets you a paying GSTIN. The _product loop_ decides whether it stays one. This is the branch that is specific to DZZLO and that no generic SaaS metric post will give you.

```
   ACTIVE DEALERS                     input   — dealers who opened the app this week
        │
        ▼
   CONFIRMED RATES                    input   — rates confirmed in the 10 PM–6 AM window
        │   (the front door of the whole loop)
        ▼
   ORDERS PLACED                      input   — orders against confirmed rates
        │
        ▼
   DISPATCHES COMPLETED (driver OTP)  input   — the physical event, verified
        │
        ▼
   INVOICES RAISED                    output  — PRODUCT / CASH_REIMBURSE / GST tiers
        │
        ▼
   PAYMENTS RECONCILED                output  — voucher matched, ledger closed
        │
        ▼
   → TRUST → RENEWAL → RETAINED GSTIN
```

Two readings a CEO takes from this chain that a COO does not.

**Where the chain breaks tells you what the product is.** If dealers confirm rates but never raise invoices, DZZLO is a rate-communication tool and the pricing should be a rate-communication tool's pricing. If they invoice but never reconcile payments, the ledger claim in the positioning ([[06-strategy-ii-moats-positioning-and-the-business-model|lesson 06]]) is not yet earned. The chain is a strategy diagnostic wearing a funnel's clothes.

**The conversion rate between adjacent stages is the switching-cost meter.** Vertical SaaS gets its stickiness from becoming the system the business runs on — employees carry it between jobs, and suppliers and customers start mandating it ([Tidemark — Vertical SaaS truisms](https://www.tidemarkcap.com/vskp-chapter/vertical-saas-truisms)). Rates → orders → invoices → reconciliation _is_ that mechanism at VSYST, stage by stage. A dealer who has reconciled ninety days of payments in DZZLO cannot leave in an afternoon. A dealer who only confirms rates can.

The discipline this table enforces: **at the monthly review, you may report the outputs, but you may only make decisions about the inputs.** "Paying GSTINs are flat" is not a decision. "We held six founder conversations against a goal of twenty, so the root input is starved, and the fix is four district days in the calendar before the 15th" is a decision. This is the Amazon rule imported wholesale, and it is the single most useful thing in this lesson.

One warning that belongs here rather than in §8. A tree with only inputs at the bottom and only outputs at the top is a _hypothesis about causation_, and at N=6 you have not proven any of the arrows. Write the tree in pencil, mark each arrow with the confidence you actually have, and treat the arrows as assumptions in the register ([[05-strategy-i-diagnosis-and-the-strategy-kernel|lesson 05]], `Strategy Kernel` tab C). The first year of running the dashboard is partly an experiment in whether your tree is the right tree.

## 4. The CEO dashboard for VSYST

This is the instrument. It is the `CEO Dashboard` tab of [vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx](toolkit/vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx) reproduced exactly — same rows, same order, same goals and red lines, same owners. **Fill one column per month, by the 5th.** Status computes from Direction, Goal and Red line; you never type a colour.

Read the `#` column as the workbook prints it: the group headers occupy numbers 4, 10, 15 and 19, which is why the metric numbers skip. There are **eighteen metrics**.

**All goals and red lines below are illustrative starting values set on 2026-08-25 — `**VERIFY LIVE**` before you treat any of them as a commitment. The rupee thresholds must be re-set against your actual bank balance and the trailing-three-month burn with your CA; the customer counts must be re-set at the first annual plan ([[C28-annual-plan-and-ceo-okr-sheet|C28]]).** Rows 3 and 4 are a matched pair in the other direction — row 3 is already net of row 4, so never subtract it twice. Rows 5 and 6 are a matched pair and must never drift apart: **the MRR goal is the paying-GSTIN goal × the [[C11-pricing-and-packaging-decision-sheet|C11]] list price** — 20 × ₹1,799 ≈ ₹36,000 green, 8 × ₹1,799 ≈ ₹14,400 red. ### 4.1 Defending the two unusual rows

Every other row on this sheet appears on some version of every startup dashboard. These two do not, and they are the two that make it VSYST's dashboard rather than a template.

**Row 22 — days since the CEO last spoke to a paying dealer.**

The objection is obvious: this measures the CEO's calendar, not the business. That is precisely why it belongs. Three defences.

_It is the only row that fails silently and catastrophically._ Every other number on this sheet has a mechanism behind it that will keep producing a number whether or not you pay attention. Customer contact has no mechanism. It decays by default, invisibly, replaced by product work and admin, and by the time it shows up in churn you are two quarters late. It is a **controllable input with a long lag to a lagging output** — exactly the class of thing that requires a dashboard row, because willpower does not survive a busy month.

_It is a leading indicator of the specific way technical founder-CEOs fail._ The named trap in [[13-product-and-technology-leadership-for-a-technical-ceo|lesson 13]] is the CEO who retreats into the codebase because the codebase gives clean feedback and dealers do not. This row is the tripwire on that retreat. Twenty-one days without a dealer's voice and a red light appears next to your name, in front of the other two directors, monthly.

_It protects the strategy, not the relationship._ Graham's point about recruiting users manually is not about sales volume, it is about _information_ ([Do Things That Don't Scale](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)); YC's standing advice to talk to users is the same claim ([Y Combinator Library](https://www.ycombinator.com/library)). A dealer conversation is how the assumption register (row 12) gets tested, how the objection bank stays current, and how you learn that the new AC banquet hall on the highway is taking your Saturdays. The goal of 7 days and the red line at 21 are deliberately tight: **VERIFY LIVE** against your own calendar reality after three months and raise them only with a written reason in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]].

**Row 21 — money-affecting product incidents.**

The objection: incidents are the COO's domain (COO [[12-product-and-engineering-operations|12]] owns monitoring, [[T17-incident-postmortem|T17]] owns the postmortem), and uptime is already tracked weekly. Correct — and this row is not uptime.

_This row counts a category, not a severity._ DZZLO's entire proposition is that a dealer can trust a number in it about money: a rate, an invoice tier, a TCS addition past the ₹50 lakh threshold (**VERIFY LIVE** with the CA against the current year's rate chart — see [TDS/TCS rate chart FY 2026-27](https://blog.tdsman.com/2026/03/tds-tcs-rate-chart-fy-2026-27/)), a voucher, a reconciled balance. An API outage is an operations problem; a **wrong rupee figure** is a strategy problem, because it destroys the one thing the company is selling. A three-hour outage and a ₹4,000 double-posted voucher are the same "1 incident" to an uptime chart and are not remotely the same event to a dealer's accountant.

_Trust is a lagging output with no other instrument._ You cannot measure "do dealers trust the ledger." You can measure the events that destroy it. The goal is 0 and the red line is 1 because in this category the second occurrence is not twice as bad as the first — it is categorically worse, since the first can be an accident and the second is a pattern. The `Annual Plan & OKRs` tab already carries this as a key result under _"Earn the right to be trusted with a dealer's money"_ with the note **"One is too many. This row never gets deleted"** — the one row exempted from the two-quarter deletion rule.

_It is the row that will matter most later._ When VSYST talks to an OMC, a payment gateway's risk team, or a certification auditor ([[certification-and-standards-roadmap|Certification and Standards Roadmap]]), "zero money-affecting incidents in 14 months, here is the log" is a sentence with commercial value. Start counting before anyone asks.

## 5. Reading the three statements as a CEO

Once a month your CA sends a P&L, a balance sheet and (if you asked for it) a cash-flow statement. Most founders either don't open them or read them like a report card. Both are wrong. **The accountant's job is to make the statements correct. Your job is to read them for the three or four things they say about your strategy that nothing else says.**

The mechanics — what each statement is, how they interlock, how a change in one ripples into the other two — are taught properly and at length in [[finance/03-phase-3-three-statements|Finance Phase 3]], and the runway and burn machinery in [[finance/07-phase-7-bootstrapping-runway-burn|Phase 7]]. **This section does not repeat them.** Read Phase 3 once, then come back here for the reading protocol. The full course index is [[finance/00_README|Finance for Founders]].

| Statement                                 | What it answers ([Finance Phase 3](../../docs/learning/finance/03-phase-3-three-statements.md)) | What the accountant checks                                                                                                       | What the **CEO** looks for                                                                       |
| ----------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **P&L** (period, a movie)                 | Are we making or losing money?                                                                  | Are revenue and expenses in the right period and the right head?                                                                 | **Gross margin trend**, and whether the expense mix matches what the strategy said we'd spend on |
| **Balance sheet** (moment, a photo)       | What do we own and owe right now?                                                               | Does it balance; are provisions right                                                                                            | **Cash, receivables ageing, statutory dues, director loans** — in that order                     |
| **Cash-flow statement** (period, a movie) | Where did the cash actually go?                                                                 | Are the three activity buckets classified per AS-3 ([ClearTax — cash flow statement](https://cleartax.in/s/cash-flow-statement)) | **Why profit and cash disagree**, and whether the gap is growing                                 |

### 5.1 The P&L: two questions, not twenty

**Question one: which way is gross margin moving?** Not the level — the _direction_, over the last six months. Gross margin is revenue minus the cost to deliver, and it is the number that decides whether this is a software business or a services business wearing software's clothes. a16z's framing is the one to hold: gross margin is the equaliser across business models, and it is what tells you how much money is left to cover operating expenses ([a16z — 16 More Startup Metrics](https://a16z.com/16-more-startup-metrics/)). The 2025 benchmark median for B2B SaaS total revenue is **77%**, and **81%** on subscription revenue alone ([Benchmarkit — 2025 B2B SaaS Performance Metrics](https://www.benchmarkit.ai/2025benchmarks)) — **VERIFY LIVE**, and note that median is drawn from a much larger, mostly US sample than VSYST resembles.

At VSYST the cost-to-deliver line is not hosting. It is **SMS/OTP and human support minutes** — the `Unit Economics` tab puts OTP at ₹210 and support at ₹400 per tenant per month against ARPA of ₹2,339, and together they are **54% of a ₹1,125 cost to serve** — more than half. Those two lines scale with the _dealer's_ transaction volume and with your patience, not with your headcount. So the CEO's P&L question becomes concrete: **is the SMS bill per tenant falling as tenants grow, and are support minutes per tenant falling as the product improves?** If both are flat, the business does not yet scale, and no amount of adding customers fixes it. This is not a hypothetical for VSYST: on those illustrative figures gross margin is **₹1,214 on ₹2,339, or 51.9%** — below the 60% floor, not near it, and a long way under the benchmark medians above. The rule is therefore live, not precautionary: **below 60%, attack SMS cost and support minutes before you attack price.** C12's pass two does exactly that — rate confirmations moved from SMS to free push, support minutes halved — and lifts the margin to 65.6% without moving the ₹1,799 list price by a rupee ([[C12-unit-economics-and-business-model-calculator|C12]]).

**Question two: did the money go where the strategy said it would?** Open [[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager|C04]] and the year's plan next to the P&L's expense heads. If the plan says "district proof, dealer-to-dealer referral, no pan-India push," then travel and field time should be visible in the expense mix and marketing spend should be near zero. If the biggest non-salary line is a tool subscription nobody uses, the P&L has just told you the strategy is on paper and the calendar is somewhere else. This is a five-minute check and it is the highest-value five minutes in the monthly close.

### 5.2 The balance sheet: four lines, in this order

The balance sheet is a photograph, and a CEO looks at four things in it, always in the same order.

1. **Cash.** Reconcile it to row 1 of the dashboard. If they differ, one of them is wrong and you need to know which _this month_, not at audit.
2. **Receivables, aged.** Not the total — the **ageing bucket**. ₹2,00,000 receivable is a fact; ₹2,00,000 of which ₹1,40,000 is over 90 days is a story about a customer who is not going to pay. Days Sales Outstanding is the compressed version of the same thing and the COO's scorecard carries it once invoicing is live; the CEO reads the buckets, because at N=6 an average DSO is meaningless and one name is everything. When you have exactly one large debtor, **the receivable is not a number, it is a relationship** — go and have the conversation yourself.
3. **Statutory dues.** GST payable, TDS payable, PF/ESI if applicable, advance tax. These are the balance-sheet lines that turn into personal exposure for directors, and they are the ones a founder most often lets slide when cash is tight because there is no angry human on the other end — until there is. Dashboard row 20 is the leading indicator; this is the lagging one. The compliance calendar itself is the COO's ([[T16-compliance-calendar|T16]], [[13-compliance-calendar-risk-and-insurance|COO 13]]); the _balance_ is the CEO's, because [[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties|lesson 09]] and section 166 of the Companies Act put the duty on the director personally ([Companies Act 2013 — s.166, duties of directors](https://ca2013.com/166-duties-of-directors/); [full Act text](https://www.indiacode.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/2114/5/A2013-18.pdf)).
4. **Director loans / unsecured loans from directors.** At a bootstrapped family-founded Pvt Ltd this line is how the company is actually financed, and it is the line that becomes contentious later — at a fundraise, at a valuation, or in a disagreement between founders. Know the balance per director, know whether interest is being charged, and make sure the paperwork exists. **VERIFY LIVE** with the CA and CS: the treatment of director loans, deposit rules and disclosure requirements are exactly the kind of thing that changes and that nobody tells you about.

### 5.3 The cash-flow statement: why profit and cash disagree

Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact. The cash-flow statement is the reconciliation between them, split into operating, investing and financing activities under AS-3 ([ClearTax — cash flow statement](https://cleartax.in/s/cash-flow-statement)); [[finance/03-phase-3-three-statements|Finance Phase 3]] §4–§5 shows the mechanics and the interlock.

The CEO reads it for one thing: **the gap, and whether the gap is growing.** Four causes account for nearly all of it at VSYST's stage, and each one means something different:

| The gap                                                    | What it means                                                         | What to do                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| ---------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Profit > cash because **receivables grew**                 | You sold to people who haven't paid. Growth is being financed by you. | Collections is a CEO problem at N=6. Call them.                                                                                                                                                         |
| Profit > cash because you **bought something capitalised** | A laptop or a server is an asset on the BS and cash out of the door.  | Nothing — but know it, so you don't celebrate a profit you can't spend.                                                                                                                                 |
| Cash > profit because of an **annual prepayment**          | A dealer paid 12 months up front. Cash today, revenue over a year.    | Celebrate — and _do not_ spend it as if it were earned. Deferred revenue is a liability ([[finance/07-phase-7-bootstrapping-runway-burn\|Phase 7]] §5 calls annual prepayment DZZLO's cash superpower). |
| Cash > profit because you **stopped paying something**     | Statutory dues or a vendor are being stretched.                       | This is a red flag about your own behaviour. Fix it this month.                                                                                                                                         |

The last row is the one to watch for in yourself. A month where cash looks fine because GST wasn't paid is not a good month, and the cash-flow statement is the only document that will tell you so plainly.

### 5.4 The five questions to ask your CA, every month

VSYST has no CFO and will not have one for years; [[finance/10-phase-10-pre-revenue-to-profitable|Finance Phase 10]] is explicit that the founder holds the CFO seat at this stage. That makes the monthly CA conversation a management instrument, not an admin task. Ask these five, in writing, every month, and keep the answers:

1. **"Is anything in this month's numbers an estimate rather than a fact — and which line?"** (Accruals, provisions and unbilled expenses are where surprises live. Ask before you rely on a figure.)
2. **"What did we owe on the last day of the month that isn't in these statements yet?"** (Unbilled vendor work, unclaimed reimbursements, a bonus you promised verbally. This is the question that turns an optimistic burn number into an honest one.)
3. **"Which statutory payment is closest to being late, and what is the penalty if it is?"** (Turns row 20 from a count into a specific, dated risk.)
4. **"If I had to show these books to a buyer or an investor tomorrow, what would embarrass me?"** (The best diligence question in existence, asked twelve months before diligence. Cheap now, expensive later — [[C14-fundraise-readiness-and-data-room-index|C14]].)
5. **"What changed in the law this month that affects us?"** (GST rules, TDS/TCS rates, MSME payment timelines, Companies Act thresholds. Your CA reads these; you do not. Make it their standing job to tell you, and log the answer.)

Two rules about the answers. **Get them in writing** — email or WhatsApp, then filed — because an oral answer cannot be shown to anyone later and cannot be checked against what was said three months ago. And **the CA is a source, not a decision-maker**: they can tell you that a line is an estimate; only you can decide whether an estimate is good enough to bet on. That distinction is the whole of [[02-how-a-ceo-thinks|lesson 02]] applied to accounting.

## 6. SaaS metrics, honestly

Everything in this section is borrowed vocabulary. MRR, churn, NRR, activation, CAC, LTV — these words were built by and for companies with thousands of customers and a decade of history, and they arrive at VSYST looking authoritative. They are useful. They are also the easiest place in this entire lesson to fool yourself, and the damage is delayed: you will not find out that your MRR definition was generous until an investor's analyst rebuilds it from your invoices in a diligence data room, or until you and your co-founder discover in year three that you have been arguing about two different numbers for eighteen months.

So this section does two jobs at once. It teaches the metrics with their traps attached, and it **writes down the definition VSYST will use, in binding form**, so that the argument happens now, cheaply, between three people who agree, rather than later, expensively, in front of someone who does not.

> **The rule that makes the rest of this section work: a metric definition at VSYST changes only through a dated entry in the `Decision Journal` tab ([[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]]), and when it changes, the history is restated or the change is marked on the dashboard row for twelve months.** A silently amended definition is indistinguishable from a lie, even when it was an honest improvement.

### 6.1 MRR and ARR, and the six ways they get inflated

**MRR** is the sum of the monthly-normalised recurring subscription value of every active paid subscription on the last day of the month. **ARR is MRR × 12.** It is never the other way round: you do not take a good month's bookings, multiply by twelve, and call the result ARR. a16z names the mistake precisely — _"often, people will multiply one month's all-in bookings by 12 to get to ARR. Common mistakes with this method include: (1) counting non-recurring fees such as hardware, setup, installation, professional services/consulting agreements; (2) counting bookings"_ ([a16z — 16 Startup Metrics](https://a16z.com/16-startup-metrics/), 2015).

Every inflation below is something an honest founder does by accident, in a spreadsheet, at 11 PM, while feeling good about a month.

| #   | The inflation                                                 | How it would show up at VSYST specifically                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              | The guard                                                                                                                                                   |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | **Annualising a good month**                                  | March had two signings and a renewal; ×12 becomes "₹8.4 lakh ARR". April has none.                                                                                                                                                                                                                      | ARR = MRR × 12, computed from the _closing_ subscription base, never from a month's bookings.                                                               |
| 2   | **Counting bookings, not billings**                           | A dealer signs in the association meeting and pays in six weeks. The signature is not revenue.                                                                                                                                                                                                          | The North Star counts **invoiced and collected** (§2.2). Signed-not-paid lives in the `Pipeline` tab as weighted pipeline, nowhere else.                    |
| 3   | **Counting one-offs as recurring**                            | Onboarding help, data migration from the dealer's old register, a paid training day, a customisation. Real money — **not** MRR.                                                                                                                                                                         | One-off revenue is a separate line in the P&L and is excluded from MRR by definition. It may be in revenue; it may never be in ARR.                         |
| 4   | **Counting GST inside the number**                            | A ₹2,760 invoice at 18% GST is ₹2,339 of revenue and ₹421 you are collecting on the government's behalf. Quoting the gross figure inflates MRR by 18% instantly.                                                                                                                                        | **MRR is GST-exclusive, always.** Write it in the definition; it is the single most common Indian-SaaS overstatement.                                       |
| 5   | **Counting list price instead of the price actually charged** | The first six dealers get a district-proof discount, or a free quarter. Booking them at list makes the MRR line a fiction and the churn line unreadable when the discount ends.                                                                                                                         | MRR uses the **net contracted price after discount**, with the discount's end date recorded. A discount that ends is a future churn event; treat it as one. |
| 6   | **Counting the free riders**                                  | The dealer's transport fleets, factories and hospitals ride free on the dealer's tenant by design ([[app-store-economics/08-dzzlo-subscription-strategy\|the subscription decision]]). Presenting "340 businesses on DZZLO" alongside a revenue number invites the listener to divide one by the other. | State the two numbers in different sentences with different labels: **paying GSTINs** and **businesses served**. Never in the same table column.            |

One more that is specific to VSYST's chosen pricing shape. **Annual prepayment is a cash event, not a revenue event.** A dealer who pays twelve months up front adds ₹2,339 to MRR and ₹23,378 to the bank (₹28,065 of annual list, less the 16.7% prepay discount); recognising the whole ₹23,378 in that month's revenue overstates the month by 10× and understates the next eleven. [[finance/07-phase-7-bootstrapping-runway-burn|Finance Phase 7]] §5 calls annual prepayment DZZLO's cash superpower, and it is — but the unearned portion sits on the balance sheet as deferred revenue, which is a **liability**, and §5.3 of this lesson tells you why the cash-flow statement is where you will see it.

### 6.2 Three churns, and why they disagree

There are three distinct questions hiding under the word churn, and a company that uses one word for all three will eventually report the flattering one by accident.

| Metric                                 | The question it answers                             | Formula (VSYST's binding form)                                                                                                                                         | What it misses                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| -------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Logo churn** (customer / unit churn) | How many _firms_ left?                              | Paying firms lost in the month ÷ paying firms at month start ([a16z](https://a16z.com/16-startup-metrics/): _"monthly unit churn = lost customers/prior month total"_) | Size. Losing your largest dealer and your smallest count the same.                                                                                                                             |
| **Gross revenue retention (GRR)**      | How much _money_ left, ignoring any good news?      | (MRR at start − MRR lost to churn and downgrade) ÷ MRR at start. Capped at 100% by construction.                                                                       | Nothing — this is the honest floor. It is the number that cannot be flattered.                                                                                                                 |
| **Net revenue retention (NRR)**        | How did the _existing base_ move, net of expansion? | (MRR at start − churn − downgrade + expansion) ÷ MRR at start. Can exceed 100%.                                                                                        | Everything, if one account expands while many leave. NRR above 100% with logo churn above 20% is a real and common combination, and it means "we are losing the market and keeping one whale." |

Benchmarks, so you know what the words are worth in the wider world. The 2025 B2B SaaS median GRR is **88%** and NRR is **101%** ([Benchmarkit — 2025 B2B SaaS Performance Metrics](https://www.benchmarkit.ai/2025benchmarks)); for **bootstrapped** private B2B SaaS companies at \$3–20M ARR the 2026 medians are NRR **103%**, GRR **91%**, and revenue growth **15%** annually, with the 90th percentile at NRR 117.9% and GRR 100% ([SaaS Capital — 2026 benchmarking metrics for bootstrapped SaaS companies](https://www.saas-capital.com/blog-posts/benchmarking-metrics-for-bootstrapped-saas-companies/)); across all ACV bands the 2025 median NRR sits near 102%, with a top quartile of 111% and a bottom quartile of 97% at the \$25–50k ACV band ([SaaS Capital — what is a good retention rate](https://www.saas-capital.com/blog-posts/what-is-a-good-retention-rate-for-a-private-saas-company/)). All **VERIFY LIVE** — and all drawn from US samples of companies fifty to a thousand times VSYST's size, at ACVs in dollars that VSYST will not see in rupees for years. Use them to know which direction is good, never as a target ([[16-metrics-dashboards-and-scorecards|COO 16]] §9's rule: no targets imported from benchmark blogposts).

**Why NRR matters more at VSYST than it would at most seed-stage companies**, and this is a direct consequence of the pricing decision: dealer companies pay **per GSTIN**, users are unlimited and free. That means expansion revenue is a natural, frequent, _earned_ event — a dealer who adds a second retail outlet or a second registered entity adds a paying GSTIN without any renegotiation, any price rise, or any upsell conversation. A vertical SaaS company that lands inside a dealer's operating loop and grows with the dealer's own footprint is the mechanism Tidemark describes when it says vertical software compounds by becoming the system the business runs on ([Tidemark — Vertical SaaS truisms](https://www.tidemarkcap.com/vskp-chapter/vertical-saas-truisms)). So NRR is the number that will eventually prove or disprove the per-GSTIN bet.

And the honest caveat, which belongs in the same breath: **at VSYST's N, NRR is a sentence about one dealer.** With six paying firms, one dealer adding a GSTIN moves NRR by fifteen points. Report it as a fraction and a name (§7.5), not as a percentage with a decimal point.

### 6.3 Cohort retention: the only honest picture of whether the product works

Aggregate churn is a blend, and a blend of a healthy old cohort with a sick new one can look fine for a year. **Cohort retention** removes the blend: group customers by the month they _first paid_, and follow each group forward.

```
                Still paying at month …
Cohort   N    M1    M3    M6    M9   M12
────────────────────────────────────────
Sep-26   2    2/2   2/2   2/2   1/2   1/2
Oct-26   1    1/1   1/1   1/1   1/1    ·
Nov-26   3    3/3   2/3   2/3    ·     ·
Dec-26   2    2/2   2/2    ·     ·     ·
Jan-27   4    4/4    ·     ·     ·     ·
```

Read it two ways. **Down a column** tells you whether newer cohorts are better than older ones — whether onboarding, the product, or the kind of dealer you are selling to has improved. **Across a row** tells you the shape of the curve, and the shape is the whole game: a retention curve that keeps falling means the product is a leaky bucket and every rupee of acquisition drains out; a curve that **flattens** means there is a group of customers for whom this product is now part of how they work, and that flat asymptote is the closest thing to a measurable definition of product-market fit ([Andrew Chen — Retention is King](https://andrewchen.com/retention-is-king/); [Reforge — the silent killer of growth](https://www.reforge.com/blog/retention-engagement-growth-silent-killer)).

Three rules for running cohorts at VSYST's size:

1. **Cohort by first payment, not by signup.** Signup is free and noisy. First payment is the event whose repeat you actually care about.
2. **Print counts, never percentages, while a cohort is under twenty.** `2/3` is honest. "67% retention" is a claim your data cannot support (§7.5).
3. **Run a second cohort table keyed on activation month.** DZZLO's activation event — first invoice raised — is closer to the causal moment than first payment is, and comparing the two tables tells you whether your churn is a _selling_ problem (paid, never activated) or a _product_ problem (activated, still left).

The COO course already runs a weekly cohort retention curve as part of the research rhythm ([[16-metrics-dashboards-and-scorecards|COO 16]] §9, drawing on the [[company/README|Company Playbook]]'s cadence). **The CEO does not build a second one.** The CEO reads that one, monthly, and asks a single question of it: _did the newest cohort do better than the previous one, and if not, what did we change that we thought would help?_

### 6.4 Activation is the leading indicator of churn

Churn is a lagging output with a long lag. By the time a dealer declines to renew, the decision was made months earlier and you cannot argue with it. **Activation is where that decision was actually made**, and it happens in the first fortnight.

The definition is the COO's and the CEO does not touch it: **≥1 customer linked and ≥1 invoice raised within 14 days of signup** ([[10-customer-operations-support-and-success|COO 10]] §7). What the CEO adds is the reading.

|                                   | What it says                                                    | What the CEO does about it                                                                    |
| --------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Activated, still paying at M6** | The product is load-bearing for this dealer.                    | Ask them for a referral and a quote. This is your case study and your district proof (row 9). |
| **Activated, churned anyway**     | A **product or trust** failure. They used it and chose to stop. | This is the expensive one. Do the churn post-mortem personally, on a call, within a week.     |
| **Never activated, still paying** | You are being paid out of politeness or inertia. It will end.   | Treat as churned. Go and onboard them yourself.                                               |
| **Never activated, churned**      | An **onboarding** failure, not a product failure.               | Fix the SOP, not the roadmap. Days-to-first-invoice is the row that moves.                    |

The benchmark literature says the median activation rate across products is about **25%**, roughly **30%** for SaaS alone, and around **50%** for B2B enterprise SaaS, with the working definition _"activation rate = [users who hit your activation milestone] / [users who completed your signup flow]"_ and the design rule that activated users should retain **at least 2× better** than non-activated ones ([Lenny's Newsletter — what is a good activation rate](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/what-is-a-good-activation-rate)) — **VERIFY LIVE**, and note that this sample is overwhelmingly self-serve products where nobody helps the user.

**VSYST is not that.** DZZLO is sales-assisted and district-by-district; a founder or the domain director is physically present for the first invoice. So VSYST's activation rate should be **far above 50%, and closer to 100%**, because a human is doing the activating. Which produces the useful inversion: _if your human-assisted activation rate is 60%, the problem is not the benchmark, it is that your onboarding SOP is not being followed_ — and that is a COO fix with a named mechanism, not a product debate.

The row the CEO actually watches is not the rate. It is **median days to first invoice**, plus the raw count of signups past 14 days without one. Both work when N is four (§7.4), both point at a named dealer you can call today, and neither requires a denominator you do not have.

### 6.5 CAC payback beats LTV/CAC, and below ~30 customers the ratio is noise

**CAC** at VSYST is not an advertising number, because VSYST does not advertise. It is **founder time**, and the `Unit Economics` tab computes it that way: founder days spent selling × a loaded day rate, plus travel and demo costs, divided by paid conversions in the period ([[C12-unit-economics-and-business-model-calculator|C12]]). Distinguish **blended CAC** (all acquisition cost ÷ all new customers) from **paid CAC** (paid-channel cost ÷ customers from paid channels) and report blended, since at VSYST paid CAC is zero and would flatter enormously ([a16z — 16 Startup Metrics](https://a16z.com/16-startup-metrics/)).

Two ratios are built on top of CAC, and they are not equally trustworthy.

|                          | **CAC payback (months)**                                                                                               | **LTV / CAC (a ratio)**                                                                                    |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Formula**              | CAC ÷ monthly gross profit per customer                                                                                | (ARPA × gross margin ÷ monthly churn rate) ÷ CAC                                                           |
| **Inputs**               | Two measured quantities: money spent, money collected                                                                  | One measured quantity and **an estimate of churn**, which is an estimate of the future                     |
| **Conventional bar**     | Recover CAC in **under 12 months** ([David Skok — SaaS Metrics 2.0](https://www.forentrepreneurs.com/saas-metrics-2/)) | **LTV > 3 × CAC** ([same](https://www.forentrepreneurs.com/saas-metrics-2/))                               |
| **Behaviour at small N** | Degrades gracefully. With three customers it is still a real number with a real error bar.                             | Falls apart. Churn is in the **denominator**, so a small error in churn produces an enormous error in LTV. |
| **Fit at VSYST today**   | **Report this one.** It is the honest one.                                                                             | **Do not report until 30 paying GSTINs and 12 months of churn history.**                                   |

The argument against LTV at this stage is not squeamishness; it is arithmetic. Bill Gurley's essay is the canonical warning — the LTV formula smuggles in assumptions about churn, discount rate and cohort representativeness, and _"the formula is deceptively simple"_ in a way that makes wildly speculative numbers look like accounting ([Above the Crowd — The Dangerous Seduction of the Lifetime Value (LTV) Formula](https://abovethecrowd.com/2012/09/04/the-dangerous-seduction-of-the-lifetime-value-ltv-formula/), 2012). a16z makes the narrower version of the same point: LTV must be the present value of future **net profit**, and _"a common mistake is to estimate the LTV as a present value of revenue or even gross margin of the customer instead of calculating it as net profit"_ ([a16z](https://a16z.com/16-startup-metrics/)).

Now put VSYST's numbers into it. With ten paying GSTINs and one churn in a month, measured monthly churn is 10% — but the 95% confidence interval on that proportion runs from **1.8% to 40.4%** (§7.3). LTV = ARPA × gross margin ÷ churn, so that interval maps to a lifetime of somewhere between **2.5 months and 55 months** — a 22× range. Any LTV/CAC ratio you compute inside that range is not a measurement; it is a mood. The number becomes usable when the interval narrows, and the interval narrows with N and with elapsed time, which is why the house rule names both.

**The house call for VSYST: report CAC payback in months on the `Unit Economics` tab, quarterly, and leave the LTV cell blank with the note "insufficient churn history — see lesson 17 §6.5" until 30 paying GSTINs and 12 months.** A blank cell with a reason is a more credible artefact in a data room than a filled cell with a fantasy, and any investor worth taking money from will read it that way ([[C14-fundraise-readiness-and-data-room-index|C14]]).

## 7. Metrics for a company with almost no data

VSYST today has zero paying GSTINs, a handful of pilot tenants, three people and about a year of product history. Section 6 just handed you an apparatus built for companies with a thousand customers. This section is the correction: **what you actually measure when N is six, and how to do it without either lying or giving up.**

Two failure modes bracket the honest path. The first is **not measuring** — "we're too early for metrics" — which is how a company arrives at month eighteen with no idea which of its beliefs were wrong. The second is **measuring as though you were big** — reporting a 33% conversion rate that is two out of six, computing an LTV, drawing a funnel chart with three people in it. The second failure is worse, because it produces confident wrong decisions instead of merely uninformed ones. Eric Ries's _innovation accounting_ is the standing answer: at this stage the thing you are measuring is not the business, it is **whether your learning is progressing** ([The Lean Startup — principles](https://theleanstartup.com/principles)), and Steve Blank's first principle is the same claim in a harder form — a startup is a temporary organisation **searching** for a repeatable, scalable business model, so the metrics of a search are not the metrics of an operation ([Steve Blank — What's a startup? First principles](https://steveblank.com/2010/01/25/whats-a-startup-first-principles/), 2010).

### 7.1 Count things; do not rate things

Every rate is a fraction, and a fraction with a small denominator is mostly denominator noise. Counts do not have that problem: a count is a fact, it is checkable, and it is attached to a name you can call.

| The rate you are tempted to report | The count to report instead                                                | Why the count is better below N=20                                               |
| ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Demo-to-pilot conversion rate      | **Demos delivered** and **pilots started**, both as counts                 | Two numbers you can each verify against the calendar; no unstable quotient       |
| Trial-to-paid conversion rate      | **Trials running** and **paid conversions**, as counts, with names         | At six trials, "50%" and "33%" are one dealer apart                              |
| Activation rate                    | **Tenants activated** and **tenants past 14 days without a first invoice** | The second count is a _to-do list_, which a rate never is                        |
| Churn rate                         | **Firms lost this month**, listed by name                                  | At N=6 the name is the entire content of the metric                              |
| Support-ticket rate per tenant     | **Tickets from the top three tenants**, counted                            | An average across six tenants tells you nothing; three names tell you everything |
| Feature adoption %                 | **Tenants who have used it at least once**, counted                        | You can call the ones who haven't                                                |

The corollary rule, which is easy to state and easy to forget: **every count on a pre-revenue dashboard should be clickable down to a list of names.** If you cannot produce the six names behind the number six, the number is not instrumented (§10).

### 7.2 Coding customer conversations, so qualitative data becomes countable

The richest data VSYST has is not in Mongo. It is in the founder's memory of forty conversations with dealers, and memory is a lossy, self-flattering store. **Qualitative coding** is the cheap discipline that turns those conversations into something you can count without pretending it is quantitative.

The method, at the smallest useful size:

1. **Write the conversation up within an hour**, in the dealer's words, not yours. Verbatim phrases only — _"I keep the rate in a diary and my son copies it into WhatsApp"_ — because a paraphrase has already had your hypothesis baked into it. This is the whole discipline of [_The Mom Test_](https://momtestbook.com/): ask about their life, not your idea, and record what they did rather than what they would do.
2. **Tag each write-up with 1–4 short codes** from a running list you maintain in the vault — `rate-confirmation-pain`, `ledger-dispute-with-customer`, `TCS-confusion`, `son-does-the-computer`, `price-objection-free-alternative`, `wants-driver-tracking`. Add a code when you need one; never merge two codes to make a number bigger.
3. **Count the codes monthly** and put the top five in the monthly review, with the raw count and the total number of conversations: _"`ledger-dispute-with-customer` — 11 of 17 conversations."_
4. **Promote a code to an assumption** when it appears in the majority of conversations. It goes into the register in the `Strategy Kernel` tab with a confidence score and a test date, and it becomes a candidate for dashboard row 12.

This is a stripped-down thematic analysis, and the full version — familiarisation, coding, generating themes, reviewing, naming — is worth reading once so you know what you are simplifying ([Braun & Clarke — thematic analysis](https://www.thematicanalysis.net/)). The reassuring finding from usability research is that small samples find most of what there is to find when the thing you are looking for is common: five users surface roughly 85% of the problems in an interface, and further users mostly repeat ([Nielsen Norman Group — Why You Only Need to Test with 5 Users](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/why-you-only-need-to-test-with-5-users/)). The same logic holds for discovery interviews with a homogeneous buyer — and the caveat holds too: **it only works if the five are the same kind of dealer.** Five petrol-pump dealers and five bulk-diesel operators are two samples of five, not one of ten.

Two quantitative instruments are cheap enough to run at this N and worth the effort. The **Sean Ellis product-market-fit question** — _how would you feel if you could no longer use this product?_ — with the 40% "very disappointed" threshold as the rough line ([Sean Ellis — The Startup Pyramid](https://www.startup-marketing.com/the-startup-pyramid/)), operationalised into a repeatable engine by Rahul Vohra at Superhuman: segment the "very disappointed" group, ignore the rest, and build only for the people who already love it ([First Round Review — How Superhuman Built an Engine to Find Product/Market Fit](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit/)). At N=15 that survey gives you a fraction, not a percentage — **6 of 15** — and it is still the most informative fifteen minutes in the quarter. The COO course already runs it weekly as part of the research rhythm; the CEO reads the segmented answer monthly.

### 7.3 The confidence interval you can compute in your head

You do not need statistics to run VSYST. You need one table, once, to inoculate yourself against your own rates. Here is what a 95% confidence interval actually looks like on a proportion at the sample sizes you have ([Wikipedia — binomial proportion confidence interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binomial_proportion_confidence_interval), Wilson score method):

| Observed          | The percentage you would have written | What the data actually supports (95%) | Width     |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | --------- |
| 1 churn in 6      | 16.7%                                 | **3.0% – 56.4%**                      | 53 points |
| 1 churn in 10     | 10.0%                                 | **1.8% – 40.4%**                      | 39 points |
| 3 of 12 converted | 25.0%                                 | **8.9% – 53.2%**                      | 44 points |
| 1 churn in 20     | 5.0%                                  | **0.9% – 23.6%**                      | 23 points |
| 5 of 50           | 10.0%                                 | **4.3% – 21.4%**                      | 17 points |
| 50 of 500         | 10.0%                                 | **7.7% – 12.9%**                      | 5 points  |

Read the last two rows together: **the same 10% is a useless number at N=50 and a decision-grade number at N=500.** Nothing about the arithmetic changed; only the evidence did.

And the one result worth memorising for a company with no bad events yet — the **rule of three**: if you have observed zero failures in _n_ trials, the upper bound of the 95% interval is approximately **3/n** ([Wikipedia — rule of three (statistics)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_three_%28statistics%29)). Zero churn in six paying dealers is consistent with a true churn rate as high as **50%**. Zero money-affecting incidents in twelve months of releases is consistent with a true rate of one per four years. This is not pessimism; it is the correct size of the claim, and it is why dashboard row 21's red line is 1 and not 3 — you cannot yet distinguish "we are safe" from "we have been lucky."

### 7.4 Time-based measures work at any N

The class of metric that survives a small N intact is **elapsed time**, because each observation is a complete measurement rather than one-sixth of a fraction. One dealer produces one honest number.

| Measure                                              | Definition                                                | What it diagnoses                                                               | Where it lives                                                            |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Days to first invoice**                            | Tenant creation → first non-test invoice                  | Onboarding friction; the leading indicator of churn (§6.4)                      | Mongo; COO scorecard weekly, CEO reads the median monthly                 |
| **Days to first rupee**                              | First conversation with a dealer → first payment received | The true length of your sales cycle. Nobody's forecast survives measuring this. | `Pipeline` tab                                                            |
| **Days from demo to decision**                       | Demo delivered → yes/no/silence-declared-a-no             | Whether you are selling or being politely stored                                | `Pipeline` tab                                                            |
| **Days since the CEO last spoke to a paying dealer** | Self-explanatory; dashboard row 22                        | Founder drift (§4.1)                                                            | Calendar                                                                  |
| **Days since the last money-affecting incident**     | Dashboard row 21, inverted                                | Reliability, expressed as a streak you do not want to reset                     | Incident log                                                              |
| **Cycle time: bug reported → fixed in production**   | For money-affecting bugs only                             | Whether the trust promise is operationally real                                 | COO's engineering ops ([[12-product-and-engineering-operations\|COO 12]]) |

Two properties make these the right family for a pre-revenue company. They are **fully observable from day one** — the very first dealer produces a real value. And they are **actionable without a comparison group**: forty-one days to first invoice is bad on its own terms, whatever anyone else's number is. Notice also that a streak — "days since" — is the one cumulative number that is not a vanity metric, because it **resets to zero on bad news** (§8.1).

### 7.5 The fraction rule

This is a house rule, it is binding, and it is the cheapest honesty mechanism in the course:

> **A percentage computed on a denominator under 20 is written as a fraction, with the denominator visible, in every document VSYST produces — dashboard, board pack, investor update, deck, WhatsApp message.** `2/6`, not "33%". If the format demands a percentage, write `2/6 (33%)`, never "33%" alone.

Three reasons, in increasing order of importance. It is **more honest**, because the reader sees the evidence and not just the ratio. It **stops the number from travelling**: "33% of dealers churn" gets quoted back at you in a board meeting a year later, detached from its denominator, while "2 of 6" cannot be quoted without carrying its own caveat. And it **changes what you do next**: a fraction has a numerator you can name, so the natural next question becomes _"which two?"_ — which is the question that actually leads somewhere.

Two companion rules. **Never put a decimal place on a percentage whose denominator is under 100** — "16.7%" from six observations implies a precision of one part in a thousand from data that supports one part in six. And **never compute a rate-of-change on a rate whose denominator is under 20**: "churn improved 40% quarter on quarter" from 2/6 to 1/6 is a sentence with no information in it.

### 7.6 The assumption register is the pre-revenue company's real scoreboard

When there is no revenue to measure, the honest measure of a month's work is **how much less wrong you are than you were.** [[05-strategy-i-diagnosis-and-the-strategy-kernel|Lesson 05]] builds the assumption register in the `Strategy Kernel` tab: each load-bearing belief written as a falsifiable statement, with a confidence score, the test that would move it, an owner and a date. Dashboard row 12 — _assumptions tested this quarter_ — is that register's output, and it is the closest thing VSYST has today to a productivity metric for the CEO's own job.

| Assumption (illustrative)                               | Confidence before | The test                                                                                                          | Result | Confidence after |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------ | ---------------- |
| Dealers will pay per GSTIN rather than per user         | 70%               | Price the next 8 conversations per-GSTIN; count objections that are _specifically_ about the unit, not the amount | ?      | ?                |
| District proof creates dealer-to-dealer referral        | 55%               | Land 3 dealers in one district; count inbound referrals in the following 90 days                                  | ?      | ?                |
| The ledger, not the ordering, is what they will pay for | 60%               | Offer a rates-only tier at half price; count how many take it                                                     | ?      | ?                |
| The son, not the dealer, is the real user               | 80%               | Log who actually operates the app in the first 30 days, per tenant                                                | ?      | ?                |

Three rules about this table. **A test needs a date, a method and a written result** — a conversation in which someone agreed with you is not a test. **A confidence score that never moves means the test was not a test.** And **two per quarter is the bar** because it is achievable while also selling and shipping; a quarter with zero tested assumptions is a quarter in which the company learned nothing it can point to, however busy it was.

## 8. How numbers lie

Numbers do not lie on purpose. They lie because a number is a compression of reality, and every compression throws something away. The CEO's job is to know **what each of your numbers threw away**, because that is where the surprise is stored.

Here are the six that will actually come for VSYST. Not a generic list — these are chosen because each one has a specific, predictable form at a three-person, per-GSTIN, fuel-distribution SaaS company selling district by district.

| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | **Vanity metrics** — numbers that flatter without informing: cumulative, unowned, unconnected to any branch of the tree                                                                                                                                       | "4,200 app downloads." "38 registered tenants." "₹9 crore of GMV flowed through DZZLO." All true, all rising, none of them a business. The customer side rides the dealer's tenant _by design_, so downloads measure the dealer's push, not your market                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         | The three-question test ([[16-metrics-dashboards-and-scorecards\|COO 16]] §8): can a named owner move it? does it change a decision? which branch does it feed? | 2   | **Survivorship** — you only measure the customers who are still there                                                                                                                                                                                         | Your NPS, your feature requests, your "what do dealers want" read, and your entire product roadmap come from the dealers who stayed. | 3   | **Averages over a bimodal reality** — one huge dealer and nine tiny ones                                                                                                                                                                                      | ARPA of ₹2,339 across ten GSTINs where one pays ₹12,950 and nine pay ₹1,160. | 4   | **Simpson's paradox** — a trend that holds in every subgroup reverses when the subgroups are pooled                                                                                                                                                           | Activation looks like it is falling company-wide while it is _rising_ in both dealer types, because you started selling to more bulk-diesel operators, who activate slower. | 6   | **Goodhart's law** — _"when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"_ ([Goodhart's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goodhart%27s_law); its enforcement-flavoured twin, [Campbell's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campbell%27s_law)) | Weighted pipeline is the obvious one — probabilities drift upward the week before the review. Founder conversations become five-minute phone calls that tick the box. Ticket first-response time gets "solved" by an auto-reply. The industrial-scale example is Wells Fargo, where a cross-sell target produced roughly 3.5 million unauthorised accounts ([Wells Fargo cross-selling scandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wells_Fargo_cross-selling_scandal))                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               | Three guards. **Pair every input metric with a quality metric** — conversations with _notes filed_; pipeline with _stage-defined_ probabilities that cannot move per-deal without a note ([[C10-founder-led-sales-pipeline-and-script\|C10]]). **Never attach money or an OKR score to a single row.** And apply the third test from §3.1 before adding any row: _is it hard to game without also doing the real work?_                                                                                                                                             |

Three closing rules that cut across all six.

**The person who fills a number should not be the only person who reads it.** At three people this is nearly impossible, which is exactly why the MBR (§9) exists and why the other two directors are in the room. One reader is a diary; two readers is an instrument.

**Write down what a number would look like if you were wrong.** Before the month starts, write the value that would falsify your current belief. If you cannot state it, the metric is decorative — and this is the same discipline as the pre-mortem in [[C30-pre-mortem-crisis-playbook-and-succession|C30]] and the "what would change my mind" field in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]], applied to a spreadsheet cell.

**The most dangerous number on any dashboard is the one nobody has questioned in six months.** Rotate the scepticism: at each quarterly review, pick two rows at random and re-derive them from the raw source. You will find an error roughly every other quarter, and finding it yourself is free.

## 9. The monthly business review

The dashboard is an instrument. The **monthly business review** is the meeting where somebody reads it and something changes as a result. Without the MBR the dashboard becomes a filled sheet nobody acts on, which is the most common way a metrics system dies — not from bad numbers, from unread ones ([[20-the-autopilot-test-and-scaling-the-machine|COO 20]] names the same anti-pattern for dashboards).

**Ownership, said plainly so the two courses do not collide.** The COO owns the **weekly** rhythm: the Monday ops meeting ([[T03-weekly-ops-meeting-agenda|T03]]) and the written Friday weekly business review ([[T28-weekly-business-review|T28]]). The **CEO owns the monthly business review**, and it is a different meeting with a different agenda, not a longer version of the weekly one. The weekly asks _is the machine healthy_; the monthly asks _is this working, and are we going to survive._

### 9.1 The run of show

Ninety minutes, once a month, on a fixed date — the second working day after the 5th, so the dashboard is already filled. Same order every month, because a fixed order means the data sets the agenda rather than whoever feels most strongly ([Commoncog — the Amazon WBR](https://commoncog.com/the-amazon-weekly-business-review/); [EOS — Level 10 Meeting](https://www.eosworldwide.com/level-10-meeting); [Scaling Up — the meeting rhythm](https://scalingup.com/)).

### 9.2 Who is in the room at three people

All three directors. Not "the leadership team" — the **directors**, because that is what the MBR is rehearsing. The technical CEO leads; the domain-expert director owns the customer and market segment; the third director asks the question nobody wants asked. Whoever holds the COO seat attends for the customer and red-row segments and brings the weekly scorecard as history, not as agenda.

Two seats to add later, and the trigger for each: an **independent voice** once there is one (see [[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties|lesson 09]] §8 on the independent voice a family board needs) — invite them to the MBR before you invite them to the board; and the **CA**, for the cash segment only, once monthly close is real rather than quarterly.

### 9.3 The four rules

1. **Numbers are filled before the meeting and never during it.** The sentence _"let me just pull that up"_ ends the meeting's usefulness — it converts ninety minutes of judgment into forty minutes of data entry with an audience. If a row is missing at 10:00, it is reported **blank and red**, and the fix is the meeting's first decision.
2. **No number enters the discussion that is not on the sheet.** An anecdote is an anecdote; if it is important enough to steer a decision, it becomes a row next quarter. This is the COO's WBR rule promoted to the CEO's meeting.
3. **Green rows get no airtime.** Zero. Management by exception is the whole design: attention is the scarcest input in a three-person company and it goes to variances ([[16-metrics-dashboards-and-scorecards|COO 16]] §7).
4. **Every red row leaves with a name and a date, or with an explicit decision to accept it.** "We'll keep an eye on it" is not an outcome. _"Accept until 15 October, owner: CEO"_ is.

### 9.4 What the MBR feeds

The MBR is not a terminal meeting; it is the middle of a chain, and this is why the discipline is worth it at three people.

| Downstream                                                                                                                       | What it takes from the MBR                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              | Cadence                            |

At three people, in a room where everyone already knows what happened, this will feel like theatre for the first two months. Run it anyway. The MBR is the mechanism that makes a board meeting boring — and a boring board meeting, where nothing in the pack is a surprise, is the single clearest sign that a company is being run rather than reacted to.

**The append-only event log is the one to act on this week**, and here is why it is disproportionate. Cohort retention, NRR, GRR and net new (§6.2, §6.3) are all computed from _transitions_ — started, upgraded, downgraded, churned — and a database that stores only current state cannot reconstruct them. If you store only `subscription.status = "active"`, then in eighteen months, when an investor asks for a cohort table, the honest answer is _we cannot produce one for the period before we started logging._ Adding the collection now costs a few hours and buys a metric you cannot otherwise buy at any price. **State is cheap to store and impossible to reconstruct; log the events before you need the metric.**

### 10.3 Three rules that keep the instrumentation honest

**One file, two authors.** The company keeps **one** metric-sources note — `coo-workbook/metric-sources.md`, the file [[16-metrics-dashboards-and-scorecards|COO 16]] §5 creates. The COO owns it and writes the scorecard rows; the CEO **appends** the rows only the dashboard carries and the binding definitions from §6.6. Two files means two truths, and the reconciliation tax gets paid at every review forever.

**Re-run every query once a quarter and paste the result with the date.** Queries rot: a schema changes, a field gets renamed, a `$match` silently excludes a tenant type you started selling to. The quarterly re-run is thirty minutes and it is how you find out before a board meeting rather than during one.

**Read-only credentials, always.** The person pulling a metric never has write access to production for that purpose — the COO's Atlas access is read-only by design ([[07-tools-and-it-foundation|COO 07]] §4), and the CEO's should be too, technical founder or not. A metric query is not a reason to hold a write credential, and the ISO 27001 work ahead ([[certification-and-standards-roadmap|Certification and Standards Roadmap]]) will ask you to prove exactly this.
