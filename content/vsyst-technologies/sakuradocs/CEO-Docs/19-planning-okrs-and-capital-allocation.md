# 19 — Planning, OKRs and Capital Allocation

_Phase 4 · Running the Company and Yourself · Months 6–24. After this lesson you can prove what VSYST's strategy actually is by auditing where last quarter's rupees and founder-hours went, and name the gap between that and the written plan; write a one-page annual plan for a company that cannot forecast — one bet, three or four objectives, an explicit no-list and the conditions under which the plan changes; trace an unbroken chain from the strategy kernel's diagnosis to every single key result and delete the objectives that fail the trace; argue the honest case against OKRs at three people and then run the stripped-down version that survives it; read a falling confidence line six weeks before a missed quarter announces itself; allocate the next 200 founder-hours and the next ₹1,50,000 with a written method rather than a mood; run a zero-based review of every recurring cost once a year; and execute a real persist-or-pivot decision with pre-committed evidence criteria, so that "six more months" has to be earned rather than assumed._

## Explain-it-like-I'm-5

A family in Raipur runs two businesses out of the same house. There is a kirana shop on the ground floor that the grandfather opened, and there are two second-hand trucks that the family bought four years ago to carry cement and rice to Bilaspur and Jagdalpur.

Every year after Diwali the family sits down and the father says the same sentence: **"This year we grow the transport side. The shop is fine, the shop runs itself. Transport is the future."** Everybody nods. Somebody writes it in the back of a notebook.

Now look at what actually happened last year. The surplus — about ₹3 lakh — went into a new deep freezer for the shop, a fresh coat of paint, and a second billing counter for the Diwali rush. Nothing went into the trucks. And look at the father's own days: he was at the shop counter every single morning from 7 to 12, because that is where he is comfortable and that is where the till is. He met one transport client all year, in November, for forty minutes.

So what was the family's real strategy last year? **It was "grow the shop."** Not because anyone decided that — nobody decided it, and if you asked, all four adults would tell you the strategy was transport. It was the strategy because that is where the money went and that is where the hours went. The notebook was a claim. The freezer and the diary were the evidence.

There is a second thing hiding in that story. The second truck has not made money in three years. Every single Diwali, the eldest son says: "The rates are bad right now. Give it one more season." He has now said that three times. That is not persistence — persistence is when you can say what would make you stop. That is a decision the family has never actually made, made again by default, three years running.

This lesson is those two things. **Where the money and the hours go is the strategy; the document is only a claim.** And: **a bet you cannot say the failing conditions for is not a bet, it is a habit.** Everything else here is method.

## 1. Resource allocation is the real strategy

[[05-strategy-i-diagnosis-and-the-strategy-kernel|Lesson 05]] taught you to write a strategy. This section is the uncomfortable follow-up: **the strategy you wrote is not necessarily the strategy you have.** The strategy you have is the one encoded in your bank statement and your calendar, and it is usually different — not because anyone lied, but because a written strategy is a decision made once while allocation is a thousand small decisions made continuously, by whoever is in the room, under whatever pressure was loudest that week.

### 1.1 The two documents that tell the truth

Two artefacts, both of which already exist at VSYST, contain the company's actual strategy. Neither of them is the strategy doc.

| Artefact                            | What it proves                                                 | Where it lives at VSYST                                                                                                                 | The question to ask it                                                                                                                                                                          |
| ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **The bank statement, categorised** | What the company was willing to pay for when it had a choice   | Bank + the `Budget vs Actual` categories the COO already maintains ([[T20-budget-vs-actual-and-cash-forecast\|T20]])                    | Take last quarter's discretionary spend — everything that was not a statutory or keep-the-lights-on line — and sort it descending. Does the top of that list match the top of the strategy doc? |
| **The three founders' calendars**   | What the company was willing to spend its only real capital on | Google Calendar, plus the honest reconstruction of what actually happened ([[15-the-ceo-operating-cadence-and-calendar\|lesson 15]] §1) | Take last month, bucket every hour into five or six categories, and read the top three. Those are the company's real priorities, whatever the plan says                                         |

This is not a metaphor. The best-documented study of how CEOs actually spend time tracked 27 chief executives of large companies 24/7 for 13 weeks and found they averaged around **62.5 hours a week, with roughly 72% of working time spent in meetings** ([Porter & Nohria, "How CEOs Manage Time", _HBR_, July–August 2018](https://hbr.org/2018/07/how-ceos-manage-time)). The important finding is not the number of hours. It is that a CEO's agenda is a scarce, contested, mostly-committed resource that gets consumed by whoever asks — and that unless it is allocated deliberately, it gets allocated anyway, by other people's requests.

The rule this course runs on:

> **Write the strategy on one page. Then audit the budget and the calendar against it once a quarter. Where they disagree, the budget and the calendar are right about what you are doing, and the page is right about what you intended. Close the gap on purpose, in one direction or the other — but never leave it unnamed.**

### 1.2 Allocation inertia — the research says this is normal, not a personal failing

The reason this needs a mechanism rather than good intentions is that resource allocation is one of the stickiest behaviours in management, and it is sticky at every size.

| Finding                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          | Source                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                    | What it means at VSYST                                                                                                                                                                          |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Companies allocate **90% or more of resources to the same activities year after year**, largely regardless of changes in the environment or in the stated strategy; the study covered 1,616 US-listed firms                                                                                                                      | [Hall, Lovallo & Musters, "How to put your money where your strategy is", _McKinsey Quarterly_, March 2012](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/how-to-put-your-money-where-your-strategy-is)                                                                                                                                                               | Last year's spending pattern is the single best predictor of this year's, and it will quietly beat your annual plan unless the plan explicitly moves money                                      |
| **The resource allocation process is where strategy is actually made** — a firm's real strategy is the aggregate of the investment decisions its people make, not the document senior management publishes. Bower's 1970 study is the origin of this line; it runs through Christensen's work on why good firms miss disruptions | [Bower & Gilbert, _From Resource Allocation to Strategy_, OUP, 2005](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/from-resource-allocation-to-strategy-9780199277452); [Christensen, "How Will You Measure Your Life?", _HBR_, July–August 2010](https://hbr.org/2010/07/how-will-you-measure-your-life)                                                                                                       | At three people this is _more_ true, not less. There is no bureaucracy between the intention and the spend, which means nothing catches the drift either                                        |

| Execution failures are usually blamed on poor alignment; the survey data says the real culprits are coordination and **the inability to move resources once they have been committed**. Read those four rows together and the conclusion is not "try harder". It is: **allocation drifts by default and only moves when something forces it.** The annual plan and the quarterly session are that something. That is their entire job.

### 1.3 What "resources" means when the company is three people

At VSYST there is no capex, no headcount plan worth the name, and no business units to shift capital between. There are exactly three resources, and only two of them are scarce enough to matter.

| Resource                    | The honest quantity (illustrative — recompute yours)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             | Who controls it                                                                                                                   | Reallocation cost                                                                                       |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Founder-hours**           | Three directors at roughly 55 / 45 / 25 usable hours a week ≈ **125 hours/week ≈ 1,625 hours a quarter**. Subtract the floor — support, releases, compliance, the close pack, the weekly cadence, travel time — call it 500 hours. **≈ 1,100 genuinely discretionary founder-hours per quarter** | Each founder, minute by minute, mostly unconsciously                                                                              | Near zero to reallocate, near total to _fail_ to reallocate. This is the company's real capital account |
| **Cash**                    | Bootstrapped and pre-revenue. At this stage the discretionary pot is a few lakh a year, not a few crore                                 | The board of three, via [[C13-runway-burn-and-scenario-planner\|C13]] and the [[08-capital-runway-and-fundraising\|runway rules]] | Reversible up to the point where it is spent. Then not                                                  |
| **Attention / credibility** | One founder can hold maybe **five** live external relationships properly — an OMC contact, a gateway, two CA firms, an association office-bearer ([[14-partnerships-ecosystem-and-founder-relationships\|lesson 14]])                                                                            | The CEO, almost entirely                                                                                                          | Very expensive to reallocate. Dropping a relationship you cultivated is visible and remembered          |

Two consequences fall straight out of that table and neither is obvious.

**Founder-hours are the binding budget, not rupees.** A three-person pre-revenue company almost always has more cash than hours in the sense that matters: you can survive a wasted ₹50,000, but a wasted 200 founder-hours in a 1,100-hour quarter is 18% of the year's capacity to change anything. **So the annual plan must be denominated in hours before it is denominated in rupees.** Most founders do the opposite because rupees are easier to count.

**Money that buys hours back is categorically different from money that buys anything else.** A ₹40,000 contractor month that removes 60 hours of screen-building from the CEO is not a cost line, it is a capital transaction — it converts the plentiful resource into the scarce one at a known exchange rate. §6.4 makes that rate explicit and uses it as a decision rule.

### 1.4 The allocation audit — a real 45-minute procedure

Run this at every quarterly session, before any planning. It is the CEO's half of the session; the COO's half is the scorecard and budget-vs-actual review ([[19-planning-okrs-and-the-quarterly-rhythm|COO lesson 19]] §5).

1. **Pull the quarter's spend and strip the floor.** Remove statutory, infrastructure and keep-alive lines. What remains is the discretionary pot — the money the company had a genuine choice about.
2. **Sort it descending and read the top five lines out loud.** Do not comment. Just read them.
3. **Reconstruct founder-hours into six buckets.** Building · selling and dealer contact · onboarding and support · partnerships · admin, compliance and internal · everything else. Reconstruct rather than track: three people going through their own calendar and WhatsApp for the last 13 weeks, half an hour each, produces numbers accurate enough to argue about. Precision is not the point; the _ranking_ is the point.
4. **Put the two rankings next to the annual plan's priorities in one three-column table.** Money rank · hours rank · plan rank.
5. **Write the gap statement.** One sentence, in the plan doc, in this exact form:

> _Last quarter we said the priority was **X**. **N%** of discretionary hours and **₹N** of discretionary spend went to **Y**. Our real strategy last quarter was **Y**._

6. **Then decide which one was wrong** — the plan or the behaviour — and log the decision in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]]. Sometimes the behaviour is right and the plan was wrong: the market told you something and you responded before you had words for it. That is the Intel case, and it is a _good_ outcome as long as somebody notices and rewrites the plan. What is never acceptable is leaving the two in silent disagreement, because then nobody can learn anything from either.

## 2. The annual plan for a company that cannot forecast

VSYST cannot forecast. It is pre-revenue, it has never run a full sales cycle to renewal, it does not yet know its conversion rate, its churn or its true cost to serve, and its pricing has been _decided in shape_ — per GSTIN, unlimited users, web-only billing — but not in number ([[app-store-economics/08-dzzlo-subscription-strategy|subscription strategy]]). Any twelve-month revenue number it writes is fiction with a decimal point.

This is not a reason to skip the annual plan. It is a reason to make the annual plan **a different kind of document from the one big companies write.**

### 2.1 What the plan is for, when the forecast is worthless

| The big-company annual plan               | The pre-revenue annual plan                                                                    | Why the difference                                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| ----------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| A committed financial forecast            | A **stated bet** and the evidence that would settle it                                         | You are not optimising a known machine; you are trying to find out whether the machine exists                                                                                                                                  |
| Resource allocation across business units | Resource allocation across **one company's hours**                                             | There is only one unit                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| Targets to be defended                    | Targets that are **consequences** of the bet, not the bet itself                               | A target with no mechanism behind it is Rumelt's "goals as strategy" ([_Good Strategy Bad Strategy_, Crown, 2011](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/91324/good-strategy-bad-strategy-by-richard-rumelt/9780307886231/)) |
| Revised annually                          | **Change conditions stated up front**, so revision is a mechanism rather than an embarrassment | The plan should be designed to be falsified, not defended                                                                                                                                                                      |
| Written by finance                        | Written by the CEO, in one page, in ninety minutes                                             | There is no finance function; there is no CFO                                                                                                                                                                                  |

Eisenhower's line — that plans are worthless but planning is everything — is quoted so often it has stopped meaning anything, so state the operational version instead: **the value of the annual plan is the argument that produces it and the refusals it makes explicit.** The artefact's job afterwards is to be the thing the quarterly session is measured against, and the thing the mid-quarter "can we just…" gets tested against.

### 2.2 The one-page shape

Six blocks. This is the structure of the `Annual Plan & OKRs` tab in [vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx](toolkit/vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx) and of [[C28-annual-plan-and-ceo-okr-sheet|C28]]. §11 gives you the whole thing filled in for FY 2026-27.

| Block                                              | What goes in it                                                                                                             | Length                       | Owner                             |
| -------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| **A. The year's one bet**                          | One sentence naming the thing you believe that most people in your position would not, and on which the year is being spent | 1 sentence                   | CEO alone                         |
| **B. The one thing that must be true by year end** | The single outcome that decides whether the year worked. Not a list. One                                                    | 1 sentence, with a number    | CEO                               |
| **C. Objectives (3–4) with key results**           | The measurable promises. Each traceable to the kernel per §3                                                                | 3–4 objectives, 2–3 KRs each | CEO sets; COO runs the mechanics  |
| **D. The no-list**                                 | What you are deliberately not doing this year, with the trigger that would reopen each one                                  | 5–8 lines                    | CEO — this block is not delegable |
| **E. The allocation**                              | Founder-hours by bucket as a percentage, and the discretionary rupee budget by category. The numbers from §1.3              | 1 small table                | CEO with both directors           |
| **F. Change conditions**                           | The three to five beliefs the plan rests on, each with the observation that would kill it and the date it gets checked      | 3–5 lines                    | CEO                               |

Blocks D and F are the two that get skipped and the two that do all the work. A plan without a no-list is a wish list, because at three people every yes is arithmetically a no to something else and refusing to write down which one is just refusing to do the subtraction. A plan without change conditions cannot be wrong, which means it cannot be learned from — and a plan that cannot be wrong is the same artefact as a horoscope.

### 2.3 The year's one bet — and why it must be singular

Everything else in the plan is downstream of one sentence. The bet is not the objective, not the target and not the vision; it is **the contestable belief the year's resources are being spent to test.**

Format: _"We believe **X**, which most people in our position do not, and we are spending this year finding out."_

For VSYST, drawn straight from [[05-strategy-i-diagnosis-and-the-strategy-kernel|lesson 05]]'s guiding policy:

> **We believe that in Indian fuel distribution, district-level trust density and a ruthlessly short time-to-first-invoice beat both features and price — and that a dealer who has been onboarded properly by a person he trusts will renew and refer, which is what turns twelve districts into a business. We are spending FY 2026-27 finding out whether that is true, in Raipur and Durg divisions, with three people.**

Three tests before you accept a sentence as the year's bet.

1. **Could a serious competitor bet the opposite?** Yes — plenty of Indian vertical SaaS companies bet on self-serve and volume, and some of them are right. A bet nobody could sanely take the other side of is a platitude.
2. **Does the whole year's allocation follow from it?** If the bet is trust density, then hours go to dealer contact and onboarding, not to a feature backlog, and rupees go to travel and reference-building, not to brand. If the allocation would look the same under the opposite bet, the bet is decorative.
3. **Can you say what would settle it, and by when?** If not, you have written a mission statement. §8 is where this gets teeth.

**One bet, not three.** Three bets at three people is one bet and two hobbies, and the hobbies will be the ones that feel most like progress.

### 2.4 Budget as commitment, forecast as guess — and who owns which

The COO course already draws this line and this lesson does not redraw it: **a spend budget is a decision and is enforced monthly; a revenue forecast is a guess and is labelled as one** ([[19-planning-okrs-and-the-quarterly-rhythm|COO lesson 19]] §2). What the CEO adds on top is the direction of travel and the trigger ladder.

| Artefact                                                              | Owner           | Cadence                     | Where it lives                                                                                  |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------- | --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Annual plan — bet, objectives, no-list, allocation, change conditions | **CEO**         | Once a year, March          | [[C28-annual-plan-and-ceo-okr-sheet\|C28]] + `Annual Plan & OKRs` tab                           |
| Quarterly plan doc, rocks, OKR mechanics, scoring, the retro          | **COO**         | Every quarter               | [[T19-quarterly-plan-and-review\|T19]], [[T06-okr-planning-sheet\|T06]]                         |
| Category budget and budget-vs-actual enforcement                      | **COO**         | Monthly                     | [[T20-budget-vs-actual-and-cash-forecast\|T20]]                                                 |
| Runway, scenarios and the trigger ladder                              | **CEO**         | Monthly, re-based quarterly | [[C13-runway-burn-and-scenario-planner\|C13]], [[08-capital-runway-and-fundraising\|lesson 08]] |
| Weekly scorecard, on/off-track                                        | **COO**         | Weekly                      | [[T05-kpi-scorecard\|T05]]                                                                      |

The one-line rule to remember: **the CEO decides what the money is for; the COO decides that it is spent correctly and reports when it is not.** Confusing those is how a founder ends up either micromanaging a spreadsheet or, worse, discovering in month nine that the strategy quietly changed because nobody owned the direction of the spend.

## 3. From the strategy kernel to key results — the chain that must hold

An objective that cannot be traced back to the diagnosis is a pet project with a number attached. This section is the trace, drawn end to end for VSYST, plus the test that catches a broken link.

### 3.1 The five links

```
DIAGNOSIS          what is actually going on (lesson 05 §2.3)
   ↓ because of that
GUIDING POLICY     our overall approach, and what it forbids
   ↓ which requires
COHERENT ACTION    the few things we do, each making the others stronger
   ↓ measured by
OBJECTIVE          what a human cares about this year, no numbers
   ↓ proved by
KEY RESULT         the number a spreadsheet can settle
   ↓ watched on
DASHBOARD ROW      the instrument that shows it moving (lesson 17)
```

Every link is an _implication_, not an association. If you cannot say the word "therefore" between two adjacent boxes and have it be true, the chain is broken there.

### 3.2 The chain, drawn for VSYST

The diagnosis and guiding policy below are quoted from [[05-strategy-i-diagnosis-and-the-strategy-kernel|lesson 05]] §2.3, unchanged. The right-hand columns are this lesson's contribution — and they are drafts to argue with, not answers.

**Diagnosis:** _Indian fuel dealers do not lack software; they lack a reason to change a paper-and-Tally process that has never visibly failed them. The binding constraint is adoption friction and trust, earned at district level, one dealer at a time, through people the owner already believes._

**Guiding policy:** _Win district clusters completely rather than India thinly — starting in Chhattisgarh — by making the first fourteen days of a dealer's life the product we optimise above all else, and by borrowing trust from the three channels the owner already believes. Which means we will not sell outside the cluster, will not build for non-onboarded prospects, will not compete on price, and will not spend on brand marketing before referral density exists._

| Coherent action (lesson 05)                                                                                                               | Therefore this objective                                             | Therefore these key results                                                                                                                                            | Watched on                                                                        |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **1. Choose the cluster and publish the refusal** — Raipur + Durg divisions, 12 districts; everything else waitlisted with a dated reason | **O1 — Prove the district motion in one cluster**                    | Paying GSTINs (billed and collected, not signed) · districts with ≥1 paying dealer · out-of-cluster leads refused with a logged reason ÷ out-of-cluster leads received | `CEO Dashboard`, `Pipeline`                                                       |
| **2. Make time-to-first-invoice the company's obsession** — founder-run white-glove onboarding including ledger migration                 | **O2 — Make onboarding survive without a founder**                   | Median days signup → first invoice · dealers onboarded with no founder on site · activation rate at day 14                                                             | `CEO Dashboard`, [[T05-kpi-scorecard\|T05]]                                       |
| **3. Manufacture three reference dealers deliberately** — chosen for peer credibility, over-served, filmed in the local language          | **O3 — Turn served dealers into the distribution channel**           | Filmed references live · dealer-sourced introductions received · introductions that reach a demo                                                                       | `Pipeline`                                                                        |
| **4. Buy trust wholesale through gatekeepers** — one association office-bearer, one OMC territory manager, two CA firms                   | (folds into O1 and O3 — **it is a mechanism, not an objective**)     | Written next-step commitments recorded per quarter — an input KR, because the timing belongs to other organisations                                                    | `Pipeline`                                                                        |
| **5. Ship only onboarding-blocking work**; everything else to the no-list with the requester's name                                       | (folds into O2 — a **constraint on how**, not a separate objective)  | Share of shipped work traceable to an onboarding blocker                                                                                                               | Release log, [[C25-product-bets-and-the-no-list\|C25]]                            |
| _Not in the kernel — but non-negotiable_                                                                                                  | **O4 — Stay default alive and be trustworthy with a dealer's money** | Runway in months at year end · money-affecting incidents (target zero) · board meetings held with signed minutes                                                       | `Runway & Scenarios`, [[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties\|lesson 09]] |

Three things to notice, because they are the actual lesson of this section.

**Not every coherent action becomes an objective.** Actions 4 and 5 fold into others. A one-to-one mapping from actions to objectives produces five or six objectives, which at three people is the same as none. **Fold aggressively; the test is whether folding loses a measurement, not whether it loses a line item.**

**O4 does not come from the kernel and that is fine — but it must be labelled.** Survival and governance are not strategy; they are the floor. A director's statutory duties do not become optional because they do not serve the guiding policy ([[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties|lesson 09]]). Label the row so nobody mistakes the floor for the plan.

**Some KRs are inputs, not outcomes, and must be marked as such.** "Written next-step commitments with IOCL" is a thing you control; "IOCL agreement signed" hands your score to somebody else's committee. The COO course states this rule and it is the right one ([[19-planning-okrs-and-the-quarterly-rhythm|COO lesson 19]] §12).

### 3.3 The orphan test — the ten-minute audit that deletes pet projects

Once a year, and again whenever the objective list grows, run this on every objective and every key result.

1. **Say "we are doing this because…" and finish the sentence with the diagnosis.** Out loud, in the room. Not "because it's important" — because of the specific named constraint.
2. **If the sentence needs more than one "and", the link is weak.** Real implications are short.
3. **If the honest answer is "because a customer asked" or "because it would be embarrassing not to" or "because we already started",** you have found an orphan.
4. **An orphan gets one of three fates, decided in the room, never deferred:** promoted (the diagnosis is wrong and needs rewriting — a real and respectable outcome), demoted (it is maintenance, so it leaves the plan and joins the floor), or killed (logged in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] with a review date).

The most common orphan at a technical founder's company is a piece of infrastructure work with a plausible-sounding rationale and a genuine engineering merit — the refactor, the test-coverage push, the migration. It is usually _correct_ engineering. It is usually _not_ this year's bet. Demote it to the floor with a stated maintenance budget in hours and stop giving it an objective slot.

## 4. OKRs and their honest critics

Before the technique, the case against it — properly, not as a straw man to knock down. If you cannot argue the other side, you are not making a decision, you are following a fashion.

### 4.1 Where they came from, in one paragraph

Andy Grove adapted Peter Drucker's Management by Objectives at Intel into a system he called iMBOs, built on two questions — where do I want to go, and how will I pace myself to see if I am getting there. John Doerr carried it from Intel to Kleiner Perkins and into Google in 1999, and later wrote it up as _Measure What Matters_ ([whatmatters.com](https://www.whatmatters.com/)). Google's own published playbook is the most useful primary artefact, and its most quoted mechanics are the split between **committed OKRs, where the expected score is 1.0 and anything less requires an explanation**, and **aspirational OKRs, where the expected average score is 0.7 with high variance** ([Google's OKR Playbook, via whatmatters.com](https://www.whatmatters.com/resources/google-okr-playbook)). The playbook itself ends with a disclaimer worth more than the rest: _"This is Google's approach to OKRs. Your approach may — and should — differ."_

### 4.3 The case for, at exactly this size

Three arguments survive all of the above, and they are the reason the house call is not "skip it".

1. **A written number ends an argument that is otherwise unendable.** "Onboarding is better now" is a sentence three relatives can disagree about for a year. "Median days from signup to first invoice went 21 → 12" ends it in four seconds. At a **family-founded** company where disagreement is socially expensive, this is worth more than it is at an ordinary startup, because it moves the disagreement from people to numbers.
2. **Baseline-to-target forces the baseline.** Half the value of a KR is extracted before the quarter starts, at the moment somebody has to write down what the number is _today_ — and discovers nobody has ever measured it.
3. **The quarter is the only scheduled opportunity to subtract.** Without a bounded list, additions never displace anything ([[19-planning-okrs-and-the-quarterly-rhythm|COO lesson 19]] §11).

## 5. Confidence as the real signal

Completion percentage is a lagging, self-flattering and easily-gamed number. **Typed confidence is a leading, uncomfortable and hard-to-game one.** This is the smallest change in this lesson and the one most likely to save a quarter.

### 5.1 Why percent-done lies

A key result that reads _60% complete_ in week 7 tells you almost nothing, because the remaining 40% is not the same kind of work as the first 60% and everyone in the room knows it. The classic pattern has a name in project management — **watermelon status**: green on the outside, red all the way through — and it survives because reporting green is socially cheap every single week until the week it becomes impossible.

Now compare two rows in week 7 of a 13-week quarter:

| KR                                          | % done         | Confidence                                  | What you should actually do                                                                                                                                                                       |
| ------------------------------------------- | -------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Median days signup → first invoice: 21 → 10 | 55%            | **High**                                    | Nothing. Leave it alone                                                                                                                                                                           |
| Paying GSTINs: 0 → 8                        | 50% (4 signed) | **Low, and falling — was Medium in week 4** | **This is the most important sentence anyone will say this month.** Six weeks of runway remain to act on it, and the owner is telling you the second four will be much harder than the first four |

The second row is the entire argument. Percent-done said both KRs were fine. Confidence said one of them was not, six weeks before quarter end, while there was still time.

### 5.2 The mechanic, in full

- **Typed, not computed.** The owner types a number or a band. Any formula that derives confidence from progress reproduces the thing you were trying to escape.
- **Bands or percentages, pick one and never mix.** The COO course uses High / Medium / Low and that is the right default for a three-person room ([[19-planning-okrs-and-the-quarterly-rhythm|COO lesson 19]] §3). The `Annual Plan & OKRs` tab holds a percentage because a spreadsheet can plot a percentage and a trend line is what you are after. **Rule: bands in the room, a percentage in the sheet, mapped once — High = 0.8, Medium = 0.5, Low = 0.25 — and never argued about again.**
- **Updated monthly by the KR owner, in writing, before the meeting.** Not in the meeting. A number typed in a room adjusts to the room.
- **The history is kept.** A confidence of 0.5 is nearly meaningless; a confidence that went 0.8 → 0.6 → 0.4 over three months is a finding. Keep the previous months' values in adjacent cells. The trend is the instrument, not the level.
- **A drop of two bands, or any drop below 0.4, is an automatic agenda item** at the next monthly review — no vote, no judgment, the same way a red dashboard row two months running becomes a board agenda item automatically ([[17-the-numbers-a-ceo-watches|lesson 17]] §4).
- **Never punish a low number.** The instant a low confidence produces a hard conversation about the person rather than the mechanism, every number becomes 0.7 forever and the system is dead. This is the same discipline as the blameless postmortem ([[19-planning-okrs-and-the-quarterly-rhythm|COO lesson 19]] §7).

### 5.3 Reading the line

| Pattern over three monthly readings | What it usually means                                                                            | The CEO's move                                                                                                                     |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Flat and high (0.8, 0.8, 0.8)       | Either genuinely on track, or the target was set too low                                         | Ask what it would have taken to make this a 0.5. If nobody can answer, the KR was sandbagged — note it and set the next one harder |
| Falling steadily (0.8 → 0.6 → 0.4)  | The owner is learning something the plan does not know yet                                       | **Ask what they learned, not why they are behind.** The learning is worth more than the KR                                         |
| Cliff (0.8 → 0.8 → 0.2)             | Somebody has been protecting the room, or a single dependency broke                              | Both cases are process failures. Find out which and fix the reporting, not the person                                              |
| Low and flat (0.3, 0.3, 0.3)        | The KR was wrong at the start and everyone knows it                                              | Kill it at the next quarterly with a logged reason. Do not carry a known-dead KR to quarter end for the sake of tidiness           |
| Rising (0.4 → 0.7 → 0.9)            | Good — and the _mechanism_ that caused the rise is the most valuable thing this quarter produced | Write down the mechanism in the retro so it can be repeated                                                                        |

The habit that makes this compound: at the March session, put the year's four confidence readings for every KR side by side and ask a single question — **were we well calibrated?** If confidence was consistently above what happened, the room is optimistic and should discount itself next year. If consistently below, it is anxious and is probably under-committing. Either way you have learned something about your own judgment, which is the only forecasting instrument a three-person pre-revenue company actually owns, and it is the same discipline the decision journal builds ([[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]], [[02-how-a-ceo-thinks|lesson 02]]).

## 6. Capital allocation at ₹-scale

Warren Buffett's observation is the frame for this whole section: most chief executives arrive in the seat having excelled at marketing, engineering or administration, and then "must make capital allocation decisions, a critical job that they may have never tackled and that is not easily mastered" ([Berkshire Hathaway, 1987 shareholder letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1987.html)). His analogy is that it is as if the final step for a talented musician were to be named Chairman of the Federal Reserve.

That is written about CEOs deploying hundreds of crores. It is _more_ true, not less, at VSYST — because at ₹-scale there is no CFO to catch a bad call, no board to challenge it, and the decisions are small enough that they get made without ever being recognised as capital allocation at all. The literature on the discipline is worth reading even at this size ([Mauboussin & Callahan, "Capital Allocation", Counterpoint Global Insights, Morgan Stanley, 2022](https://www.morganstanley.com/im/publication/insights/articles/article_capitalallocation.pdf); Thorndike, _The Outsiders_, [Harvard Business Review Press, 2012](https://store.hbr.org/product/the-outsiders-eight-unconventional-ceos-and-their-radically-rational-blueprint-for-success/10716)) — the mistake is assuming it starts at a scale you have not reached.

### 6.1 A method you can defend

Six inputs, one ranking, one gate. The output is not a decision — it is an argument made explicit, which is the point.

| Input                        | How to score it                                                                                                                                                                        | Why it is in the formula                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **K — Kernel fit**           | 0–3. Does this act directly on the diagnosed constraint (adoption friction and trust at district level)? 3 = directly · 2 = enables something that does · 1 = adjacent · 0 = unrelated | An allocation that scores 0 is by definition off-strategy, however attractive                                                                                                                                                                   |
| **P — Probability it works** | Your honest %, typed, before you see the ranking                                                                                                                                       | Forces the optimism into the open where it can be argued with                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| **V — Value if it works**    | Expressed in the year's one number (paying GSTINs), on a 1–5 scale                                                                                                                     | Converting to a single currency is what makes unlike options comparable                                                                                                                                                                         |
| **C — Cost**                 | In the resource being allocated: founder-hours, or rupees. Never both in the same comparison                                                                                           | Two currencies in one ranking makes the ranking meaningless                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| **T — Time to signal**       | Weeks until you would know whether it is working                                                                                                                                       | A 4-week signal is worth far more than a 30-week one at the same expected value, because it returns the resource sooner                                                                                                                         |
| **R — Reversibility**        | One-way or two-way door                                                                                                                                                                | Bezos's Type 1 / Type 2 distinction: reversible decisions get a light-weight, fast process; irreversible ones get a heavy one ([Amazon 2016 letter to shareholders](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2016-letter-to-shareholders)) |

**Score = (K × P × V) ÷ (C_normalised × T_normalised)**, then apply the gate: **a one-way door does not get decided by the ranking at all.** It gets a written [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] entry with options considered, what would change your mind, and a review date — regardless of how well it scored.

Two honesty notes, without which this instrument is worse than nothing. **The arithmetic does not decide; it exposes.** Its actual output is the sentence "we disagree about P on option 3", which is a much better conversation than "I feel we should do the integration". And **do the scoring separately before comparing** — three people scoring in a room converge on the first number said out loud, which is the anchoring effect doing your capital allocation for you.

### 6.4 The exchange rate — the number that makes hire-vs-do-without decidable

Most founders argue about whether to spend money on help. The arguable version is a single number:

> **₹ per founder-hour recovered = (fully-loaded cost of the help) ÷ (founder-hours it actually removes)**

Three rules for using it honestly.

1. **Fully loaded, not the quoted rate.** Add the hours of specifying, reviewing, correcting and managing. A contractor at ₹40,000/month who consumes 15 hours a month of the CEO's supervision has a real cost of ₹40,000 _plus 15 of the hours you were buying back_. That is why the first contractor engagement almost always recovers less than half of what it looked like on paper.
2. **Compare against the hour's value in the year's bet, not against a market salary.** If 200 founder-hours on dealer contact is what settles the year's bet, an hour recovered from screen-building is worth what that hour produces on the bet — which at VSYST's stage is far more than any rate you would pay for it.
3. **India-specific: know which relationship you are creating, before you create it.** Contractor, fixed-term consultant and employee are different legal animals with different TDS treatment, different statutory obligations (EPF, ESI, gratuity, bonus) and different exit costs, and the thresholds move. **VERIFY LIVE with the CA and CS before the first engagement**, and put the answer in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] — the first hire is closer to a one-way door than it looks, and the second one inherits whatever precedent the first one set. [[10-building-the-team-hiring-equity-and-firing|Lesson 10]] owns the hiring decision itself; this section owns only whether the rupees are better spent there than elsewhere.

### 6.6 The allocation record

Every allocation decision above the delegation limit gets four lines in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]], written _before_ the outcome is known:

```
What we allocated:      200 founder-hours / ₹1,50,000
To:                     [option], over [period]
Instead of:             [the runner-up, named]  ← this line is the one people skip
Because:                [K/P/V/C/T scores, and the one sentence]
Door:                   one-way / two-way
What would change my mind: [the observation, not a feeling]
Review on:              [a date, in the calendar]
```

The "instead of" line is the whole point of the record. A year later, the useful question is never "was that a good decision?" — it is "was that better than the thing we did not do?", and that question is unanswerable unless the runner-up was written down at the time.

## 7. Zero-based thinking once a year

Section 6 was about where the _next_ rupee and the _next_ hour go. This section is the other half, and it is the one nobody schedules: **where the rupees and hours you already committed, years ago, are still going — silently, monthly, on a standing instruction.**

One question, asked once a year, of every recurring cost and every ongoing activity:

> **If we were not already doing this, would we start it today — at this price, at this size, with what we now know?**

If the answer is no, you are not "keeping" it. You are **choosing it again**, with today's money, for reasons you have not stated. That is the whole idea, and everything below is the machinery for making the question unavoidable.

### 7.1 Where it comes from, and the objection that does not apply at three people

Zero-base budgeting was invented by **Peter Pyhrr**, a manager at Texas Instruments, who built it there in the 1960s, wrote it up in _Harvard Business Review_ in November–December 1970, and then got a phone call from a staffer for the Governor of Georgia — Jimmy Carter — who took it into Georgia's state budget and later to the federal one ([Peter Pyhrr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Pyhrr); [Zero-based budgeting — overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero-based_budgeting)). The mechanic is a single inversion. **Incremental budgeting** starts from last year's number and argues about the delta. **Zero-base budgeting** starts from zero and makes every line argue for its own existence.

That inversion is the direct antidote to §1.2's finding — that firms re-spend last year's pattern year after year almost regardless of strategy. Incremental budgeting is not a neutral process that happens to produce inertia; **it is the mechanism by which inertia is produced.**

The standard objection is real and you should know it: at large companies ZBB is expensive, consumes months of finance-team effort, and frequently degenerates into a slash-and-burn cost programme that cuts the future along with the fat ([McKinsey, "The return of zero-base budgeting"](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/the-return-of-zero-base-budgeting)).

**None of that applies to VSYST**, for one reason: the cost of the exercise scales with the number of budget lines, and VSYST has perhaps forty. The whole review is **one hour, once a year**, run by three people who personally authorised every line. The large-company objection is an objection to the _overhead_ of ZBB, and at this size the overhead is an hour.

What _does_ apply is the second objection — that a cost programme can cut muscle. The defence is §7.5's floor rule: **name what is not up for debate before you start**, so the hour is spent on the discretionary lines rather than on re-arguing whether to have backups.

### 7.2 The extension that matters more than the money

The rupee version of this question is the easy half. At a three-person, pre-revenue company the recurring **rupees** are small; the recurring **activities** are enormous, because they consume the only genuinely scarce resource (§1.3).

So run the same question over five registers, not one.

| Register                               | What is in it at VSYST                                                                                                                                              | What the question typically catches                                                                                                                                                         | Who assembles the list                                                    |
| -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Recurring cash**                     | Cloud and database tiers, SMS/OTP, monitoring, design and dev tooling, domains and email, developer-programme fees, gateway charges, CA and CS retainers, insurance | Tiers sized for a load you no longer have; two tools doing one job; seats for people who left; an annual plan auto-renewing at a price you would not accept today                           | COO, from 12 months of bank and card lines grouped by payee               |

### 7.4 The three answers that mean "cut", and the one that does not

Three sentences get said in every one of these reviews. All three are cuts.

1. **"We might need it later."** Then start it later. What actually matters is not whether you might need it but what it would cost to restart — and for almost everything in the cash register (a SaaS tool, a larger tier, a subscription) the restart cost is fifteen minutes and a credit card. Ask _"what is the switching cost back in?"_ rather than _"might we want it?"_, and the argument resolves itself. Where the restart cost is genuinely high — a data-retaining service where cancellation deletes history, a domain, a certificate, an app-store account with review history attached — that is a real KEEP, and it is a KEEP for a stated reason rather than a vague one.
2. **"It's only ₹900 a month."** Multiply by twelve. Then multiply by the number of lines on the list to which the same sentence was applied. The small-line defence is exactly the mechanism by which ₹8,000 a month of nothing accumulates at a company with no procurement process, and it is worth naming out loud the first time it is used so that the second use is embarrassing.
3. **"Someone set that up and I'm not sure what breaks if we stop it."** An unknown dependency is a **finding**, not an argument. Timebox thirty minutes to find out, this week, with an owner. At a three-person company, a recurring charge nobody can explain is a small governance failure and it is better discovered here than during diligence ([[C14-fundraise-readiness-and-data-room-index\|C14]]).

The one answer that is a legitimate KEEP: **"cutting it costs more than it saves, and here is the number."** Insurance is the archetype. So is the CA retainer, so are backups, so is anything whose absence creates an asymmetric downside ([[18-risk-crisis-and-the-hard-things\|lesson 18]]). Note the shape of that sentence — it contains a number. "It's important" does not.

### 7.5 The floor — declared before you start

Some recurring things get zero-based **for price and tier, never for existence.** Read this list out at minute zero and strike it off:

- Statutory and professional: CA retainer, CS retainer, audit fee, statutory filing fees, the compliance calendar's costs ([[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties\|lesson 09]] §6, [[T16-compliance-calendar\|T16]]).
- Insurance of any kind that is in force.
- Backups, disaster recovery, and anything holding customer data or an audit trail.
- Security-relevant subscriptions and the domain and certificate renewals.
- Anything a customer contract obliges you to run.
- The board and governance cadence itself.

Everything else is on the table, including things that feel permanent. **Especially** things that feel permanent — feeling permanent is what this hour is for.

### 7.6 The discipline of actually killing it

Here is how this exercise fails, and it fails this way at almost every company that tries it once: **the review happens, the verdicts get recorded, and nothing is cancelled.** Six months later the same list is reviewed again, with the same verdicts. The reason is mundane. Cancelling a subscription is twenty minutes of irritating admin — a login nobody remembers, a retention flow designed to exhaust you, a vendor that requires an email — and irritating admin with no deadline does not happen.

Five mechanisms, in order of how much they matter.

1. **One block, not eight tasks.** Put a single 90-minute "cancellations" block in one person's calendar within seven days, with the whole CUT list in the invite. Eight separate to-dos will produce two cancellations; one block will produce eight.
2. **The proof is the bank statement.** A cancellation is complete when **the charge is absent from next month's statement** — not when someone marks a task done. Put a reminder on the statement date to check the specific lines. This is the same evidence standard the COO's budget-vs-actual already enforces ([[T20-budget-vs-actual-and-cash-forecast\|T20]]).
3. **Renewal dates go in the compliance calendar.** Every annual subscription's renewal date belongs in [[T16-compliance-calendar\|T16]] alongside the statutory dates, with a **30-day-before** reminder. An auto-renewal that fires before the decision is made has removed a choice from you, and that is the actual harm — more than the money. This applies to the developer-programme fees too: the Apple Developer Program is an annual paid membership and Google Play's is a one-time registration fee ([Apple Developer Program](https://developer.apple.com/programs/); [Google Play Console](https://play.google.com/console/signup)) — **VERIFY LIVE** current fees, both change and both are billed in foreign currency, which means the rupee number moves even when the price does not.
4. **What you already paid is irrelevant.** The money spent on a tool last year is gone whether you keep it or not; the only question is whether the _next_ payment is worth making. This is the sunk-cost effect, and it is one of the most robust findings in behavioural science precisely because it is so hard to override in the moment ([Arkes & Blumer, "The psychology of sunk cost", _Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes_ 35(1), 1985](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/4812596_The_psychology_of_sunk_cost)). Arkes and Blumer's explanation is worth carrying: the driver is **the desire not to appear wasteful** — which is a social instinct, not a financial calculation, and at a family company it is stronger, not weaker.
5. **Log the count, not just the amount.** Write in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log\|C29]]: how many lines were reviewed, how many were cut, and the annual rupees and hours released. Next March, the comparison is the interesting number. **A review that cuts nothing two years running is not a disciplined company; it is a review that has stopped being real.**

## 8. Persist or pivot

This is the most consequential decision a founder-CEO makes, and it is the one most often made by drift. Not decided badly — **not decided at all.** Persist is the default that happens when nobody schedules the alternative, and it renews itself silently, one week at a time, until the bank balance makes the decision on your behalf.

The data supports the shape of that claim. CB Insights' post-mortem work finds that "ran out of capital" tops the list of stated causes — but it is almost always the _final_ cause of death rather than the root, with poor product-market fit and bad timing sitting underneath ([CB Insights, "Why Startups Fail: Top Reasons"](https://www.cbinsights.com/research/report/startup-failure-reasons-top/)). Read that carefully, because it is the argument for this entire section: **if cash-out is the symptom and no-market-need is the disease, then the decision was available months earlier and was not taken.** The company did not fail to pivot because pivoting was hard. It failed to pivot because nobody had put a date on the question.

### 8.1 Why the default is persist, and why it is so strong

Four forces, all of them stronger at a bootstrapped family company than at a VC-backed one.

| Force                                           | The mechanism                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       | Why it is worse at VSYST                                                                                                                                                                      |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Escalation of commitment**                    | People commit _more_ resources to a failing course of action when they were personally responsible for choosing it — the effect is strongest for the person who made the original call ([Staw, "Knee-deep in the Big Muddy", _Organizational Behavior and Human Performance_ 16, 1976](https://www.academia.edu/23160004/Knee_deep_in_the_big_muddy_a_study_of_escalating_commitment_to_a_chosen_course_of_action)) | The founder-CEO chose the strategy, wrote the code, and made the promise. There is nobody in the room who did not choose it                                                                   |
| **Sunk cost, and the wastefulness norm**        | Having invested, people continue even when continuing is irrational — driven by the desire not to appear wasteful ([Arkes & Blumer, 1985](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/4812596_The_psychology_of_sunk_cost))                                                                                                                                                                                            | Two years of a technical founder's life is the most emotionally expensive sunk cost there is, and it is not on any balance sheet                                                              |
| **No external forcing function**                | A VC-backed company has a board, a next round and a burn multiple that forces the conversation                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      | VSYST has none of these. The only forcing function available is one you install yourself, in advance                                                                                          |
| **Family cost of changing your mind in public** | Reversing a stated direction in front of relatives who invested money and reputation is socially expensive in a way it is not among unrelated co-founders                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           | This is real and it should be named in the room rather than pretended away ([[11-culture-and-values-as-a-ceo-instrument\|lesson 11]], [[18-risk-crisis-and-the-hard-things\|lesson 18]] §9.8) |

The counter-mechanism for all four is the same and it is boring: **decide the review date and the evidence criteria in advance, while confidence is high**, and let that version of you bind the version who will be defensive. That is §8.5. Everything before it is the evidence standard the review runs on.

### 8.2 What counts as evidence — the assumption register does the work

You cannot falsify a belief you never wrote down. The artefact that makes this section possible already exists, and **this lesson extends it rather than replacing it**: the **assumption register** is defined in [[05-strategy-i-diagnosis-and-the-strategy-kernel|lesson 05]] §7.5 and ships as the three-row register in §9.2, held in the `Strategy Kernel` tab of [vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx](toolkit/vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx). **Lesson 05 owns A1, A2 and A3, and the wording below is lesson 05's — do not renumber or re-word those three here.** Rows **A4 onwards are additions this lesson introduces**, because planning depends on beliefs the kernel did not have to name. Each row is a belief the strategy rests on, with a test, a **falsifying observation** and a date; if this table and lesson 05 ever disagree about what A1, A2 or A3 means, lesson 05 is right and this file is the one to fix.

The intellectual ancestor is McGrath and MacMillan's **discovery-driven planning**: for a venture where the numbers are unknowable, you plan by listing the assumptions, ranking them by how much rides on them, and designing the cheapest test of each — rather than by projecting a spreadsheet ([McGrath & MacMillan, "Discovery-Driven Planning", _HBR_, July–August 1995](https://hbr.org/1995/07/discovery-driven-planning); [overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery-driven_planning)). The plan is a list of things you believe, in the order in which their being wrong would hurt.

Here is VSYST's register as it stands, in that form — **A1–A3 carried over from lesson 05 §7.5 and §9.2 unchanged in identity and wording, A4–A6 added here.** The three-row kernel register is the parent; this is the same register with the planning rows attached. **These are drafts to argue with at the March session, not answers** — and the last two columns are this section's contribution: the minimum sample that makes a falsifying observation count, and what a failure of each belief would actually imply, in §8.6's pivot vocabulary.

| #                     | The belief                                                                                                                                                                                                                  | The observation that would falsify it                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          | Minimum sample before it counts                                                                                                      | If falsified, this implies                                                                                                                                                                                                                   |

Three rules govern this table, and they are what separate it from a list of worries.

1. **A falsifying observation is an observation, not a feeling.** "Dealers don't seem very keen" is not evidence. "Nineteen of twenty onboarded dealers declined to pay ₹1 when asked by name, in person" is. If the criterion cannot be checked by someone who was not in the room, rewrite it.
2. **It must be written before it happens.** A criterion invented after the disappointing result will always be adjusted, unconsciously, until the result does not count. This is the single most important sentence in this section.
3. **The minimum sample is part of the criterion.** At three dealers, nothing is falsified and nothing is proved; that is not evidence, it is anecdote with a percentage sign ([[17-the-numbers-a-ceo-watches|lesson 17]] §7). Write the N into the row so that "we tried it and it didn't work" cannot be said about four attempts.

### 8.3 "Not working yet" versus "not working"

Both look identical from inside. The five questions below separate them, and they must be answered in that order, because question 1 disqualifies most of the pivots founders are tempted into.

| #     | The question                                                             | "Not working **yet**"                                                                                                                         | "**Not** working"                                                                                                  |
| ----- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **1** | **Did we actually run the plan, at the intended volume?**                | The motion was executed as designed — twenty conversations, not six; the full onboarding, not a partial one                                   | You executed perhaps 30% of it, mostly the comfortable parts. **You cannot falsify a hypothesis you never tested** |
| **2** | **Is any leading indicator moving, even though the lagging one is not?** | Second meetings are happening, day-14 activation is climbing, unprompted referrals have started, WhatsApp questions are getting more specific | Every indicator flat or falling across the whole period, at every stage of the funnel                              |
| **3** | **Do people behave differently from how they speak?**                    | People say "not now" but keep using it, keep asking, keep introducing you                                                                     | Everyone says yes warmly and **nobody signs**. Warmth is not a signal in Indian B2B; signatures and payments are   |
| **4** | **Is the failure in the belief or in the execution?**                    | The belief held; the execution was slow, under-resourced or badly sequenced — and you can name specifically what you would do differently     | You did it well and the belief did not survive contact                                                             |
| **5** | **Have enough complete cycles run?**                                     | Your cycle is 10 weeks and you have watched three full ones end to end                                                                        | You are reading three weeks of a ten-week cycle and calling it a trend                                             |

**Question 1 is the one that saves companies.** The most common false pivot in a small company is pivoting away from something that was never actually attempted at full strength — because the half-attempt produced a weak result, the weak result felt like evidence, and pivoting is more exciting than doing twenty more dealer conversations. Before any pivot verdict, force the sentence: _"We did the thing we said, at the volume we said, for the period we said."_ If it is not true, the verdict is **persist and execute**, and that is not a failure of nerve, it is the only honest reading of the data.

**Question 4 is the one that decides.** An execution failure is fixed by persisting differently. A belief failure is fixed by pivoting. Confusing them produces the two classic errors — pivoting away from a good strategy you never ran (question 1), and persisting on a dead belief with better project management (question 4).

### 8.4 The trap: the strategy that is always six months from proof

This deserves its own section because it is the specific way that intelligent, honest founders lose three years.

Here is the pattern. At every review, the answer is _"we're close."_ And at every review there is a real, specific, **true** reason why it has not landed yet — the OMC's territory manager was transferred; the payment gateway onboarding took eleven weeks; harvest season; the biggest prospect's son took over the firm; the release slipped. Every reason is credible. Every reason is different from last time. Nobody is lying, nobody is even being optimistic in any way they could detect.

**The reasons are individually true and collectively a pattern**, and the pattern is the finding. Six diagnostics.

1. **Count the "almost"s.** Go to [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] and the past four quarterly reviews and write down every date on which somebody said this was roughly a quarter away. **If there are three such dates, the estimate is not the problem — the model is.** One slipped forecast is life; three is a systematic error in how you believe the world works, and that error is what needs revising, not the deadline.
2. **Same obstacle or a new one each time?** A recurring obstacle is a **diagnosis** — you have found the real constraint and it is not the one in your kernel, which is §8.2's **A4** failing quietly rather than being tested. A brand-new obstacle every quarter, each one unforeseeable, is usually a rationalisation engine: the mind will always produce a locally valid reason, and it will produce a fresh one indefinitely.
3. **Apply §7's question to the strategy itself.** _If we were not already doing this, knowing everything we now know — the conversion we have seen, the cycle length we have measured, the objections we have heard — would we start this today?_ This is exactly why zero-based thinking sits next to persist-or-pivot in this lesson. It is the same question at two different altitudes, and at the strategy altitude it is the more dangerous one to skip.
4. **Check the calibration of whoever owns the claim.** §5.3's exercise, applied to a person: what did their confidence line do over the last four quarters, and what actually happened? A founder who has been at 0.8 through four missed quarters is not dishonest — they are **uncalibrated**, and their next 0.8 should be read as a 0.4 until the record changes.
5. **Compare the honest time-to-proof against the runway.** If proving the bet genuinely needs nine more months and the runway is five, the decision has already been made by arithmetic and only the announcement is pending. Being **default alive** — able to reach profitability on current trajectory without new money — is the frame here, and a founder who cannot say which side of that line they are on is not in a position to argue for six more months ([Paul Graham, "Default Alive or Default Dead?"](https://paulgraham.com/aord.html); [[08-capital-runway-and-fundraising|lesson 08]]).
6. **Ask what would have to be true for the next six months to be different from the last six.** Not what you _hope_. What is **structurally different** — a shipped capability, a signed partner, a new channel, a person hired. If the honest answer is "we'll work harder and the market will be readier", that is the same six months again.

**The other side, honestly.** Real category-creating businesses do take longer than plan, and a great many people who quit at month eighteen were wrong. This lesson will not pretend that persistence is a vice. What distinguishes the ones who were right to persist is **not** that they ignored the evidence — it is that their leading indicators were moving even while the lagging ones were flat (§8.3, question 2). Andy Grove's account of Intel's memory exit is the canonical version: the signal was visible in the operating data long before the corporate self-image caught up, and the people who saw it first were not the executives ([Andrew Grove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Grove); and §1.2's Burgelman citation).

So the honest formulation is not "persistence versus pivoting". It is:

> **Persistence with a falsifiable claim and a moving leading indicator is a strategy. Persistence without either is a habit wearing a strategy's clothes.**

### 8.5 The pre-committed review date

The whole of §8 reduces to one mechanism, and it costs nothing.

**Set the date and the criteria at the March session, while confidence is high, and write both into block F of [[C28-annual-plan-and-ceo-okr-sheet|C28]] and into the calendar.** Then honour it the way you honour a board meeting, which is to say: it happens whether or not it is convenient, and it happens _especially_ when it is not.

Two levels, deliberately different in weight:

|                     | **The quarterly check**                                                  | **The annual verdict**                                                                                                                              |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **When**            | Attached to the quarterly session — first week of July, October, January | The March annual session                                                                                                                            |
| **Question**        | "Has any change condition fired?"                                        | "Persist, adjust, pivot or stop?"                                                                                                                   |
| **Time**            | 15 minutes                                                               | 60–90 minutes, its own agenda item, before next year's plan is written                                                                              |
| **Default outcome** | Continue                                                                 | **None.** Persist must be argued for                                                                                                                |
| **Who**             | All three directors                                                      | All three directors, plus the outside advisor if one exists ([[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties\|lesson 09]] §8.3)                      |
| **Output**          | A dated line: conditions checked, none fired                             | A written verdict in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log\|C29]] and, if anything changed, an edit to [[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager\|C04]] |

The form to fill, at the annual verdict:

```
Belief under review:       [the year's one bet, verbatim from block A]
Set on:                    [date]        Reviewed on:  [date]
What we said would settle it (verbatim, from block F and its §8.2 register row —
                           do not paraphrase; register rows A1–A3 are lesson 05's):

Did we run the plan at intended volume?     yes / partly / no   + evidence
Leading indicators over four quarters:      moving / flat / falling
Number of times this was "one quarter away":
Runway today:  ___ months     Runway at the next review date: ___ months
Honest time-to-proof from here:  ___ months
VERDICT:  PERSIST unchanged / PERSIST with a named change / PIVOT [type] / STOP
If PERSIST — what is structurally different this time:
If PIVOT   — what is preserved, what is discarded, which kernel line changes:
Next review date:          [in the calendar before anyone leaves the room]
Signed:                    [all three directors]
```

Four rules that make the form more than paperwork.

1. **Invert the burden of proof.** At the review, **PERSIST is the option that must be argued for**; it is not the outcome that occurs when nobody argues. This single inversion is most of the value of the mechanism, because it converts an unexamined default into a decision with an author.
2. **"More of the same, harder" is a legitimate verdict exactly once.** The second time it is offered, it is a pattern (§8.4, diagnostic 6) and the answer must name something structurally different or move to PIVOT.
3. **Read the pre-mortem before you read the results.** You already wrote how this fails, at the start of the year, in [[C30-pre-mortem-crisis-playbook-and-succession|C30]]. Read that document first, cold, before looking at the numbers — and check whether the thing you predicted is happening. It very often is, and it is very often invisible until you re-read your own prediction.
4. **Never hold this review in a bad month.** Not because the answer should be softer, but because a verdict formed in the week of a lost deal or a bad outage is a mood, not a finding. Fix the date in advance precisely so that it lands on an arbitrary day rather than an emotional one.

### 8.6 The varieties of pivot, and what each preserves

Eric Ries defines a pivot as _"a structured course correction designed to test a fundamental new hypothesis about the product, strategy and engine of growth"_ and catalogues ten types — zoom-in, zoom-out, customer segment, customer need, platform, business architecture, value capture, engine of growth, channel and technology ([Ries, _The Lean Startup_](https://theleanstartup.com/); [summary of the ten types](https://www.forbes.com/sites/martinzwilling/2011/09/16/top-10-ways-entrepreneurs-pivot-a-lean-startup/)). Note the two words doing the work: **structured**, and **new hypothesis**. A pivot is not a change of mood; it is the replacement of one falsifiable claim with another.

Four of the ten matter at VSYST. What distinguishes them is **what each one preserves**, because at a company with two years of accumulated assets, the preserved thing is the entire argument for pivoting rather than starting over.

| Pivot                                                                                         | The question it answers                          | Preserves                                                                                                                           | Discards                                                                                     | The VSYST version                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            | Cost and speed                                                                                                                       |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Segment** — same product, different customer                                                | "Is the product right and the buyer wrong?"      | The codebase, the domain knowledge, the product loop (rate → order → dispatch → invoice → voucher), most of the pricing metric      | The pipeline, the references, the ICP work, the district plan, much of the messaging         | Petrol-pump dealers → **bulk-diesel operators** or **lubricant distributors**; or the **transport fleet** as the tenant rather than the free rider                                                                                                                                           | **Cheapest available to VSYST**, because the fuel-distribution loop is shared across all three segments. Weeks, not quarters         |
| **Problem** (customer need) — same customer, different problem                                | "Is the buyer right and the pain wrong?"         | The relationships, the district trust, the sales channel, the association and OMC access — **the most expensive assets VSYST owns** | Most of the product surface, and the roadmap                                                 | The dealer's real burning problem turns out to be **credit collection from B2B customers**, or **OMC ledger reconciliation**, or **driver/tanker compliance records** — rather than order management. DZZLO already contains a candidate: the voucher/payment/reconciliation layer           | Slow and expensive in engineering; **preserves the thing that took longest to build** — trust. Two to four quarters                  |
| **Business model** (value capture) — same product, same customer, different way of being paid | "Is the value real and the meter wrong?"         | Literally everything except the pricing page and the billing code                                                                   | The revenue model, and any forecast built on it                                              | Per-GSTIN subscription → **per-transaction or per-KL**; → **payments take-rate** through the gateway ([[14-partnerships-ecosystem-and-founder-relationships\|Easebuzz relationship, lesson 14]]); → **OMC- or enterprise-paid** seats; → free product with paid reconciliation or onboarding | Fast to test, slow to unwind if wrong. **This one reopens a settled decision** — see the warning below                               |
| **Channel** — same everything, different route to the customer                                | "Is the offer right and the road wrong?"         | Nearly everything: product, segment, problem, pricing                                                                               | The go-to-market plan, the cost structure, possibly the district thesis (A4, and A1 with it) | Founder-led district selling → **association-led**; → **OMC territory-manager-led**; → **the dealer's CA or accountant as referrer**; → **dealer-referral-led**; → self-serve                                                                                                                | **Cheapest to test of all four**, and therefore the first to try when the constraint might be distribution rather than product       |
| _(Not a pivot)_ **Zoom-in**                                                                   | "Which one part of this is actually being used?" | Everything                                                                                                                          | Scope                                                                                        | Narrowing DZZLO to the one module dealers genuinely open daily and making that the product                                                                                                                                                                                                   | Frequently mistaken for a pivot. It is a **focusing decision**, it is usually right, and it does not require any of §8.7's machinery |

**The warning on the business-model row.** VSYST has a settled direction — dealer companies pay per GSTIN, users unlimited and free, billing web-only to avoid store commission, app gated server-side ([[app-store-economics/08-dzzlo-subscription-strategy|subscription strategy]]). A business-model pivot is, by definition, a decision to reopen that. That is allowed — settled is not sacred — but it is allowed **only at a review, with a [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] entry and a kernel edit**, never in a hallway conversation and never because one prospect asked for a different pricing shape. The value of a settled decision is that it stops being re-argued weekly; reopening it casually destroys that value permanently.

**And the boundary of the word.** A change that preserves nothing is not a pivot. It is a new company wearing the old company's name — and inheriting its cap table, its obligations, its customers, its contracts and its technical debt. That is sometimes the right thing to do, and when it is, decide it as what it is: a shutdown plus a new venture, with capital, entity, founder contracts and vesting all revisited ([[03-the-ceo-core-value-and-the-founder-contracts|lesson 03]], [[08-capital-runway-and-fundraising|lesson 08]], [[C02-founders-agreement-and-vesting-checklist|C02]]). Calling it a pivot is how founders skip that conversation.

> **A pivot preserves something on purpose. Name the preserved thing out loud, before you announce anything — because if you cannot, you are not pivoting.**

### 8.7 What a pivot changes downstream — the two-week checklist

A pivot that changes the slides and not the artefacts is a mood swing. Every pivot updates the following, in this order, inside two weeks.

| #   | Artefact                                                                                                                                         | The specific edit                                                                                                                                                                                                           | Why the order matters                                                                                                                                                                      |
| --- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1   | [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log\|C29]]                                                                                               | The full entry — evidence, options considered, what was preserved, what would make you reverse it — **written before the change is announced**                                                                              | Writing it first is what stops a pivot being retro-fitted with better reasoning later                                                                                                      |

| 4   | [[C28-annual-plan-and-ceo-okr-sheet\|C28]]                                                                                                       | Block A (the bet), B (the one thing), C (objectives and KRs), D (the no-list), E (the allocation), F (new change conditions)                                                                                                | A pivot with the old allocation is not happening. §1                                                                                                                                       |

| 6   | [[C08-positioning-and-messaging-canvas\|C08]], [[C09-icp-and-customer-discovery-guide\|C09]], [[C10-founder-led-sales-pipeline-and-script\|C10]] | Positioning, ICP, script, objection bank                                                                                                                                                                                    | The sales conversation is where a half-executed pivot is most visible and most damaging                                                                                                    |
| 7   | The board                                                                                                                                        | A pivot is a **board matter**: tabled, discussed, minuted — not a WhatsApp message ([[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties\|lesson 09]])                                                                            | The minute book is the only record that a decision was made rather than drifted into                                                                                                       |

**How to announce it.** With the evidence, not with the enthusiasm.

> _"Here is what we said would settle it. Here is what we observed. Here is what we are doing instead, and here is what we are keeping."_

That sentence keeps a team's and a customer's trust, because it shows the decision had a method. **"Exciting new direction!"** burns it, because everyone in the room remembers that the last direction was also exciting, and they will now discount the next one too. A pivot announced as evidence buys you the right to pivot again if you need to. A pivot announced as enthusiasm spends that right.

### 9.3 The interlock rules

Six rules govern how the three cadences fit together. They exist because each one was learned by getting the order wrong.

1. **The quarterly session precedes the board meeting. Always.** You bring the board a **scored quarter and a decided next quarter** — not a request that the board plan for you. A board that plans is a board doing the CEO's job, and the founder-directors will do it badly because they are also the executives.
2. **The MBR precedes the quarterly session, and feeds it.** The quarter's last monthly review is the input to the session, not a competitor to it. If they land in the same week, move the MBR earlier, never the session later.
3. **The confidence update precedes the MBR.** Typed the last working day of the month, before anyone is in a room (§5.2). A number typed in a meeting adjusts to the meeting.
4. **The statutory calendar never moves; planning bends around it.** The CS owns those dates, the penalties are real, and no planning session is worth a ₹100-per-day late fee with no cap ([[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties|lesson 09]] §6).
5. **The zero-based review (§7) and the persist/pivot verdict (§8) both attach to the March session, deliberately.** Same day, same people, same evidence — because the answer to "what should we stop paying for?" and "should we still be doing this at all?" are the same question at two altitudes, and separating them lets each one be deferred in favour of the other.
6. **Nothing in this system is allowed to grow.** If the annual session needs a full day, the pre-work was not done. If the MBR needs two hours, the dashboard has too many rows. Cadence bloat is the way an operating system dies, and it dies by addition ([[15-the-ceo-operating-cadence-and-calendar|lesson 15]]).

### 9.4 What the whole system costs

Founders reject planning on the grounds that they do not have time for it. That claim can be checked.

| Item                                     | Per year          | CEO hours             |
| ---------------------------------------- | ----------------- | --------------------- |
| Annual session + pre-work                | 1 × (4h + 3h)     | 7                     |
| Quarterly sessions                       | 3 × 2.5h          | 7.5                   |
| Monthly business review                  | 12 × 1.5h         | 18                    |
| Confidence update                        | 12 × 20 min       | 4                     |
| Zero-based hour + cancellations          | 1h + 1.5h         | 2.5                   |
| Persist/pivot verdict + quarterly checks | 1.5h + 3 × 15 min | 2.25                  |
| **Total**                                |                   | **≈ 41 hours a year** |

Against roughly 2,600 usable CEO hours in a year, that is **about 1.6%.** The honest sentence is therefore not "we don't have time for planning." It is: _"we would rather spend those forty-one hours on something else"_ — which is a real position, and one you should have to say out loud before adopting.

## 10. When the plan meets reality mid-quarter

The plan is written in the first week of the quarter, and by week three the world has moved. This is normal and the system is designed for it. What the system is _not_ designed for is the quarter quietly becoming a suggestion.

### 10.1 The rule

> **Goals do not change mid-quarter. Plans change constantly. The two are different objects and must never be confused.**

- The **goal** is the key result: the number, the baseline, the target, the date.
- The **plan** is the set of actions you currently believe will move it.

Plans should change whenever you learn something — weekly, if you are learning fast. Goals should hold still for thirteen weeks. The reason is not discipline for its own sake. It is that **the entire value of §5's confidence line depends on the target holding still**: you cannot read a trend against a moving baseline, and the moment a KR can be renegotiated in week seven, every KR becomes a forecast rather than a commitment and the score stops meaning anything at all.

### 10.2 The five things that actually happen, and what each licenses

| What happened                                                                                                                          | Plan change or goal change?                      | What you do                                                                                                                                                                                                     | What you log                                                                         |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Someone is behind**                                                                                                                  | **Neither.** This is not an event                | Nothing procedural. This is exactly what a falling confidence number is for (§5)                                                                                                                                | The confidence drop, which is already logged                                         |
| **A better idea appears**                                                                                                              | **Plan change**                                  | If it moves the _same_ KR — take it, today, no ceremony. If it moves a _different_ one, it goes on the no-list with a trigger and waits for the quarterly session                                               | One line in the quarter's plan doc                                                   |
| **A new external fact makes the KR genuinely impossible** — a counterparty's decision slips, a regulation changes, a partner withdraws | **Plan change + a logged exception on the goal** | The KR **stays on the sheet**, marked `EXCEPTED` with the date and the observable fact. At quarter end it is scored "not scored — excepted." **Never rewritten to a number you can hit. Never quietly deleted** | The exception record (§10.4)                                                         |
| **A genuinely bigger opportunity appears** — an OMC pilot, a GSP window, an enterprise transporter                                     | **Depends. Run the gate in §10.3**               | The gate, in writing, in under an hour                                                                                                                                                                          | [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log\|C29]] if it displaces anything material |
| **A crisis** — an outage, a money-affecting incident, a founder unavailable                                                            | **The floor pre-empts the plan, automatically**  | No meeting required. [[18-risk-crisis-and-the-hard-things\|Lesson 18]] owns this. The quarter absorbs the hit and is scored honestly                                                                            | The incident record, plus one line in the quarter's retro                            |

The first row is the one people misuse. **"We're behind" is not an exception**, it is the normal state of week seven, and treating it as an event is how a quarter gets renegotiated into meaninglessness.

### 10.3 The genuinely-bigger opportunity — a gate, not a veto

At a three-person company, the real mid-quarter threat is not distraction by rubbish. It is distraction by something **genuinely good** — because that is the one nobody can argue against without sounding small. Four questions, in writing, in under an hour.

1. **Does it act on the same diagnosed constraint?** If yes, it is not a distraction at all — it is a **better plan for the same goal**, and you should take it immediately. Most "should we chase this?" arguments dissolve at this question.
2. **Is it time-boxed and reversible?** A two-week spike with a defined exit and a named deliverable is a plan change and the CEO can decide it alone. An open-ended pursuit is a **strategy change** and belongs at the quarterly session, not on a Tuesday.
3. **What is it displacing — by name?** Capacity is conserved at three people. Write down the specific thing that will now not happen, and tell whoever owned it, the same day. "We'll fit it in" means "something will silently not happen and we will find out in week eleven".
4. **Does it require reopening a settled decision?** Per-GSTIN pricing, web-only billing, cluster-only selling, the no-list. **If yes, it is not decided mid-quarter at all.** It gets a [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] entry and a slot at the next session. This is the only veto in the gate, and it is what keeps settled decisions settled.

### 10.4 The logged exception

```
Date:                    Quarter:            Decided by:  CEO alone / all three
KR affected:
What changed in the world:   [observation, with a source — not an interpretation]
Plan change:                 [what we will now do instead]
Goal:                        unchanged  /  EXCEPTED  (rare; requires an external fact)
Displaced — by name:         [the specific thing that will now not happen, and who owned it]
Reversible?                  yes / no      Time-boxed to:  [date]
Reviewed at:                 [the next quarterly session]
```

Two counting rules make the record useful rather than decorative.

- **Three or more exceptions in a quarter is itself the finding.** Bring the _count_ to the quarterly session, not the individual exceptions. Either the plan was written against a world that does not exist — which is a planning defect worth fixing — or the quarter is being treated as a suggestion, which is a discipline defect. They need different responses and the count is what tells you which.
- **Zero exceptions across four quarters is also a finding.** It usually means the goals were soft enough that reality never collided with them.

### 10.5 Adapting versus abandoning

The difference is not in the size of the change. It is in whether the change has an author.

|                              | **Adapting**                                  | **Abandoning**                                     |
| ---------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| **The goal**                 | Unchanged, and still on the sheet             | Quietly stops being mentioned                      |
| **The change**               | Written, dated, with the displaced item named | Undocumented; visible only in hindsight            |
| **Who knows**                | All three directors, the same day             | Discovered later, by someone else                  |
| **At quarter end**           | Scored honestly, including a zero             | _"We moved on from that"_                          |
| **Reversibility**            | You could still go back                       | The path back closed and nobody noticed it closing |
| **How it feels in the room** | Uncomfortable — constraint                    | **Relief**                                         |

That last row is not a joke and it is the most useful diagnostic in this section. **Abandonment feels like relief; adaptation feels like constraint.** When a mid-quarter change makes the room feel lighter, stop and check whether you have just deleted the hardest thing on the list — because the hardest thing on the list is, reliably, the thing the year's bet depends on.

The one-line rule to carry:

> **You may change what you do on any day of the week. You may change what you promised only at the quarterly session, out loud, with the reason written down.**

### Block A — the year's one bet

### Block B — the one thing that must be true by 31 March 2027

> **Eight paying GSTINs — billed _and collected_, GST-exclusive — across at least four districts, of which at least two were onboarded with no founder on site.**

Three clauses, each load-bearing, and each one is there to close a specific escape hatch: **collected** closes "signed but unbilled" (§4.2's ethics row); **four districts** closes "one friendly dealer eight times"; **two without a founder** closes "the motion works only because the CEO personally does it", which is the difference between a business and a job.

## 12. Exercises

Each exercise produces a dated artefact. Do them in order — **12.1 depends on 12.2 and 12.5 having been done first**, so if you only have one evening, do 12.5, then 12.2, then 12.1.

**12.1 — Fill [[C28-annual-plan-and-ceo-okr-sheet|C28]] for the coming year (90 min, all three directors, one sitting, phones in another room).** Open §11's draft and work through the six blocks **in order A → F, and do not skip forward**. Rules for the room: block A must end as **one** sentence, not two (§2.3); every baseline in block C is either a real number or the word _unknown_ — never an estimate, because an estimate in a baseline cell poisons the whole year's scoring; block D must reach **at least seven lines** and every line needs a trigger, because a refusal without a trigger becomes either a permanent prejudice or a quiet reversal; block E's hours percentages must sum to 100 per person; block F needs at least three beliefs with observable falsifying conditions. **Artefact:** a filled `C28` in the vault and a filled `Annual Plan & OKRs` tab in [vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx](toolkit/vsyst-ceo-workbook.xlsx), dated and with all three names on it. **Success test:** at least four numbers differ from §11's draft, and somebody argued for at least ten minutes about block D. If nobody argued, you ratified rather than planned.

**12.2 — Trace the chain, and delete something (45 min, alone, before 12.1).** Take every objective and every key result you currently have — from the draft in §11, or from whatever informal list exists — and run §3.3's orphan test on each one. Out loud, finish the sentence _"we are doing this because…"_ with the **specific diagnosed constraint** from [[05-strategy-i-diagnosis-and-the-strategy-kernel|lesson 05]] §2.3. Mark each one **traced**, **weak** (needed more than one "and") or **orphan**. Then give every orphan one of the three fates — promoted, demoted to the floor with an hours budget, or killed — and decide it now rather than deferring it. **Artefact:** an annotated one-page chain in the vault, plus **at least one deletion logged in [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]]** with its reason and a review date. **Success test:** you deleted something you liked. If everything traced cleanly on the first pass, you were grading your own homework — hand the list to the domain-expert director and have him ask the "because" question instead.

**12.3 — The zero-based hour, run for real (60 min prep + 60 min in the room + a 90-min cancellation block within seven days).** Prep exactly as §7.3 specifies: twelve months of bank and card lines grouped by payee, **annual totals only**, sorted descending, plus the four non-cash registers from §7.2 — standing meetings, recurring reports, product surfaces with no use in 90 days, and pursuits with no written next step in two quarters. Read the floor out at minute zero (§7.5). Then run the verdicts, **default CUT**, 60 seconds a line, KEEP only if somebody says the sentence unprompted. **Artefact:** a `recurring-costs.md` register in the vault with a verdict, an owner and a date against every line; a booked cancellation block; and a reminder on next month's statement date to verify the charges are **absent**. **Success test:** the register records the totals — lines reviewed, lines cut, annual rupees released, annual hours released — because next March those four numbers are the comparison that tells you whether the review is still real (§7.6).

**12.4 — Write the persist-or-pivot review date and its evidence criteria (30 min alone, then 15 min with the other two).** Fill block F using §8.2's shape: for each belief, the **falsifying observation** (an observation, not a feeling — checkable by someone who was not in the room), the **minimum sample** before it counts, and the **date**. Name the §8.2 register row each belief comes from, in brackets, as block F already does — and note that **A1, A2 and A3 belong to [[05-strategy-i-diagnosis-and-the-strategy-kernel|lesson 05]] §7.5**: you may sharpen their falsifying observation and their sample, never their wording or their number. If the session wants to change what one of those three _means_, that is a kernel edit in [[C04-strategy-kernel-one-pager|C04]], not a planning edit here. Then, for each one, write the single word in §8.6's vocabulary for what its failure would imply — _segment_, _problem_, _model_, _channel_, or _not-a-pivot_. Put the annual verdict date in all three calendars, marked immovable, and write into [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] the sentence that binds you: **"At this review, PERSIST must be argued for; it is not the default."** **Artefact:** block F of C28 filled, three calendar entries, one C29 entry. **Success test:** you can hand block F to someone outside the company and they could tell you, from the numbers alone, whether the bet had failed — without asking you how you feel about it.

**12.5 — The allocation audit and the exchange rate (45 min, with the bank statement and the calendar open, before 12.1).** Run §1.4 for the quarter just ended. Bucket **last quarter's discretionary spend** into §11 block E's five categories and sort descending; then reconstruct **last month's hours** for all three directors into the same buckets, honestly, including transit. Put the two lists next to the strategy kernel's guiding policy and write **one sentence** naming the largest gap between what the page says and what the money and hours did. Then compute §6.4's exchange rate for the one piece of help you are most tempted to buy: fully-loaded cost ÷ founder-hours it would actually remove, _including_ the hours of specifying, reviewing and correcting. **Artefact:** the two sorted lists, the gap sentence, and **one reallocation decided and logged in C29 with the "instead of" line filled in.** **Success test:** the top spending category and the top hours category are not the same as the top line of the strategy — which is the normal result, and the reason this exercise exists (§1.2).

**12.6 — Optional, at the next March session: the calibration review (20 min).** Put every KR's four quarterly confidence readings side by side with what actually happened (§5.3), and answer one question: **were we well calibrated?** If confidence consistently ran above outcomes, the room is optimistic and should discount its own numbers next year; if consistently below, it is anxious and is under-committing. Then do the same for §8.4's diagnostic 1: count, from [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]], every date on which somebody said something was about a quarter away. **Artefact:** a one-page calibration note in the vault, carried into the next annual session as a standing agenda line. **Success test:** you write down a correction factor and apply it to next year's targets — because a forecasting instrument you never adjust is not an instrument, it is a habit.
