# 11 — Culture and Values as a CEO Instrument

_Phase 3 · The Inward Job · Months 2–9. After this lesson you can define culture in a form you can actually operate — the behaviours a company rewards, tolerates and punishes — and audit which ones VSYST rewards today by accident, name the two cultures this company inherits by default (a technical founder's and a family firm's) and predict what each becomes at thirty people, write values that constrain by naming what they forbid and what they cost instead of values nothing could violate, convert every value into at least one hiring question, one review criterion, one ritual, one public reward and one firing rule — plus the counter-signal that would prove it fake, propose and then argue a first five-value set for VSYST to a board minute, run the five rituals only a CEO can run, anticipate the predictable culture failures at 10, 30 and 100 people, put written ground rules under a company where two people at the standup are related, surface dissent in an Indian workplace where disagreeing upward is expensive, and read culture from six honest signals without owning a survey tool._

## Explain-it-like-I'm-5

Imagine a mithai shop on a lane in Raipur. The board outside says **shuddh desi ghee**. The owner says it to every customer who walks in, and he means it.

Now it is four in the morning, three days before Diwali. Two hundred kilos of laddoo have to be on the counter by eight. The youngest helper opens the last tin of ghee and finds it half empty. On the shelf behind him sits a fifteen-kilo tin of vanaspati. The owner is asleep upstairs.

What that boy does in the next ninety seconds is the shop's culture. Not the board outside. Not what the owner tells customers. What the boy does when nobody is watching and the clock is against him.

And what he does was decided long before this morning. It was decided last Diwali, when somebody did reach for the vanaspati — and the owner either threw the whole batch away in front of everyone and lost a day's takings, or shouted for two minutes and sold it anyway, or noticed nothing at all. Whichever of those three things happened is now the rule of the shop, and every helper knows it even though nobody ever wrote it down. Your job as CEO is to be the owner who threw the batch away: once, early, visibly, at real cost. That is the whole lesson. Everything below is detail.

## 1. Culture, defined so that you can use it

Most writing on culture fails at the first step, because it defines culture as a feeling. A feeling cannot be designed, audited or handed to a new hire. So start with definitions you can operate.

| Definition | Whose | What it gives you | What it costs you | Fit at VSYST today |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| "The way things get done around here" | Deal & Kennedy, via [Organizational culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organizational_culture) | Memorable; points at behaviour, not posters | Describes; does not tell you how to change anything | Useful as a one-liner for a new hire, useless as a tool |
| Three levels: **artifacts** (visible things), **espoused values** (what you say), **basic underlying assumptions** ("the deeply embedded, taken-for-granted behaviours which are usually unconscious") | Edgar Schein ([Edgar Schein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_Schein); _Organizational Culture and Leadership_) | Explains the gap between the wall poster and the 4 AM decision — the gap _is_ level 2 versus level 3 | Diagnostic, not prescriptive; you can classify without fixing | The single most useful diagnostic frame in this lesson. Use it to ask: which of our values are espoused only? |
| Culture is **a set of actions** — "who you are is what you do" — designed or inherited, never absent | Ben Horowitz, _What You Do Is Who You Are_ ([a16z](https://a16z.com/books/what-you-do-is-who-you-are/)) | Makes culture a CEO's to-do list rather than a mood | Understates how much culture is set by systems, not by heroic acts | The governing frame for this lesson |
| Eight styles on two axes — how people interact (independence ↔ interdependence) and how they respond to change (flexibility ↔ stability) | Groysberg, Lee, Price & Cheng, [The Leader's Guide to Corporate Culture](https://hbr.org/2018/01/the-culture-factor), HBR Jan–Feb 2018 | Lets you name the culture you have versus the one the strategy needs | Needs a survey instrument you do not own | Not yet. Trigger: ~25 people, or the first time you and the COO disagree about "what kind of company this is" |

Now the working definition, the one to write on the first page of [[C21-values-and-operating-principles-worksheet|C21]]:

> **Culture is what people do when the CEO is not in the room. It is the running sum of the behaviours a company rewards, the behaviours it tolerates, and the behaviours it punishes — with tolerance doing most of the work.**

Three things follow, and they are the reason this is a CEO lesson and not an HR one.

**Tolerance is the loudest of the three.** Rewards are occasional and visible. Punishments are rare and remembered. Tolerance happens forty times a week and is invisible, which is exactly why it sets the baseline. Horowitz's line is the operational version: "if you see something off-culture and ignore it, you've created a new culture" ([a16z](https://a16z.com/books/what-you-do-is-who-you-are/)). Walking past a thing _is_ the decision, and it is a louder decision than the values page you wrote in January.

**Culture is not the handbook.** The [[COO-Docs/index|COO course]] owns the handbook — code of conduct, POSH, leave, IT and acceptable use, grievance and disciplinary procedure, the twelve-row policy table in [[T27-policy-handbook-toc|T27]] and the first six policies that ship before the first hire ([[09-people-operations|COO 09]] §7). Those are the floor: written rules that make behaviour legally defensible and consistent. Culture is the ceiling: what people do in the enormous space the policies do not cover, which at a three-person company is roughly 98% of the working day. Do not let the arrival of a handbook convince you that culture has been handled.

**Culture is not a morale programme.** Netflix's memo says it bluntly: a great workplace "is not espresso, lush benefits, sushi lunches, grand parties, or nice offices" ([Culture: Freedom & Responsibility](https://www.slideshare.net/reed2001/culture-1798664), the 125-slide deck viewed over 17 million times). At VSYST there is no espresso budget to confuse anyone, which is a small mercy.

### 1.1 Why "values on a wall" fails, in one paragraph and one example

Patrick Lencioni opened his 2002 HBR essay with four words — "Communication. Respect. Integrity. Excellence." — and then noted whose values they were: Enron's ([Make Your Values Mean Something](https://hbr.org/2002/07/make-your-values-mean-something), HBR, July 2002). The list is not wrong. It is _unfalsifiable_. No employee at any company has ever been fired for insufficient Excellence, because nobody can say what would count as a violation. Lencioni's diagnosis is that most companies mix four different things and call them all values: the two or three **core** values that are genuinely non-negotiable, the **aspirational** ones the company wishes it had, the **permission-to-play** ones every company needs to not be sued (integrity, honesty), and the **accidental** ones that seeped in from whoever happened to get hired first. Publishing them as one undifferentiated list is what makes the list decoration.

The test that survives from that essay, and the one this lesson runs on:

> **A value that nothing could violate is not a value. It is a mood.**

### 1.2 The three-question audit you can run this week

Before writing a single new value, audit the ones you already have — because you already have them, whether or not anyone wrote them down.

1. **What behaviour got rewarded here in the last 90 days?** Not praised — _rewarded_. Who got the interesting work, the credit in front of the other directors, the benefit of the doubt, the exception to a rule.
2. **What behaviour got tolerated?** List the things that happened that should not have, and were not addressed. Missed dealer callbacks. A commit straight to main. A decision reversed at dinner. Somebody's number that turned out to be a guess.
3. **What behaviour got punished — or just made expensive?** Including the silent punishments: a person raised an objection and the meeting got cold; someone brought bad news and the next twenty minutes were unpleasant.

Those three lists **are** your current values, stated honestly. Exercise 12.1 turns them into an artefact. Everything after that is the difference between the culture you have and the culture you would choose — and closing that gap is the CEO's job because nobody else in the company has the authority to change what gets rewarded.

## 2. Why the CEO cannot opt out

At three people there is no culture "programme", no head of people, and no distance between the founders and the work. That does not mean there is no culture. It means the culture is a **direct cast of the founders' habits, including the bad ones**, taken at high resolution, and it will be reproduced by every person you hire for the next five years because your first hires copy your behaviour, not your instructions ([[startup-operations-plan|Startup Operations Plan]]; [[09-people-operations|COO 09]] preamble makes the same point).

Two consequences the CEO of a three-person company usually gets wrong.

**The window is now, and it is short.** Culture is cheap to set and expensive to change. Zappos discovered the price of a late reset: when it moved to holacracy and offered severance to anyone who did not want to adopt it, "about 14% of the company left voluntarily in 2015", and by January 2016 roughly **18%** of the workforce had taken the buyout ([Holacracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holacracy)). Basecamp discovered the price of announcing a change without a process: six new policies published in April 2021 — no societal or political discussion on the company account, no paternalistic benefits, no committees, no lingering on past decisions, no 360 reviews — triggered a wave of resignations and a public argument the company is still known for ([Changes at Basecamp](https://world.hey.com/jason/changes-at-basecamp-7f32afc5), April 2021). Neither company was careless. Both were paying, late, for something a three-person company can set for free.

**Every hire is a culture decision before it is a skill decision.** At three people, hire number four moves the culture by 25%. At thirty people, hire number thirty-one moves it by 3%. The leverage is now, running in the direction you do not want: hiring in your own image is fast and comfortable, and it is how a founding team's blind spots become a company's blind spots. [[10-building-the-team-hiring-equity-and-firing|Lesson 10]] owns the hiring machinery; this lesson owns the question of what the machinery is selecting _for_.

### 2.1 VSYST's specific inheritance — two cultures, arriving whether you choose them or not

VSYST does not inherit a generic startup culture. It inherits two specific ones, both with real strengths, and both with well-documented pathologies at scale. Naming them honestly is the point of this section; a lesson that flattered the founders here would be worthless.

| Inheritance | Where it comes from | What it produces that is genuinely good | What it becomes at 30 people if unexamined | The tell, visible today |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Build first, ship** | A technical CEO who writes the code | Extraordinary speed; a working product on both app stores before revenue; nothing waits for a committee | **A company that ships what is interesting to build instead of what a dealer will pay for.** Roadmap by curiosity. Features nobody asked for, polished; features every dealer asked for, deferred | Any week where the commit log is full and the visit log is empty |
| **Argue on merit** | Engineering culture; the best argument wins | Fast, honest technical decisions; low politics; ideas are not owned by ranks | **Merit becomes a licence for a style.** The most fluent arguer wins, not the most correct one. Quiet people and non-engineers stop contributing. New hires learn that being right loudly is the promotion path | Whether the domain-expert director's fuel-distribution objections get the same airtime as a technical objection |
| **Avoid customers** | Building is legible and safe; a dealer visit is neither | Deep focus; real engineering hours | **Ten people, none of whom have met a dealer.** Support gets treated as an interruption. The product's model of the customer drifts from the customer | Count the founders' dealer-facing hours last month against build hours ([[07-customers-markets-and-founder-led-sales\|lesson 07]] §1) |
| **Loyalty over performance** | A family firm; people who have been trusted for decades | Trust that a hired team takes years to build; tolerance for lean years; nobody is job-hunting | **Nobody is ever removed.** A wrong seat becomes permanent because moving the person is a family event. Non-family employees learn that performance is not the currency, and the good ones leave first | The first time a performance conversation is postponed because of who the person is |
| **Indirect conflict** | Family norms: disagreement is expensive in a relationship you cannot leave | Surface harmony; few blow-ups; things stay civil | **Decisions get made in corridors and unmade at dinner.** Issues get raised with third parties instead of principals. Lencioni's first two dysfunctions — absence of trust, then "the desire to preserve artificial harmony" ([The Table Group](https://www.tablegroup.com/product/dysfunctions/)) | Whether any disagreement in the last month was raised directly, resolved, and written down ([[04-how-to-become-a-ceo-in-2026\|lesson 04]] §4, criterion 13) |
| **Hierarchy by age** | Indian family and workplace norms both | Respect; a genuine deference to thirty years of domain experience that is often correct | **Nobody younger contradicts anybody older, ever** — which at 30 people means the people closest to the customer and the code are structurally silent. Amplified by a national baseline: high power distance means "clearly established hierarchies" and less questioning of authority ([Hofstede's cultural dimensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hofstede%27s_cultural_dimensions_theory)) | The order in which people speak in your meetings. Watch it once this week |

Read the two halves together and the compound risk is visible: **the technical culture supplies the confidence, and the family culture supplies the silence.** A company that is confident and silent is a company that will be confidently wrong for a long time, which is exactly the false-consensus risk [[02-how-a-ceo-thinks|lesson 02]] §9 names as VSYST's largest cognitive exposure. Culture is where that risk is either mechanised away or baked in.

Two honest notes. First, both inheritances are also assets — trust and speed are not consolation prizes, they are the reason a three-person bootstrapped company can compete at all. The job is not to remove the inheritance; it is to keep the strength and mechanise against the pathology. Second, none of this is a VSYST peculiarity. India's family enterprises are overwhelmingly family-led — Deloitte's 2025 survey found **96% of Indian family enterprises are still led by family members and 51% are second-generation** ([Deloitte India](https://www.deloitte.com/in/en/about/press-room/india-s-family-enterprises-drive-economic-power.html), Dec 2025) — and PwC's family business survey found **52% of Indian family businesses cite resistance from the senior generation** as the biggest barrier to next-generation leadership, against 29% globally ([Forbes India on PwC's 12th Family Business Survey](https://www.forbesindia.com/article/news/indian-family-businesses-high-on-ambition-low-on-governance/2991239/1), Feb 2026). You are inside the norm. The norm has a known failure mode.

## 3. Writing values that constrain

A value is a constraint on behaviour that you accept in advance, before you know what it will cost. If it never costs anything, it is not doing any work.

### 3.1 The three tests

Every candidate value must pass all three, in writing, before it goes anywhere near a wall:

1. **The forbidden-behaviour test.** Name at least one specific, plausible, *tempting* behaviour this value rules out. If you cannot, delete the value.
2. **The trade-off test.** Name what you are giving up. Every real value trades against something else you also want — speed, revenue, harmony, a hire you liked. Write the trade in one sentence: _"We accept ___ in order to protect ___."_
3. **The costly-case test.** Describe one concrete situation in the next twelve months where honouring this value will cost money, a customer, a deal or a person. If no such situation is imaginable, the value is permission-to-play, not core.

A fourth test, borrowed from Schein, catches the most common failure: **the assumption test.** Ask whether the value describes a basic underlying assumption you already act on, or a wish. Both are allowed — but label them. A wish published as a fact is how a team learns that the values page is fiction ([Edgar Schein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_Schein)).

### 3.2 Three generic values, sharpened

| Generic (fails all three tests) | Sharpened (passes) | The behaviour it forbids | The trade-off it accepts |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **"Customer first"** | **"We do not ship a change to a dealer's live data without a named dealer who asked for it and a named dealer who tested it."** | Building from an internal hunch; a "small" schema change reasoned about in the office; using production as the test environment | Slower shipping. Some genuinely good ideas die because no dealer will spend twenty minutes testing them |
| **"Integrity"** | **"We say the number. If we do not have the number, we say we do not have it — to each other, to a dealer, to IOCL, and in every deck."** | Rounding a pilot count up in a partner meeting; "good traction" as an answer; a demo showing seeded data without saying so | Some conversations get harder and some meetings end worse. Partners occasionally hear a smaller number than a competitor gives them |
| **"Move fast"** | **"Ship to one district before you demo to a hall. A feature is done when a dealer used it unaided on a Tuesday, not when it merged."** | Conference-driven roadmaps; launch announcements ahead of adoption; calling a merged PR "delivered" | You look smaller than you are for longer. Some marketing moments are missed on purpose |

Notice what changed. The sharpened versions are longer, uglier, and specific enough to lose an argument with. That is the entire difference.

### 3.3 Real values with teeth — four companies, examined

**Netflix — the sharpest and the most misquoted.** Netflix's memo defines the company as a "dream team" modelled on a professional sports team rather than a family: teams "focus on performance and picking the right person for every position, even when that means swapping out someone they love for a better player" ([jobs.netflix.com/culture](https://jobs.netflix.com/culture)). Its enforcing mechanism is the **keeper test** — "if X wanted to leave, would I fight to keep them?", also framed as "knowing everything I know today, would I hire X again?" The original deck stated the cost plainly: "adequate performance gets a generous severance package" ([the deck](https://www.slideshare.net/reed2001/culture-1798664)). Patty McCord's account is the readable version: "Trust people, not policies. Reward candor. And throw away the standard playbook" ([How Netflix Reinvented HR](https://hbr.org/2014/01/how-netflix-reinvented-hr), HBR, Jan–Feb 2014). Netflix's stated values — selflessness, judgment, candor, creativity, courage, inclusion, curiosity, resilience — are not the instrument. **The keeper test is the instrument**, and it is a mechanism, not a sentiment.

The critique, which matters more for VSYST than the praise: a keeper test run by a founder-CEO with no board, no HR function and family on the team is not a talent-density mechanism. It is an unappealable dismissal power with a slogan attached. Netflix pairs it with very high pay, real severance, and enough scale that one manager's judgement is checked by others. VSYST has none of those. **Do not import the keeper test. Import the honesty about cost.**

**Amazon — the most operational, and the most weaponisable.** Amazon's sixteen Leadership Principles work as an instrument because they are written as *behaviours in tension*, not virtues. "Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit" reads: "Leaders are obligated to respectfully challenge decisions when they disagree, even when doing so is uncomfortable or exhausting" ([Amazon Leadership Principles](https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles)). That is a forbidden behaviour — silent acquiescence — stated as an obligation. "Frugality", "Dive Deep", "Insist on the Highest Standards" and "Earn Trust" carry the same shape, and they are used verbatim in interview loops, performance narratives and promotion documents, which is what makes them real rather than decorative.

And the serious critique: the same principles, embedded in a performance system, produced what the _New York Times_ described in August 2015 as a bruising white-collar workplace — a peer-feedback tool usable to undermine colleagues, employees describing being managed out after illness or bereavement ([Inside Amazon: Wrestling Big Ideas in a Bruising Workplace](https://www.nytimes.com/2015/08/16/technology/inside-amazon-wrestling-big-ideas-in-a-bruising-workplace.html), Kantor & Streitfeld, 15 Aug 2015). Amazon disputed the characterisation. Two lessons survive the dispute: **a value written as an obligation to challenge can be used as an obligation to attack**, and **a value plugged into a performance system with a forced-exit target stops being a value and becomes a lever.** If you adopt Amazon-style behavioural principles — and §5 does — adopt them without the forced-distribution machinery, and write down that you have.

**Stripe — values as an operating manual.** Stripe publishes six operating principles: **Users first**, **Create with craft and beauty**, **Move with urgency and focus**, **Collaborate egolessly**, **Stay curious**, **Obsess over talent** ([Stripe — culture](https://stripe.com/en-in/jobs/culture)). Two are worth stealing outright. "Collaborate egolessly" is defined operationally — no information silos, open debate, credit on merit rather than hierarchy — which is a direct answer to the hierarchy-by-age inheritance in §2.1. "Obsess over talent" makes hiring quality *everyone's* job, the only version that survives a company's fourth hire. Stripe's framing also names the trap it is designed against: "Our goal is to get faster as Stripe grows" ([stripe.com/jobs](https://stripe.com/jobs)) — an admission that companies get slower by default.

**Zoho — the Indian company whose values cost it money, on the record.** Zoho states two commitments a normal SaaS company would not survive: it has "never taken money from investors", and "from the beginning, we decided we would never show ads inside our products (not even in the free editions), nor would we sell user information to third parties" ([Zoho — About us](https://www.zoho.com/aboutus.html)). Both are values in the strict sense of §3.1 — each names a forbidden behaviour and each has a documented price. Sridhar Vembu's hiring position has the same shape: Zoho Schools, founded 2004, trains people without degrees, and 15–20% of Zoho's engineers have no college degree ([Sridhar Vembu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sridhar_Vembu)). Zoho also moved operations to rural Tenkasi and Renigunta rather than a metro — directly relevant to a company headquartered in Raipur, as proof that a location others call a disadvantage can be made into a stated position.

### 3.4 An Indian values set, examined critically — Zerodha, and the counter-example

Zerodha publishes the most operationally honest values statement of any Indian company at scale, and it is worth reading as a template rather than as inspiration. Four claims from its philosophy page:

- "Customer interest over growth, transparency, and our product development philosophies — are the reasons why millions of customers have come to trust us."
- "As a for-profit company, we have no external investor pressure, thanks to us being bootstrapped and profitable from our early days."
- "Inside Zerodha, employees do not have perverse incentives as we do not impose any metric-based growth targets; be it accounts opened, app install counts, orders placed, or revenue generated."
- "Central to our philosophies are: not advertising, spamming, pushing, or inducing customers to take actions that are not in their interest."

([Zerodha — philosophy](https://zerodha.com/about/philosophy/); the company also caps non-critical push notifications at four a month.)

Apply the three tests. Forbidden behaviour: advertising, growth targets, engagement nudges — all named, all specific. Trade-off: slower growth, no paid acquisition, no sales-incentive flywheel. Costly case: every quarter, permanently. It passes.

Now the critical read, because a course that only prints admirable quotes is doing marketing. **These values are downstream of a business model that can afford them.** Zerodha is profitable, bootstrapped, and sells a commodity at an obviously cheaper price, so word-of-mouth works. A pre-revenue company that copies "no metric-based growth targets" without Zerodha's profitability is not being principled; it is avoiding accountability and calling it a value. The transferable part is the *form* — values stated as prohibitions on the company's own behaviour, with the cost visible. The non-transferable part is the specific prohibitions.

And the counter-example, which teaches more. BYJU'S built India's most valuable edtech company on a stated mission about learning outcomes while running a sales system that former salespeople described as pushing products on families who could not afford them, financed by upfront loans from partner lenders. In June 2022 the Department of Consumer Affairs raised concerns about "aggressive sales practices and deceptive marketing strategies"; its WhiteHat Jr subsidiary was made to withdraw advertisements the ASCI found misleading, including a fabricated testimonial; over 5,000 employees were dismissed in November 2022, with workers reporting they were "treated as robots" ([BYJU'S](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byju%27s)). Nothing in the stated mission caused this. **The incentive system caused it, and the stated mission provided cover.** That is the precise failure mode §4 exists to prevent: when a value and a compensation scheme disagree, the compensation scheme wins every time, and the value becomes the thing people cite while doing the opposite.

One more Indian data point, on the largest cultural question an Indian CEO gets asked publicly. In late 2023 N. R. Narayana Murthy said young Indians should work 70 hours a week and "rejected the notion of work–life balance", refusing to retract; in November 2024 he clarified it was "meant to be a matter of introspection and not debate" and should not be mandated ([N. R. Narayana Murthy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N._R._Narayana_Murthy)). In January 2025 L&T's chairman S. N. Subrahmanyan endorsed a 90-hour week, asking employees "what do you do sitting at home"; L&T defended it as "a call for collective dedication", and Anand Mahindra, Harsh Goenka and Gautam Adani criticised it publicly ([S. N. Subrahmanyan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S._N._Subrahmanyan)). Both controversies drew their force from context: the July 2024 death of 26-year-old chartered accountant Anna Sebastian Perayil, four months into a job at EY's Pune office, which her family attributed to "stress caused by overwork, presenteeism and exhaustion" and which the Union Minister of State for Labour took up as a formal complaint ([Anna Sebastian Perayil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Sebastian_Perayil)).

The lesson for a founder-CEO is not about the hours. It is this: **a CEO's public statement about effort is read by employees as a policy, not as an opinion.** If you praise the person who answered a dealer's WhatsApp at 11:40 PM, you have set a norm about 11:40 PM, whatever you intended.

## 4. From values to mechanisms — the core of this lesson

Here is the rule that makes the rest of the course's rule ("mechanisms, not intentions") specific to culture:

> **A value with no mechanism is a preference. A value becomes real at the first moment it costs the CEO something in public.**

A mechanism is a repeatable thing that runs whether or not anyone feels like it. For culture, there are six slots, and a value needs to occupy at least three of them to be real:

| Slot | What it is | Why it works | Where it lives at VSYST |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Selection** | A question in every interview, scored, with a pass bar | It is the cheapest filter you own and it compounds — every hire either raises or lowers the baseline | Competency rows in [[T10-hiring-scorecard-and-process\|COO T10]]; exec loop in [[C19-executive-hiring-scorecard-and-loop\|C19]] |
| **Evaluation** | A named criterion in the quarterly review, written before the person is evaluated | Turns a value into something a person's confirmation and pay depend on | The light quarterly review in [[09-people-operations\|COO 09]] §9 |
| **Ritual** | A recurring meeting slot or artefact where the behaviour is performed | Repetition is what turns behaviour into assumption (Schein level 3) | §6 below; cadence in [[08-the-operating-cadence\|COO 08]] |
| **Public reward** | Recognition that names the behaviour, not the outcome | People copy what visibly succeeds. Naming the behaviour is what makes it copyable | Recognition slot in [[T24-all-hands-agenda\|COO T24]]; [[C22-internal-comms-calendar-and-all-hands\|C22]] |
| **Consequence** | A written line on what a violation costs, up to and including exit | Without this, the value is advisory. This is the slot founders skip | Code of conduct in [[T27-policy-handbook-toc\|COO T27]]; firing in [[10-building-the-team-hiring-equity-and-firing\|lesson 10]] |
| **Allocation** | Where the money and the CEO's calendar actually go | The budget is the values statement nobody edits for effect | [[C27-ceo-weekly-template-and-calendar-audit\|C27]] calendar audit; [[19-planning-okrs-and-capital-allocation\|lesson 19]] |

### 4.1 The table that does the work

For each value: the behaviour it demands, the mechanism that enforces it, and — the column most companies never write — **the counter-signal**: the specific observable thing that would prove to any employee that the value is fake. Write the counter-signal column first if you want an honest table.

| Value | The behaviour it demands | The mechanism that enforces it | The counter-signal that would prove it fake |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Proximity — the district is the classroom** | Every person who builds, sells or supports has been in a dealer's office in the last 90 days | **Hiring question:** "Tell me about a time you changed your mind because of something a customer said in person." **Ritual:** every meeting opens with 90 seconds on one named dealer. **Allocation:** a district-travel line in the budget that is never the first thing cut | A quarter passes in which no founder visited a dealer, and nobody notices. Or: travel is cut in a tight month while a tooling subscription survives |
| **Say the number** | No adjective substitutes for a figure; "I don't know" is an acceptable answer with an owner and a date | **Review criterion:** "brings numbers, flags the ones that are estimates." **Ritual:** the scorecard shown at the all-hands is the unedited sheet the directors read on Monday ([[T05-kpi-scorecard\|COO T05]], [[T24-all-hands-agenda\|COO T24]]). **Consequence:** a knowingly inflated number in an external deck is a documented, escalating matter | A founder inflates a pilot count in front of a partner and the other founders say nothing in the car afterwards. Or: a number is quietly corrected between two decks with no note |
| **Write it down** | Decisions exist when they are in the vault with a decider and a date; positions are written before the meeting | **Ritual:** written pre-reads; decision log entries within 24 hours ([[T09-decision-log-and-adr\|COO T09]], [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log\|C29]]). **Selection:** a written work-sample in every hiring loop. **Public reward:** recognition for "wrote the SOP so the next person doesn't have to ask" | A decision taken at dinner is executed on Monday with no entry. Or: the person who writes things down is the person who is behind on "real work" |
| **The dealer's ledger is sacred** | Production data is touched only under a named, logged, time-boxed reason; no screenshots of a dealer's balances in WhatsApp, ever | **Consequence:** a stated, non-negotiable exit-level breach, written in the code of conduct. **Ritual:** access review each quarter ([[07-tools-and-it-foundation\|COO 07]]). **Selection:** a scenario question in every technical interview | A production query is run to answer a curiosity and it is a funny story rather than an incident. Or: an exception is made "because it's faster" during a crunch |
| **Disagree here, not after** | Objections are raised to the person, in the room, and then the decision is supported | **Ritual:** the CEO speaks last; a rotating designated dissenter; positions written independently before discussion ([[02-how-a-ceo-thinks\|lesson 02]] §9). **Review criterion:** "raised a disagreement directly this quarter" is a positive line, not a risk flag | A director hears about an objection from a third person. Or: someone who disagreed in a meeting is teased about it afterwards, even affectionately |

Three notes on using this table.

**The counter-signal column is a monitoring instrument, not a rhetorical device.** Once written, each counter-signal is something you can look for. Put the five of them on one page and read it once a quarter — §10 makes this a fifteen-minute habit. A culture does not fail by announcement; it fails by a counter-signal occurring and nobody flinching.

**Mechanisms beat exhortation because they survive your mood.** You will have weeks where you are tired, a payment has bounced, and a dealer has churned. In those weeks nothing that depends on your energy happens. The written pre-read still happens. The scorecard still gets published. That is the whole argument for mechanisms.

**One deliberate, costly, visible act is worth ten mechanisms at the start.** This is Horowitz's practical point and it is the mithai-shop paragraph in business language: the culture-defining events at a small company are the moments when a value cost something and everyone saw it ([a16z](https://a16z.com/books/what-you-do-is-who-you-are/)). Refusing a deal because the dealer wanted his competitor's data. Rebuilding a ledger month by hand rather than showing a number you could not defend. Telling IOCL a smaller pilot number than you wished you had. **You get roughly one of these a quarter. Do not waste them, and do not let them pass unnamed** — an expensive right decision that nobody hears about teaches nobody anything.

## 5. A first values set for VSYST — a draft to argue with

**This is a draft. It is not adopted, and it should not be adopted as written.** Its purpose is to give the three directors something specific to disagree with, because a values discussion that starts from a blank page produces "Integrity. Excellence. Customer focus." within twenty minutes. Each of the five below is grounded in something already true about this company — not aspirational, not borrowed. Four to six is the right count: fewer than four and it says nothing; more than six and nobody can recite them, which means they cannot be used at 4 AM.

### 5.1 The five candidates

**V1 — The district is the classroom.**
_What it means:_ decisions about the product are made from a real dealer's real day, seen in person, in his district. The person who builds a thing has watched somebody use it badly.
_It forbids:_ building from a feature request in a WhatsApp message without having seen the ledger behind it; deciding what dealers want in a room in which no dealer has ever sat; treating support as an interruption to real work.
_It costs:_ travel money and founder-days that could have been build-days; slower shipping; some good ideas die because no dealer will spend twenty minutes on them.
_Grounded in:_ the entire method of [[07-customers-markets-and-founder-led-sales|lesson 07]] and the fact that the domain-expert director's knowledge came from being physically inside fuel distribution for a working lifetime.
_Mechanism:_ every meeting opens with 90 seconds on one named dealer; a monthly minimum of dealer-facing hours per founder, published; a hiring question about changing your mind because of a customer.

**V2 — Say the number.**
_What it means:_ we speak in figures, we mark estimates as estimates, and "I don't know" is a complete and respectable answer when it carries an owner and a date.
_It forbids:_ "good traction", "growing well", "a few dealers" in any internal or external sentence; presenting seeded demo data without saying it is seeded; rounding up for a partner meeting.
_It costs:_ some conversations end worse. In an IOCL room or a fundraising room, an honest small number sits next to a competitor's flattering large one.
_Grounded in:_ a company whose product is a **ledger**. If we are casual about our own numbers, we have no standing to be trusted with a dealer's.
_Mechanism:_ the all-hands scorecard is the unedited directors' sheet; a review criterion; a written rule that an inflated external number is an escalating, documented matter.

**V3 — If it isn't written, it didn't happen.**
_What it means:_ a decision exists when it is in the vault with a decider, a date and the reasoning. Positions are written before the discussion, not after.
_It forbids:_ decisions reached at dinner and executed on Monday with no record; "we discussed this already" as an argument; knowledge that lives only in the founder's head.
_It costs:_ real hours, and — the honest risk, named in [[02-how-a-ceo-thinks|lesson 02]] §7 — it quietly advantages the best writer, which in this company is the CEO. The mitigation is part of the value, not an afterthought: **positions may be written in any form and any language** — five bullets in Hindi in a WhatsApp note counts — and the CEO writes first and shares last.
_Grounded in:_ the vault already exists. Quartz, Obsidian, a company that documents itself. This value is not a new habit; it is a promise to keep an existing one under pressure.
_Mechanism:_ decision log entries within 24 hours ([[T09-decision-log-and-adr|COO T09]]); a written work-sample in every hiring loop; recognition that names writing.

**V4 — A dealer's ledger is sacred.**
_What it means:_ tenant data is touched only for a named reason, by a named person, for a bounded time, and it never leaves the systems it lives in.
_It forbids:_ production queries to satisfy curiosity; screenshots of a dealer's outstandings pasted into WhatsApp to make a point; "just this once" access during a crunch; using one dealer's data to help another, even in aggregate, without saying so.
_It costs:_ engineering time, slower debugging, and occasionally a slower answer to a dealer who wants one now.
_Grounded in:_ the product's actual promise. A dealer's receivables position is the most sensitive number in his business; the [[07-customers-markets-and-founder-led-sales|lesson 07]] research puts a mid-sized dealer's outstanding exposure around ₹57 lakh at any moment. Also grounded in law — DPDP obligations and CERT-In incident reporting are live or arriving ([[13-compliance-calendar-risk-and-insurance|COO 13]]; **VERIFY LIVE** with a lawyer before writing any specific date or duty into the code of conduct) — and in [[certification-and-standards-roadmap|the certification roadmap]], where ISO 27001 becomes a hard requirement the moment a GSP agreement is signed.
_Mechanism:_ an exit-level clause in the code of conduct; quarterly access review; a scenario question in every technical interview.

**V5 — Ship to a district before you demo to a hall.**
_What it means:_ a feature is done when a dealer used it unaided on an ordinary Tuesday. Adoption in one district beats a launch anywhere.
_It forbids:_ conference-driven roadmaps; announcing before adoption; counting a merged pull request as delivery; building for the demo path.
_It costs:_ you look smaller than you are for longer; some marketing moments are skipped deliberately; a partner occasionally sees a narrower product than a slide could have shown.
_Grounded in:_ the sales-assisted, district-by-district motion the company already runs, and the price anchor problem — a dealer whose every previous piece of software arrived free will not be moved by a launch.
_Mechanism:_ the definition of done in [[12-product-and-engineering-operations|COO 12]]; the no-list in [[C25-product-bets-and-the-no-list|C25]]; a public reward for the person who found the dealer who tested it.

**A sixth, optional, and the hardest — V6: the family gets the harder question.**
_What it means:_ where a decision involves a director's relative, the standard of scrutiny goes **up**, not down, and the reasoning is written.
_It forbids:_ a family member being appointed, paid, promoted or retained on a decision that would not survive being written down; a performance conversation postponed because of who the person is.
_It costs:_ real family friction, and it will feel unfair to the family member on the receiving end. Say so out loud when adopting it.
_Grounded in:_ §8, and in s.166 of the Companies Act 2013, which already forbids a director achieving "undue gain" for themselves or their relatives ([Companies Act 2013, s.166](https://ca2013.com/166-duties-of-directors/)). This value is the cultural expression of a duty that is already legal.
_Mechanism:_ §8.3's eight ground rules; a register of interests; the "would we hire this person from outside?" test, written.

**Decide V6 deliberately.** Publishing it announces the problem to every future employee, which is either honest or alarming depending on how it is written. Not publishing it means the ground rules exist privately — which is still far better than nothing, and is the house call if the founders cannot agree. What is not acceptable is having neither.

### 5.2 Sanity-checking the draft against the three tests

| Value | Forbidden behaviour named? | Trade-off named? | A costly case in the next 12 months? | Type (Lencioni) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| V1 The district is the classroom | Yes — building from an unseen request | Yes — build days lost to travel | Yes: a month where the founders' calendar is full and the visit log is empty | **Core** (differentiating) |
| V2 Say the number | Yes — "good traction" | Yes — worse-sounding external conversations | Yes: the first serious IOCL or investor meeting | **Core**, with a permission-to-play floor |
| V3 If it isn't written, it didn't happen | Yes — decisions at dinner | Yes — hours, and it advantages writers | Yes: the first week you are too busy to write and do it anyway | **Aspirational → core.** Label it aspirational until 90 days of decision-log entries exist |
| V4 The dealer's ledger is sacred | Yes — casual production access | Yes — slower debugging | Yes: the first production incident at 11 PM | **Core** and partly permission-to-play (it is also the law) |

Three of these — V2, V3, V4 — are things VSYST arguably already does. That is a feature: Schein's model says the durable values are the basic assumptions already in operation, and writing down what you already do is how a founding team's implicit habits survive hire number seven ([Edgar Schein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_Schein)). V1 and V5 are closer to aspirational and should be labelled as such for the first two quarters.

### 5.3 The argue-and-adopt process — three weeks, six steps

Do not adopt values in a meeting. Meetings produce the values of whoever speaks best, which is the failure mode §2.1 named.

| Step | When | Who | What happens | Artefact |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Write independently** | Week 1, before any discussion | All three directors, separately | Each writes 3–6 values in the sharp form: what it forbids, what it costs, one costly case. Any language, any format. The CEO writes first and shares **last** | Three private drafts |
| **2. Merge on paper** | Week 1, end | CEO | Put all three drafts in one document, unedited, side by side. Mark overlaps. Do not resolve conflicts yet | One merged sheet in the vault |
| **3. Argue the conflicts only** | Week 2, one 90-minute session | All three | Discuss only where the drafts disagree. For each disagreement, name the decider before the argument starts ([[C03-decision-rights-matrix\|C03]]). The domain-expert director speaks first; the CEO speaks last | Notes; a shortlist |
| **4. The cost test, out loud** | Week 2, same session | All three | For each survivor, each director says one thing this value will personally cost **them**. A value nobody can name a personal cost for is cut on the spot | The cut list, and why |
| **5. Draft the mechanisms** | Week 3 | CEO drafts, COO seat reviews | Each surviving value gets at least three of the six slots in §4 filled, plus its counter-signal. A value with fewer than three mechanisms is cut or downgraded to aspirational | Filled [[C21-values-and-operating-principles-worksheet\|C21]] |
| **6. Adopt and minute** | Week 3, board meeting | Board | Approve the set, the mechanisms, the aspirational labels and the review date. Minute it — this is a board-level act because it constrains hiring, pay and dismissal ([[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties\|lesson 09]], [[C17-board-pack-agenda-and-minutes\|C17]]) | A resolution; the values page in the vault, versioned and dated |

Two rules for the process itself. **Review at twelve months, not before and not never** — set the date in the minute. And **the first violation is the real adoption.** Until a value has been enforced once against somebody's convenience, it has been announced, not adopted; put a line in the review that asks "which value has been enforced at a cost, and when?"

## 6. Rituals — culture's delivery mechanism

Values are delivered by repetition, and repetition happens in rituals. The split matters, because the COO course already owns most of the company's rhythm and this lesson must not build a second one.

| Ritual | Owner | Frequency | What it delivers | Where it is specified |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Daily async check-in | **COO** | Daily | Coordination | [[08-the-operating-cadence\|COO 08]] §3 |
| Weekly ops meeting, scorecard, 1:1s, pipeline review | **COO** | Weekly | Accountability | [[08-the-operating-cadence\|COO 08]] §4–§5, [[T03-weekly-ops-meeting-agenda\|T03]], [[T04-one-on-one-template\|T04]] |
| Monthly business review; the all-hands **slot and agenda** | **COO** | Monthly | Transparency | [[08-the-operating-cadence\|COO 08]] §6, [[T24-all-hands-agenda\|T24]] |
| Quarterly planning day and audits | **COO** | Quarterly | Focus | [[08-the-operating-cadence\|COO 08]] §7 |

| **The customer story that opens every meeting** | **CEO** | Every meeting | Proximity (V1) | §6.2 |
| **The written weekly note** | **CEO** | Weekly, Friday | Consistency of story; a written trail (V3) | §6.3, [[C27-ceo-weekly-template-and-calendar-audit\|C27]] |

**The rule of the split in one line:** the COO owns the *slot*; the CEO owns the *content that only a CEO can supply*. The all-hands is a COO-run 45-minute meeting with a five-minute CEO opening — and the five minutes are not optional decoration, they are the only regular moment in which everyone hears the same version of where the company is going.

### 6.1 The all-hands narrative

Five minutes, three sentences, monthly, in the same shape every time: **where we are going · what changed this month · what that means for the next four weeks.** The discipline is that sentence one should barely change month to month. A direction that changes monthly is not a direction, and the team's read on whether the CEO knows what they are doing is formed almost entirely by whether sentence one is stable ([[12-communication-the-ceo-as-chief-storyteller|lesson 12]] owns the craft; [[C22-internal-comms-calendar-and-all-hands|C22]] owns the template).

Two things belong in the five minutes that founders leave out: **what we decided not to do this month, and why** — a no-list said out loud is the strongest available evidence that a strategy exists ([[C25-product-bets-and-the-no-list|C25]]) — and **one thing that went badly**, stated before anyone asks. A CEO who only opens with good news trains a company to disbelieve the opening.

### 6.2 The customer story

Ninety seconds at the top of every meeting the CEO chairs — ops meeting, all-hands, board — about **one named dealer and what changed for him.** Not a metric. A person, a district, a thing that happened. This is the delivery mechanism for V1 and the only regular contact most future employees will have with the reason the company exists ([[T24-all-hands-agenda|COO T24]] rule 5 already writes it into the all-hands; extend it upward).

### 6.3 The written weekly note

One page, Friday, in the vault, same structure: **what I decided · what I learned from a dealer · what I got wrong · what I need from you.** Twenty-five minutes, and it does four things nothing else does: it makes V3 real by making the CEO the most-writing person rather than the least; it creates a record a future hire can read to learn how this company thinks; it dates the reasoning, which is what defuses "I told you so" in a family founding team ([[02-how-a-ceo-thinks|lesson 02]] §2); and the "what I got wrong" line, published weekly, does more for candour than any policy.

At three people this feels absurd — everyone already knows. Write it anyway and keep the archive. Its value is realised entirely in retrospect, by hire number four.

### 6.4 Celebrate the behaviour, not the outcome

Recognition that names an outcome ("great quarter, ten dealers signed") teaches people to want outcomes, which they already do. Recognition that names a **behaviour** teaches them what to copy: "she drove to Durg on a Saturday because the dealer's OTP flow failed and she wanted to watch it fail in his hands" — that is V1, made copyable.

The harder half, and the one that actually differentiates a culture: **recognise the right behaviour that produced a bad outcome.** A well-run pilot that failed. A deal walked away from on V4 grounds. If only successful behaviour is celebrated you have not built a culture, you have built a scoreboard — and [[02-how-a-ceo-thinks|lesson 02]] §1 already establishes why judging decisions by outcomes is the founding error of judgment.

### 6.5 The public post-mortem, with the CEO's own mistake first

Psychological safety is "the belief that one will not be punished or humiliated for speaking up with ideas, questions, concerns, or mistakes" ([Psychological safety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_safety); Edmondson, _Administrative Science Quarterly_ 44(4), 1999 — [Amy Edmondson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amy_Edmondson)). It is not a mood; it is produced by observed consequences. The cheapest way for a founder-CEO to produce it is to go first.

The mechanism: for every incident that reaches a dealer, run a blameless post-mortem on the COO's template ([[T17-incident-postmortem|COO T17]]), publish it in the vault, and **make the CEO's own contribution to the failure the first item in the document.** Not a ritual apology — a specific line: the decision I made, the thing I deprioritised, the question I did not ask. Once a company has seen a CEO write that down, the cost of a junior engineer reporting a mistake at 11 PM drops to near zero.

The counter-example is instructive. Bridgewater built radical transparency into machinery — all meetings recorded and viewable by any employee, a "dot collector" for real-time peer ratings, a handbook of _Principles_ — and still produced a 2016 complaint to Connecticut's human rights commission describing a "cauldron of fear and intimidation", with about a quarter of new hires leaving within two years ([Bridgewater Associates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridgewater_Associates)). **Transparency without safety is surveillance.** The CEO absorbs the first cost of candour; only then is candour asked of anyone else.

## 7. How culture breaks at 10, 30 and 100

Culture does not decay gradually. It breaks at thresholds, for structural reasons, and the reasons are predictable enough to prepare for. The underlying constraint is that human groups have size limits — Dunbar's number, "a suggested cognitive limit to the number of people with whom one can maintain stable social relationships", commonly cited at about 150 with an inner core of roughly 5 and a next ring of about 15 taking two-thirds of a person's social time ([Dunbar's number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dunbar%27s_number); the figure is contested and later methods give much lower ranges, so treat it as a shape, not a number).

| Threshold | What breaks | The mechanical cause | The tell | What the CEO does |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **~4–6** — the first hire who doesn't know the founders | Transmission. Everything was implicit and now something must be explained | The founders' shared context stops being universal. Nobody has told the new person which behaviours are rewarded, so they infer it — usually wrongly | The new person asks permission for things that need no permission, and does not ask for things that do | Write the values down **before** this hire, not after. Personally onboard: two hours on why the company exists, one dealer visit in week one, the weekly-note archive to read |
| **~8–12** — the first management layer | Direct line of sight. The CEO stops seeing most behaviour | A manager becomes the culture for their reports. Gallup's 2026 report shows the layer is where engagement is decided: manager engagement fell from 31% in 2022 to 22% in 2025 while non-manager engagement stayed flat, and South Asia had the steepest regional decline ([Gallup](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/349484/state-of-the-global-workplace.aspx)) | You hear a decision second-hand and it is not the decision you would have made — and you find out three weeks late | Promote on values as well as competence; make "models the values" an explicit criterion. Start skip-levels. Accept that you are now managing the managers, not the work ([[16-decisions-delegation-and-not-being-the-bottleneck\|lesson 16]]) |
| **~10–20** — the first office/remote split | Shared information. Two cultures form, and the remote one is the second-class one | Informal information moves in the room. Whoever is not in the room learns things later and smaller. GitLab's all-remote guide treats handbook-first documentation and deliberate informal communication as required infrastructure precisely because hybrid arrangements make this worst ([GitLab handbook — all-remote](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/company/culture/all-remote/guide/)) | A decision "was discussed" and the remote person did not know | Force the written channel for everything decision-shaped, including for the people in the room. If the room can bypass the vault, the vault will die |
| **~first time a value costs money** | Belief. This is the single biggest culture event a small company has | Everyone watches whether the value or the cash wins. Zoho's "never show ads… nor sell user information" and Zerodha's "not advertising, spamming, pushing" are values precisely because they are expensive, permanently ([Zoho](https://www.zoho.com/aboutus.html); [Zerodha](https://zerodha.com/about/philosophy/)) | A deal, a feature or a hire is on the table that violates a stated value and would materially help | Pay the cost, in the open, and **name it at the next all-hands**: what we turned down, which value, what it cost. An expensive right decision nobody hears about teaches nobody anything |
| **~first high performer who violates a value** | Everything. This decides whether the values are real | The team correctly reads that the value applies only to people who are not indispensable | People start saying "that's just how he is" | Act, and act visibly. The Netflix framing is the honest one — a team, not a family, where the question is "knowing everything I know today, would I hire X again?" ([jobs.netflix.com/culture](https://jobs.netflix.com/culture)). Import the honesty, not the mechanism: at VSYST this is a documented process with a written warning, a defined improvement period and the COO's disciplinary steps ([[T27-policy-handbook-toc\|COO T27]] row 9), not a founder's judgement call |
| **~30** | Informal coordination | Everybody no longer knows what everybody is doing; the "everyone in one WhatsApp group" model fails | Duplicate work; a decision reversed because two people did not know | Written rituals become mandatory: a real cadence, a published scorecard, role charters ([[T29-org-chart-and-role-charters\|COO T29]]) |
| **~100** | Direct CEO influence entirely | You now influence culture only through mechanisms and through the managers you promoted | You meet an employee you have never spoken to who describes the company in terms you do not recognise | Culture becomes a system to be audited, not led: a survey instrument, a values-based promotion process, and the eight-style diagnostic in [The Culture Factor](https://hbr.org/2018/01/the-culture-factor). Far away — the trigger is real, the work is not yet |

**The honest note about VSYST's position.** Three of these seven thresholds are more than two years away, and pretending otherwise is how a three-person company wastes a quarter building HR machinery. The two that are live in the next twelve months are the **first hire who doesn't know the founders** and the **first time a value costs money.** Prepare for exactly those two. The rest is a map for when you get there.

## 8. The family-firm culture problem, in full

Two people at the standup are related. This is not a footnote to the culture; for every non-family employee you ever hire, it is the **primary fact** about the company, and they will have formed a view about it before the end of week one.

### 8.1 Why the standard advice fails here

Family business research separates three overlapping systems — family, ownership and management — each with different and sometimes conflicting values, goals and actions; the standard governance answer is deliberate structure (a family council, a written family constitution, explicit rules on capital, control, career selection and conflict) that keeps family dynamics from destabilising the business ([Family business](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Family_business)). The risk is documented on both sides: nepotism produces "poorer workplace atmosphere and tension"; a 2026 survey of over 1,000 workers found **61% said they would have greater trust in their managers if promotions were based entirely on merit**; and research on Indian family firms specifically finds family CEOs in high-performing companies earn significantly more than professional CEOs, which is read as controlling families extracting private benefits ([Nepotism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nepotism)).

The standard advice — family constitution, family council, independent directors — is correct and mostly not yet actionable at three people with no employees. What **is** actionable is the small, cheap subset: written criteria, documented decisions, and a named route for complaint. Do that subset now, while it is abstract and nobody's feelings are engaged. Every one of these mechanisms is ten times harder to introduce after the first incident, because introducing it then is an accusation.

### 8.2 The question nobody will ask you

Every non-family employee, from the first one onward, silently asks some version of:

> _"If it ever comes down to me versus a relative — on a promotion, on credit for a piece of work, on whose account of a disagreement is believed — who wins? And is there any ceiling above which I cannot go, because those seats are spoken for?"_

They will not ask it. Asking it is career-limiting and socially rude, and in a high-power-distance workplace the cost of asking upward is higher still ([Hofstede's cultural dimensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hofstede%27s_cultural_dimensions_theory)). So they will answer it themselves, from evidence, within about a month — and the evidence they use will be small things: who gets interrupted, whose mistake gets a laugh and whose gets a silence, who is in the car on the way back from the dealer visit.

**Answer it before it is asked.** Five sentences, said by the CEO at the first all-hands after the first hire, and written in the vault:

> "Two of us here are related. That is a fact about how this company started, and it is an advantage — it is why we survived being pre-revenue. It also creates a risk that decisions about pay, promotion and credit get made on relationship rather than performance, and we are not going to pretend that risk isn't real. So: performance criteria here are written before the person is evaluated, not after. Family gets more scrutiny than everyone else, not less, and the reasoning is written down where you can read it. And if you ever think a decision here went the family's way when it shouldn't have, [named person outside the family] is who you take it to, and taking it there will never be held against you."

That paragraph costs nothing and buys more than any perk you could offer. What it must not be is a promise you have not built the mechanisms for — a stated commitment with no route behind it is worse than silence, because it converts a suspicion into a proven lie.

### 8.3 The eight ground rules

These are the artefact of exercise 12.4: a one-page document signed by all three directors, minuted at a board meeting, and readable by every employee.

| # | Ground rule | Why it exists | What it costs | The counter-signal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Criteria before the person.** Any role a family member might fill gets its written charter — mission, outcomes, competencies, band — published before the family member is considered ([[T29-org-chart-and-role-charters\|COO T29]], [[T10-hiring-scorecard-and-process\|COO T10]]) | Prevents the seat being shaped around the person | Slower; occasionally awkward | A role description that appears after the appointment |
| 2 | **The outside test, written.** For every family appointment, pay change or promotion, one director writes the answer to: _"If this person had no relationship to any of us, would we hire/promote/pay them this, from an outside pool?"_ — and files it | Forces the counterfactual to be stated, not felt | A genuinely uncomfortable paragraph | The test is done verbally, or "obviously yes" is the whole answer |
| 3 | **More scrutiny, not less.** Family performance conversations happen on schedule, in writing, with the same criteria and a stricter bar on evidence | Corrects the default, which runs the other way | Family friction, reliably | A review cycle where the non-family person's review happened and the family member's did not |
| 4 | **A named non-family escalation route.** One person outside the family — an advisor, the company's CS or CA, later an independent director — whom any employee can raise a concern with, named publicly, with the CEO's written commitment that using it is never held against anyone. (Separate from and additional to the statutory POSH route, which the COO course owns — [[T27-policy-handbook-toc\|COO T27]] row 1; **VERIFY LIVE** the Internal Committee threshold with a labour consultant) | An employee cannot complain about the CEO's relative to the CEO | A retainer, or a favour asked | The route exists on paper but the named person has never met the team |
| 5 | **Decisions in writing, within 24 hours.** Any decision involving family — pay, role, equity, an exception — goes into the decision log with the reasoning and the decider ([[T09-decision-log-and-adr\|COO T09]], [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log\|C29]]) | Removes "it was discussed" as a defence | Minutes of typing | A pay change nobody can locate the reasoning for |
| 6 | **Separate forums.** Family conversations and company decisions happen in different rooms with different records. A decision reached at dinner does not exist until it is minuted the next morning ([[02-how-a-ceo-thinks\|lesson 02]] §12) | Dinner has no minutes and memory diverges with feelings attached | Repeating a conversation you already had | An employee learns of a decision that has no record |
| 7 | **A register of interests, kept live.** Related-party transactions, family vendors, the pump premises, anything where a director's relative benefits — declared and minuted. s.166(4)–(5) of the Companies Act 2013 already forbids a director achieving undue gain for themselves or their relatives ([s.166](https://ca2013.com/166-duties-of-directors/)); **VERIFY LIVE** the current related-party thresholds and disclosure form with the CS | Turns a cultural risk into a governance record | An hour a quarter | A family-linked vendor nobody declared |
| 8 | **Succession is stated, not assumed.** In one sentence, in writing: whether family membership confers any claim on a role, and what happens to a director's seat and shares on exit or death ([[C02-founders-agreement-and-vesting-checklist\|C02]], [[C30-pre-mortem-crisis-playbook-and-succession\|C30]]) | The unanswered version is the ceiling question in §8.2 | The hardest conversation on this list | A shareholders' agreement that is still "on the list" a year in |

**The house call for VSYST:** rules 1, 2, 5 and 6 cost nothing and should be signed this quarter. Rule 4 needs one named outsider — start with whoever already plays that role informally, and say the name out loud at the first all-hands. Rules 7 and 8 are governance work that belongs with [[09-board-governance-and-the-directors-duties|lesson 09]] and the CS. Rule 3 is the one that will actually get skipped; put it in the quarterly review checklist so skipping it is visible.

## 9. Culture, remote work and the Indian context

### 9.1 Raipur or remote — a culture decision disguised as a hiring decision

| Option | Culture effect | What it costs | Fit at VSYST today |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **All in Raipur** | Fastest transmission; the founders' behaviour is visible; the district is an hour away | A much smaller talent pool, especially for senior engineering | The default, and correct for the first 3–5 hires — proximity to dealers (V1) is a real advantage, not a consolation |
| **Fully distributed** | Forces written culture immediately; widest pool | Requires handbook-first discipline from day one; no accidental learning ([GitLab](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/company/culture/all-remote/guide/)) | Viable for one specialist role. Not viable as the whole company while the founders themselves work in a room together |
| **Hybrid — some in the room, some not** | **The dangerous one.** Two cultures form and the remote one is second-class | Constant, invisible information asymmetry | This is what will happen by accident. Decide it deliberately or it decides itself |

The mitigation is one rule, and it applies to the people in the room as much as the people outside it: **anything decision-shaped goes into the vault, in writing, even when everyone was present.** If the room can bypass the vault, the vault dies within a quarter and the remote hire becomes a contractor in everything but title.

### 9.2 WhatsApp — the channel that is doing your culture whether you like it or not

WhatsApp is not optional at VSYST: 77% of Indian SMEs use WhatsApp Business ([Inc42](https://inc42.com/features/how-whatsapp-business-is-bringing-indias-smes-to-the-digital-fold/)), dealers order in it, and the founders live in it. What it does to a company's culture is specific and worth naming:

- **It makes everything urgent and nothing durable.** A decision in a WhatsApp thread is unfindable in three weeks, which structurally defeats V3.
- **It privileges whoever is awake.** A thread at 11:40 PM sets a norm about 11:40 PM. See §3.4 — a CEO's visible behaviour about hours is read as policy.
- **It merges audiences.** Dealer data in a group where it should not be is the most likely V4 violation this company will ever have, and it will happen because someone was being helpful.

The channel contract, in one line each: **WhatsApp for speed and for dealers; the vault for decisions; email for anything a lawyer, a bank or an OMC might read.** The COO course owns the full channel map ([[08-the-operating-cadence|COO 08]] §10); the CEO's job is the enforcement behaviour — when a decision happens in WhatsApp, the CEO is the person who replies "putting this in the vault" and then does it, every time, for six months, until someone else does it first.

### 9.3 Disagreeing upward, and the mechanisms that make it possible

Two forces stack here. The general one: employee silence is produced by fear of reprisal, by futility when suggestions are repeatedly rejected, and by hierarchy — "as perceived managerial power increases, employees become less likely to communicate critical information", the phenomenon Morrison and Milliken named as organisational silence ([Employee silence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Employee_silence)). The India-specific one: high power distance means "clearly established hierarchies" and a lower baseline of questioning authority ([Hofstede's cultural dimensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hofstede%27s_cultural_dimensions_theory)) — a national average that a specific company can beat, but only on purpose. Add hierarchy-by-age from §2.1 and you have a company where the person who knows the bug will not say so in the room.

Four mechanisms, in the order they become affordable:

| Mechanism | How it works | When it becomes affordable | Honest caveat |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Written positions before the discussion** | Everyone writes their answer independently — any language, any format — before anybody speaks. The CEO writes first and shares last | **Now**, at three people | The highest-return habit available to VSYST this quarter ([[02-how-a-ceo-thinks\|lesson 02]] §9) |
| **The CEO speaks last** | In any meeting the CEO chairs, the CEO's opinion is the final one voiced, not the first | **Now** | Costs nothing and is very hard to actually do. Count how often you fail |
| **The rotating dissenter** | One named person per decision whose job is to argue the other side, rotated so it is a role, not a personality | **Now** | Depersonalises objection, which is the whole point in a family firm |
| **Anonymous input** | A form, read by the CEO, answered publicly at the all-hands | **~8–10 people.** Below that, anonymity is fiction and pretending otherwise is worse than not offering it | Say this out loud rather than offering fake anonymity. Until then, the mechanism is the named outsider in §8.3 rule 4 |

A fifth, cheaper than all of them: **answer the first hard question well, in public.** The team's estimate of whether dissent is safe is formed almost entirely from the first two or three observed instances. Get those right and the mechanisms become maintenance.

### 9.4 Language

Decide it, do not drift into it. The working proposal: **the room runs in whatever language works — Hindi, Chhattisgarhi, English, mixed — and the vault runs in English** because the vault has to be readable by a future hire, a CA, a partner and a Quartz build. Say explicitly that a written position in Hindi is a complete written position; V3 asks for writing, not for English. A company that quietly requires English fluency to participate in decisions has selected its culture without noticing.

## 10. Measuring culture without a survey team

You cannot run an engagement survey at three people, and you should not try. What you can do is watch six signals that are free, honest and hard to fake.

| Signal | What it actually measures | How to collect it at 3–15 people | The bad reading | Honest caveat at VSYST's size |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Regretted attrition** | Whether the people you wanted to keep are staying. The keeper test run backwards ([jobs.netflix.com/culture](https://jobs.netflix.com/culture)) | For every departure, the CEO writes one line before the exit interview: _would I have fought to keep this person?_ | Any regretted departure in the first ten hires is a culture event, not a market event | n=0 today. Start the habit at the first departure so the definition is not invented under pressure |
| **Bad-news latency** | Whether people believe the messenger is safe. The most diagnostic number on this list | For each incident: hours between "someone knew" and "the CEO knew". Log it in the post-mortem ([[T17-incident-postmortem\|COO T17]]) | The number rising over two quarters | Available immediately, including between the three founders |
| **Has anyone said no to the CEO this month?** | Whether §9.3's mechanisms work at all | Count it. A named instance, in a meeting, that changed something | Zero for two consecutive months means the meetings are theatre | The single best question on this page for a family-founded company |
| **Time-to-mistake-report** | Whether errors surface or get quietly fixed | How long between a mistake happening and it being said out loud | Mistakes discovered by customers rather than reported internally | Test it on yourself first: how long did your last one take? |
| **Referral rate** | Whether people would put their own name behind working here | Of hires and serious candidates, how many came from a current person's recommendation | Nobody refers anybody, ever | The cleanest signal at 5–15 people, and it needs no instrument |
| **Counter-signal check** | Whether the values in [[C21-values-and-operating-principles-worksheet\|C21]] are still real | Read the counter-signal column from §4.1 once a quarter and mark each: seen / not seen | Any counter-signal seen and not addressed within a week | Fifteen minutes a quarter. Put it on the quarterly checklist |

Two disciplines. **Write down the reading, even when it is zero** — a series of zeros is data, an absence of records is not. And **do not add an engagement survey until roughly 25 people**; below that the sample is too small to be anonymous and the instrument will produce polite noise. Gallup's 2026 finding that global engagement fell to 20% and that manager engagement drove the decline is useful as an argument for watching your first management layer closely ([Gallup](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/349484/state-of-the-global-workplace.aspx)) — it is not an argument for buying a survey tool at six people.

## 11. At VSYST — applying this now

- **You already have a culture.** It is built out of a technical founder's habits and a family firm's norms, and it is currently unexamined. Run the three-question audit in §1.2 this week; it takes forty minutes and it is the only step here with no prerequisite.
- **The two live thresholds are hire number four and the first expensive value.** Everything about ten managers, a hundred people and survey instruments is a map for later. Prepare for exactly those two.
- **Write the values before the first hire, not after.** After means the first employee's inference — formed in week one, from small evidence — becomes the definition.
- **Five candidates are drafted in §5 and none of them are adopted.** Run the three-week argue-and-adopt process; the CEO writes first and shares last, and the domain-expert director speaks first in the session.
- **Every value needs three of the six mechanism slots and a written counter-signal.** A value with fewer than three is either cut or labelled aspirational and dated. No exceptions, including for the ones you like most.
- **The family ground rules are the highest-value, lowest-cost artefact in this lesson.** Rules 1, 2, 5 and 6 of §8.3 cost nothing and are ten times harder to introduce after the first incident. Sign them this quarter and minute them.
- **Name the non-family escalation route out loud, with a person's name in it, at the first all-hands.** A route with no name is not a route.
- **Start the CEO rituals now, at three people, even though they feel absurd.** The Friday note, the customer story, the post-mortem with your own mistake first. Their value is realised by hire number four, and they cannot be retrofitted.
- **The first violation is the real adoption.** Until a value has cost somebody something visible, it has been announced, not adopted. When it happens, say so at the all-hands: what we turned down, which value, what it cost.
- **What this lesson does not own:** the handbook, the code of conduct, POSH, the disciplinary procedure and the all-hands slot belong to the [[COO-Docs/index|COO course]] ([[09-people-operations|COO 09]], [[T27-policy-handbook-toc|T27]], [[T24-all-hands-agenda|T24]]). Hiring, equity and the mechanics of firing belong to [[10-building-the-team-hiring-equity-and-firing|lesson 10]]. The craft of the narrative belongs to [[12-communication-the-ceo-as-chief-storyteller|lesson 12]]. This lesson owns the values, the mechanisms, the CEO's own rituals and the family-firm ground rules.

## 12. Exercises

**12.1 — The accidental-rewards audit (30 min).** Answer the three questions in §1.2 for the last 90 days, in writing, with named incidents — not categories. Three columns: **rewarded · tolerated · punished (including silently)**. Then write one sentence under each list: _"Stated honestly, this company's real values are…"_ **Artefact:** a dated page in the vault titled "Behaviours we currently reward by accident", linked from [[C21-values-and-operating-principles-worksheet|C21]]. Do it before 12.2; the draft in §5 is contaminated if you read it first.

**12.2 — Sharpen the values (45 min).** Working alone, write 3–6 values in the sharp form: **what it forbids · what it costs · one costly case in the next twelve months · Lencioni type (core / aspirational / permission-to-play / accidental)**. Use §5's five as a foil — steal, cut or reject each one explicitly. Then run step 1 of §5.3 properly: the other two directors write theirs independently before seeing yours. **Artefact:** three independent drafts plus the merged sheet, filed as version 0 of [[C21-values-and-operating-principles-worksheet|C21]].

**12.3 — Value to mechanism (30 min).** For each surviving value, fill the §4.1 row: behaviour demanded · mechanism (at least three of the six slots) · **counter-signal**. Write the counter-signal column first. Then do the one thing that makes it real today: add one values-based competency row and its interview question to the Support/Ops Associate scorecard in [[T10-hiring-scorecard-and-process|COO T10]], and add the counter-signal list to the quarterly checklist. **Artefact:** the completed mechanism table on the back page of [[C21-values-and-operating-principles-worksheet|C21]], plus one edited row in T10.

**12.4 — The family ground rules (45 min).** Draft the eight rules of §8.3 as a one-page document in plain language, with a named person for rule 4 and a real answer for rule 8 or an explicit "to be decided by <date>". Circulate to both co-founder-directors, incorporate their edits, and put it on the next board agenda for a minuted resolution ([[C17-board-pack-agenda-and-minutes|C17]]). **Artefact:** `family-ground-rules.md` in the vault, versioned and dated, plus a board resolution and a [[C29-decision-journal-and-one-way-door-log|C29]] entry recording why each rule was adopted or dropped.

**12.5 — The first all-hands narrative (30 min).** Write the five-minute opening for the first all-hands after hire number one, using [[C22-internal-comms-calendar-and-all-hands|C22]]: the three sentences of §6.1, the no-list, one thing that went badly, and the five-sentence family paragraph from §8.2 with the escalation name filled in. Read it aloud once, timed. If it runs over six minutes, cut the adjectives, not the bad news. **Artefact:** a filled [[C22-internal-comms-calendar-and-all-hands|C22]] with the opening script and the twelve-month comms calendar, and the first Friday note written the same week ([[C27-ceo-weekly-template-and-calendar-audit|C27]]).
