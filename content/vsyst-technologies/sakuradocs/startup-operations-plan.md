# Startup Operations Plan — App & Website Development Company

*Created 2 July 2026. A step-by-step guide for a first-time founder covering legal setup, finances, operations, marketing, sales, support, and hiring, followed by a phase-wise implementation roadmap.*

---

## Two Business Models — Know Which One You Are

- **Services company (agency):** clients pay you to build *their* apps/websites. Revenue from projects. Easier to start, profitable early, but you trade time for money.
- **Product company:** you build *your own* app and customers pay to use it. Slower to earn, but scales without hiring per project.

Most first-time founders start as an agency (cash flow) and later build a product from the profits. This plan covers both and flags where they differ.

---

# Part 1: The Six Areas of Your Business

## 1. Legal & Company Foundation

1. **Register the company** as a private limited company / LLC. This separates personal money from company money.
2. **Founder agreement** (if co-founders exist): ownership split, decision rights, exit terms. Include **vesting** (~4 years) so a co-founder who quits early doesn't keep a full share. Skipping this is the #1 early-startup killer.
3. **Open a business bank account.** Never mix personal and company money.
4. **Basic contracts:** client services agreement, NDA, employment/contractor template. One-time lawyer fee, reused forever.
5. **Buy your domain**, claim social handles, trademark later when funds allow.

## 2. Finances

**Three numbers to know every week:**
- **Cash in bank** — what you actually have.
- **Burn rate** — total monthly spend.
- **Runway** — cash ÷ burn = months until money runs out. Below 6 months, fixing this is your only job.

**The habit:** record every transaction. Use simple accounting software (QuickBooks, Zoho Books, Wave — Wave is free) and a part-time bookkeeper/CA from early on. They file taxes; you review numbers monthly.

**Essentials:**
- **Agency pricing:** cost per hour = total monthly costs ÷ billable hours; charge 2.5–3×. Fixed-price quotes with a written scope; out-of-scope work costs extra. **Take 30–50% upfront — always.**
- **Product pricing:** one simple paid plan early. Paying users tell the truth; free users don't.
- **Pay yourself a small salary** from day one.
- **Build a 3–6 month expense reserve** before optional spending.
- **Funding:** you likely don't need investors. Agencies self-fund from client money. Raise only when demand is proven and money is the only limit.

## 3. Operations

1. **One project-management tool** (Trello, Linear, Notion, ClickUp). All work lives there.
2. **Delivery pipeline** every project follows: Inquiry → Proposal → Contract + advance → Design → Build → Client review → Launch → 30-day support → Testimonial request. Written as a checklist, no exceptions.
3. **Document everything done twice** — a short how-to doc each time. This is how you eventually delegate.
4. **Weekly rhythm:** 30-min team meeting (shipped / blocked / next) + your finance check.
5. **Review tool subscriptions quarterly** and cancel unused ones.

## 4. Marketing

1. **Positioning — be specific.** "Apps and websites" competes with millions. "Booking apps for salons and clinics" wins deals. Niche down based on your first 2–3 clients.
2. **Your website is your #1 salesperson:** who you help, what you build, proof (portfolio, testimonials), contact. No clients yet? Build 2–3 polished demo projects.
3. **One channel done well beats five done poorly.** In order of cost-effectiveness for an agency:
   - **Referrals** — ask after every project. Likely 50%+ of your business for years.
   - **LinkedIn / founder content** — 2–3 posts/week on problems solved, lessons learned. Compounds.
   - **Local meetups and business events.**
   - **Freelance platforms** — low margin but builds portfolio and reviews from zero.
   - **Paid ads: not yet** — they burn money until you know exactly who buys and why.
4. **Collect proof relentlessly:** every project → case study (problem, solution, result) + testimonial.

**Product marketing differs:** find where target users gather (communities, Reddit, forums), talk to them, launch on Product Hunt, write content/SEO answering what users search for.

## 5. Sales

**The pipeline — track every lead through:**
1. **Lead** — someone showed interest.
2. **Discovery call** — 30 min where you mostly ask: What are you trying to achieve? What happens if you don't build this? Budget? Timeline? Who decides?
3. **Proposal** — one page: problem, solution, timeline, fixed price, what's included and explicitly what's not. Send within 48 hours.
4. **Follow-up** — 3–4 times over two weeks. Silence isn't "no"; people are busy. Most deals are won here.
5. **Close** — contract signed, advance paid. **No advance = no work started.**

Track in a spreadsheet or free CRM (HubSpot free, Notion). Review weekly. Learn your math: leads → calls → proposals → closes. Once "10 leads = 1 client," growth is math, not hope.

**Two saving rules:**
- **Say no to bad clients** (hard price haggling, vague scope, "this should be easy," disrespect). They cost more than they pay.
- **Never work free or "for equity"** from strangers.

## 6. Customer Support & Retention

1. **One clear support channel** (support@ email). Promise a response time you can keep (1 business day) and keep it.
2. **Weekly progress updates to clients** during projects. Client anger comes from silence, not problems.
3. **Written post-launch policy:** e.g., 30 days free bug fixes, then a monthly maintenance retainer. Retainers = recurring revenue + warm relationships.
4. **Product:** founder answers every support email early. Keep a doc of all complaints/requests — patterns are your roadmap.
5. **Ask for feedback and reviews systematically.**

## 7. Team & Hiring

- Hire for whatever eats most founder time that someone cheaper could do.
- **Contractors before employees.**
- Typical agency hiring order: developer → designer → **project manager (the hire that frees the founder)** → salesperson.
- Written offer, clear responsibilities, 2–3 month trial stated upfront.

# Part 2: Phase-Wise Implementation Plan

## Phase 0 — Foundation (Weeks 1–4)
*Goal: legally and financially real.*
- Register company; founder agreement with vesting; business bank account.
- Accounting software + part-time CA/bookkeeper; contract templates.
- Domain, professional email, social handles; rough niche/positioning.
- **Exit:** you can legally sign a client and receive money into a company account.

## Phase 1 — First Revenue (Months 1–4)
*Goal: 3–5 paying clients and a portfolio. Don't scale anything yet.*
- Own website with 2–3 portfolio pieces (demos count).
- Reach out to entire personal network. Take small projects — goal is proof, not margin.
- Every project follows the delivery checklist; advances taken; delivered on time.
- Sales spreadsheet/CRM set up; weekly finance review habit started.
- Every finished project → case study + testimonial + referral ask.
- **Exit:** 3–5 completed paid projects, testimonials, known delivery cost per project.

## Phase 2 — Repeatability (Months 4–9)
*Goal: leads arrive without begging; delivery without heroics.*
- Commit to 1–2 marketing channels (referrals + LinkedIn) with weekly cadence.
- Sharpen niche based on most profitable/pleasant Phase 1 clients.
- Raise prices 20–30% on new clients.
- Offer maintenance retainers to past clients — start recurring revenue.
- Document delivery processes; hire first contractor/junior.
- Learn pipeline math (leads → calls → proposals → closes).
- **Exit:** 1–2 new leads/week from marketing/referrals; one project delivered mostly without founder.

## Phase 3 — Systematize (Months 9–18)
*Goal: business runs on process, not founder memory.*
- Hire/promote a project manager; founder shifts to sales + relationships + quality.
- Monthly financial review: revenue, margin per project, runway, retainer % of revenue.
- Build 3–6 month cash reserve.
- Productize into 2–3 fixed packages ("Business website — ₹X, 3 weeks" / "MVP app — ₹Y, 8 weeks").
- If client-request patterns validate a product idea, allocate 20% of team time to prototype it — funded by agency profit.
- **Exit:** founder can take a two-week holiday and the company still ships and sells.

## Phase 4 — Scale or Transform (Month 18+)
- **Path A — scale the agency:** sales lead hire, bigger clients, retainer-heavy contracts.
- **Path B — shift to product:** move team time from client work to product as paying users grow; consider funding only now, with traction.
- Quarterly planning; monthly financial statements; management layers only past ~7–8 people per team.

---

## The Five Habits That Matter More Than Any Plan

1. **Weekly finance check** — cash, burn, runway. Ten minutes, every week, forever.
2. **Advance payment before work starts.** No exceptions.
3. **Everything in writing** — scope, prices, promises, processes.
4. **Ask for the referral and testimonial** at the end of every project.
5. **One thing at a time** — one niche, one channel, one offering before adding the next. Startups die of indigestion, not starvation.

# Part 3: The Realities No One Puts in Plans

## Money Realities

- **Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact.** You can be "profitable" on paper and still die because clients haven't paid yet. A signed ₹10-lakh project is worth nothing until the money is in the bank. Watch cash, not revenue.
- **Clients pay late. Plan for it.** Invoices due in 15 days routinely arrive in 45–60. Rules that protect you: milestone-based payments (advance / mid-project / before final handover — never deliver final code before final payment), invoice immediately when a milestone completes, and a polite-but-relentless follow-up schedule. "Final files and launch happen after the last payment clears" is a normal, professional clause.
- **Client concentration is a hidden debt.** If one client is more than ~30–40% of your revenue, you don't have a business — you have a boss who can fire you. Keep selling even when you're busy; the pipeline must never go empty just because delivery is full.
- **Feast-and-famine is the agency disease.** You sell hard → get busy delivering → stop selling → projects end → panic. The cure: sales activity (outreach, content, follow-ups) happens every single week regardless of workload, even 2–3 hours.
- **Taxes have deadlines with penalties.** GST/sales tax filings, advance tax, payroll withholdings — your CA handles the filing, but *you* must know the calendar. Missed deadlines cost fines and, worse, credibility with banks and clients. Ask your CA for a one-page annual compliance calendar in month one.
- **Never spend money that's committed elsewhere.** The advance a client paid you is partly *their* project's cost (contractor fees, tools, taxes). Mentally reserve tax money the day revenue arrives — a separate "tax savings" account you sweep 20–25% into works wonders.

## Client Realities

- **Scope creep is the silent killer.** "Can you just add one small thing?" repeated ten times turns a profitable project into a loss. The discipline: anything not written in the scope gets a friendly, immediate response — "Happy to! That's outside the current scope; it'll be ₹X and adds Y days. Want me to send a change order?" Say it cheerfully and early. Clients respect it; the ones who don't were bad clients anyway.
- **Put IP ownership in the contract.** Standard and fair: the client owns the final work *after full payment*; you keep the right to show it in your portfolio and reuse generic internal code/tools. Without this clause, a non-paying client can still claim the work is theirs.
- **A kill fee protects you from cancelled projects.** If the client cancels midway, the contract should say completed milestones are payable and the advance is non-refundable. This is standard.
- **The client who haggles hardest on price demands the most and pays the slowest.** This correlation is nearly a law of nature. Price objections at the start predict the whole relationship.
- **Under-promise, over-deliver — on timelines especially.** Whatever you estimate, things take 1.5× longer. Quote the padded number. Delivering early to a realistic deadline beats delivering late to an optimistic one, every time.
- **When a project goes wrong (one will):** tell the client early, with a recovery plan, before they discover it. Bad news early is a professionalism signal; bad news late is a betrayal. Most clients forgive problems; almost none forgive surprises.

## People & Partnership Realities

- **Co-founder conflict kills more startups than competition does.** The founder agreement isn't paranoia — it's the mechanism that lets you disagree safely. Also agree early on: who has the final call in each area (product, money, people), and how much either of you can spend without asking the other.
- **Your first hires copy your behavior, not your instructions.** If you cut corners, miss your own deadlines, or badmouth clients, so will they.
- **Fire fast, hire slow.** A wrong hire drains the team for months while you hope they improve. If your gut says no at week 6, it will still say no at month 6. Trial periods exist to be used.
- **Don't hire friends and family casually.** If you must, be explicit upfront: "at work I'm your boss, and this arrangement ends if it isn't working — and we stay friends." If that conversation feels impossible, don't hire them.
- **Key-person risk applies to you.** If only you can talk to clients, only you know the passwords, only you can deploy — the business stops when you're sick. Document credentials (a password manager with shared vaults), and make sure every critical task has a second person who can do it, even badly.

## Protection & Risk

- **Get professional liability insurance** (also called errors & omissions / professional indemnity) once you're serving real clients — it covers you if a bug or missed deadline costs a client money and they come after you. Cheap relative to what it protects.
- **Cap your liability in every contract:** a standard clause limits your total liability to the fees paid for the project. Without it, a ₹2-lakh website project can theoretically expose you to a claim for the client's "lost business."
- **If you build apps that store user data, privacy law applies to you** — privacy policies, data-protection rules (GDPR if you touch European users, India's DPDP Act, etc.), and app-store requirements. Build the basics into every project and charge for it; it's also a selling point.
- **Back up everything, and test the backups.** Client code, designs, contracts, financials. One laptop theft or ransomware hit without backups can end the company. Git + cloud storage + a password manager is the minimum stack.
- **Never personally guarantee business debt casually,** and never take payment terms you can't survive. Growing by taking on risk you don't understand isn't growth.

## Personal Realities

- **Keep 6–12 months of personal living expenses** separate from the business, or keep a side income until the business reliably pays your salary. Desperate founders take bad clients at bad prices — personal runway is what buys you the power to say no.
- **It takes longer than you think. Roughly twice as long.** The plan says 18 months to a systematized business; live reality is often 2–3 years. This is normal, not failure.
- **The emotional graph is a rollercoaster, and both ends lie.** The euphoric week after landing a big client and the despairing week after losing one are both temporary. Judge the business on 90-day trends, not on this week's mood.
- **Loneliness is an occupational hazard.** Employees can't be your confidants, and family often can't relate. Find 2–3 other founders you can be honest with — a mentor, a founder community, a monthly coffee. This is infrastructure, not socializing.
- **Comparison is poison.** The companies you see celebrating funding rounds on LinkedIn are showing highlight reels; many are losing money. A boring agency that profits ₹50k/month is more successful than most funded startups.
- **Protect the machine that runs the company: you.** Sleep, exercise, and one genuinely off day a week aren't luxuries — decision quality collapses without them, and your entire job is making decisions.
- **Know your personal 'why' and your walk-away line.** Decide in advance what failure looks like (e.g., "if there's no consistent profit in 24 months, I reassess") so a future emotional moment doesn't make that call for you.

## The Classic First-Year Mistakes (a checklist to avoid)

1. Building for months before trying to sell anything — sell first, build second.
2. No written scope, then arguing about what was included.
3. Working without an advance "just this once."
4. Underpricing to win, then resenting the client and the work.
5. One giant client and no pipeline.
6. Spending on office, branding, and tools before revenue justifies it.
7. Hiring because you're busy, not because the math works.
8. Founder doing ₹500/hour work while ₹50,000/hour work (sales, key relationships) sits idle.
9. Ignoring bookkeeping for months, then facing a tax-season horror show.
10. Saying yes to every project type — five niches served badly instead of one served brilliantly.

## Learning Resources Worth Your Time

- **The Personal MBA** (Josh Kaufman) — the whole business vocabulary in one book.
- **Profit First** (Mike Michalowicz) — the cash-management system, great for agencies.
- **The Mom Test** (Rob Fitzpatrick) — how to talk to customers without them lying to you; essential if you build a product.
- **Company of One** (Paul Jarvis) — why staying small can be the goal, not a failure.
- **Rework** (Basecamp) — short, punchy antidote to startup mythology.
- Y Combinator's free **Startup School** videos (product-startup oriented, but the sales and customer-conversation lessons apply to agencies too).
