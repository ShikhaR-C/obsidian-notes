# 19 — Planning, OKRs and the Quarterly Rhythm

_Phase 4 · Autopilot · Months 6–18. After this lesson you can run VSYST's annual plan in half a day, set 3–5 quarterly rocks with owners and measurable finish lines, score them honestly at quarter end, run the quarterly half-day from a fixed agenda, close the loop into the Monday scorecard, and send a monthly written update that keeps the company mentor-visible and fundraise-ready._

Fifteen lessons have built mechanisms that run on a **week**: the Monday scorecard, the ops meeting, the pipeline review, the Friday written review. A weekly rhythm is excellent at keeping a company alive and useless at deciding what it should become — you can run a flawless heartbeat for a year and arrive nowhere in particular. Planning is the loop that fixes that, and it is three loops, not one: **the year says what matters, the quarter says what gets done, and the week says whether it is happening.** This lesson installs the two outer loops, wires them into the weekly one you already run, and adds the two written artefacts that keep the company honest between sessions — the quarter's plan doc and the monthly update.

## 1. The planning stack — three loops, one system

Everything in this lesson is one system with three clocks. Each loop answers a different question, produces a different artefact, and hands its output down to the loop inside it.

| Loop          | When it runs                                    | The question it answers                                                            | The artefact                                                                               | Who is in the room                                       |
| ------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | ------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| **Annual**    | One day in March, before the new financial year | _What is this year for?_ Three priorities, a budget, a headcount line              | The annual plan (one page) + the budget in [the workbook](toolkit/vsyst-coo-workbook.xlsx) | All three directors                                      |
| **Quarterly** | Half a day, last week of the quarter            | _What are the next 90 days for?_ 3–5 rocks with owners and measurable finish lines | The quarter plan doc ([[T19-quarterly-plan-and-review                                      | T19]]) + the `OKR Tracker` tab ([[T06-okr-planning-sheet | T06]])                                        | All three directors |
| **Weekly**    | Monday's ops meeting; Friday's written review   | _Is it happening?_ On track / off track, confidence, to-dos, issues                | Scorecard ([[T05-kpi-scorecard                                                             | T05]]), meeting notes ([[T03-weekly-ops-meeting-agenda   | T03]]), the WBR ([[T28-weekly-business-review | T28]])              | Whoever is in the operating rhythm |

You already run the inner loop — [[08-the-operating-cadence|lesson 08]] installed it and [[16-metrics-dashboards-and-scorecards|lesson 16]] gave it numbers. What this lesson adds is the **handover between loops**, which is where planning systems usually break:

- **The year's three priorities constrain the quarter.** A rock that serves none of them needs an argument, not a slot.
- **The quarter's rocks become the weekly meeting's Rocks segment.** Monday reads them on track / off track in five minutes ([[T03-weekly-ops-meeting-agenda|T03]]'s second segment) — that is the whole cascade at this size.
- **Most key results should simply _be_ scorecard rows with a quarter-end target.** If a KR needs its own measurement apparatus, it is either the wrong KR or a missing scorecard row.

This nesting is the EOS "meeting pulse" — a weekly beat paired with a quarterly session and a longer annual one ([EOS — Meeting Pulse](https://www.eosworldwide.com/meeting-pulse)) — and the same shape sits inside Scaling Up's habits, where the annual and quarterly themes are what the daily and weekly huddles report against ([Growth Institute — the Rockefeller Habits checklist](https://blog.growthinstitute.com/scale-up-blueprint/10-rockefeller-habits-checklist)).

Each loop fails in a characteristic way when it runs alone. **An annual plan with no quarterly session is a document** — reread in December, never acted on. **A quarter with no weekly review is a poster** — five rocks that are all 20% done in week 12. **A weekly rhythm with no quarter is motion** — a company that is busy, on time, and unable to say what it achieved this year. The three loops are cheap precisely because each one only handles the decisions at its own altitude.

## 2. The annual plan — three priorities, a budget, a headcount line

The annual session is **one day in March, before the new financial year** ([[08-the-operating-cadence|lesson 08]] §8 booked it): a half-day of planning, and a half-day for the operating-system audit that [[20-the-autopilot-test-and-scaling-the-machine|lesson 20]] describes. March, not January, because an Indian company's year is April–March — the budget, the compliance calendar and the CA's world all run on the financial year, and a plan on a different clock from the books creates two versions of every number.

The plan itself is **one page with four blocks**:

| Block                  | What it contains                                                                                                                                            | Where it lives                                                                             |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| **Three priorities**   | Three sentences naming what this year is for. Not ten. Everything not in them is maintenance — real work, but not the thing you are trying to change        | The annual plan note in the vault                                                          |
| **The budget**         | The year's number per [[T20-budget-vs-actual-and-cash-forecast                                                                                              | T20]] category, and what it does to runway ([[finance/07-phase-7-bootstrapping-runway-burn | Finance Phase 7]])   | `Budget vs Actual` and `Runway & 13-Week Cash` tabs |
| **The headcount line** | Which roles open in which month, employee or contractor, with the **trigger metric** that fires each one — hire on triggers, not hope ([[T21-headcount-plan | T21]])                                                                                     | `Headcount Plan` tab |
| **The assumptions**    | The three to five beliefs the plan rests on, each with the number that would prove it wrong                                                                 | The same note — this is the block people skip and later wish they had                      |

Three rules keep the day from turning into a strategy offsite:

1. **Three priorities is a hard cap.** The company has three directors and maybe a support hire; a fourth priority is one of the first three not getting done. EOS's one-year plan works the same way — a small number of measurable goals for the year, not a list of everything worth doing ([EOS — the V/TO](https://www.eosworldwide.com/blog/9684-traction-thoughts-defining-vision-visiontraction-organizer-vto)).
2. **A budget is a decision, not a forecast.** "₹X on tools this year" means somebody may spend it and nobody may exceed it without the [[T22-delegation-of-authority-matrix|DoA]] path. A revenue forecast is a _guess_ and is labelled as one; a spend budget is a _commitment_ and is enforced monthly.
3. **The year is re-cut every quarter, not defended.** The plan's job is to make the four quarterly sessions faster and more consistent, not to be right in March. When a quarter's evidence kills an assumption, the assumption dies with a logged decision ([[T09-decision-log-and-adr|T09]]) — that is the plan working, not failing.

## 3. OKR mechanics — objectives, key results, scoring, confidence

An **OKR** pairs an **Objective** — a qualitative statement of what you want to achieve this quarter, with no numbers in it — with two to four **Key Results**: the measurable benchmarks that prove you achieved it ([Measure What Matters — notes](https://grahammann.net/book-notes/measure-what-matters-by-john-doerr)). The split is the whole trick. The objective is what a human cares about; the key results are what a spreadsheet can settle. Arguments about whether a quarter went well end when the KRs are numeric.

The distinction, in DZZLO terms:

| Not a key result            | Why it fails                                             | The key result version                                                            |
| --------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "Improve dealer onboarding" | No number; nobody can say at week 13 whether it happened | Activation rate (≥1 customer linked and ≥1 invoice in 14 days) from **40% → 60%** |
| "Ship the billing work"     | A task list, not a result; done-ness is negotiable       | Subscription billing live for **3 paying tenants**                                |
| "Better support"            | Two people will grade it differently                     | Median first-response time **6 h → 2 h**                                          |
| "Talk to IOCL"              | An activity                                              | **2 written next-step commitments** recorded in the partnership tracker           |
| "Grow revenue 3×"           | Depends on a pricing decision the quarter doesn't own    | **5 paying dealer tenants** (a count you control by selling)                      |

The mechanics live in [[T06-okr-planning-sheet|T06]] and the `OKR Tracker` tab of [the workbook](toolkit/vsyst-coo-workbook.xlsx); what matters conceptually is four things.

**Baseline → target, so progress computes.** Every KR carries the number today and the number at quarter end; progress is (current − baseline) ÷ (target − baseline), which works identically for lower-is-better KRs. A KR with no baseline is a KR nobody has measured — measure it before you commit to moving it.

**Scoring 0.0–1.0, where 0.7 is a good quarter — on stretch KRs.** Doerr's convention grades each key result on a 0–1 scale and treats around 0.7 on a stretch target as success; straight 1.0s across the board mean the targets were sandbagged ([Measure What Matters — notes](https://grahammann.net/book-notes/measure-what-matters-by-john-doerr)). Use it with one adjustment at VSYST's size: **most KRs here are committed, not stretch** — a compliance KR or "13 of 13 ops meetings held" is expected to land at 1.0, and missing it is a real miss. Allow at most one deliberate stretch KR per objective, mark it as such in the sheet, and apply the 0.7 rule only to those.

**Confidence, updated weekly.** Beside on-track/off-track, each KR owner carries High / Medium / Low confidence in hitting the target. This is the system's early-warning output: a KR that is technically "on track" in week 4 with Low confidence is the most useful sentence anyone will say that month. Smoothing it — reporting Medium because the room is tense — destroys the only leading signal the quarter has.

**Not tied to pay in the early years.** The moment scores set salaries or bonuses, everyone negotiates targets downward and the numbers stop describing reality ([Quantive — OKRs and CFRs](https://quantive.com/resources/articles/okrs-and-cfrs)). The same source names what OKRs _are_ paired with: **CFRs — conversations, feedback and recognition** — the continuous performance side that makes goal-setting humane. At VSYST those already exist as mechanisms: the weekly 1:1 ([[T04-one-on-one-template|T04]]) is the conversation, the ops meeting's on/off-track pass is the feedback, and the monthly all-hands ([[T24-all-hands-agenda|T24]]) carries the recognition. Compensation is set by the [[09-people-operations|people system]], informed by the whole year, never by a score in a cell.

## 4. Rocks or OKRs — pick one language and stop arguing

EOS calls the quarter's priorities **Rocks** — the 3–7 things that must get done in the next 90 days, one owner each ([EOS — Meeting Pulse](https://www.eosworldwide.com/meeting-pulse)). OKRs call them objectives and key results. They overlap almost completely, and companies waste real hours arguing about which system they are "doing".

The honest difference is grammatical. **A rock is usually a deliverable** ("ship subscription billing"); **a key result is always a number** ("3 tenants billed successfully"). Rocks are easier for a small team to say out loud; KRs are harder to fake at quarter end. So this course uses both without confusion, by one rule:

> **Say "rocks" in the room; write them as OKRs in the sheet.** Every rock must state a measurable finish line — and a rock's finish line _is_ a key result.

Three consequences worth stating plainly. **Never run both systems in parallel** — a company with a rocks list _and_ a separate OKR set has two plans, and the second one is always the one nobody updates. **3–5, not 3–7**, at VSYST's size: with three directors already running the whole operating rhythm, a sixth rock is arithmetic, not ambition ([[T19-quarterly-plan-and-review|T19]]'s rule). And **a big rock gets a one-page charter** ([[T18-project-charter|T18]]) — problem, success metric, scope in/out, owner, milestones — because "ship subscription billing" hides a month of decisions that need writing down somewhere other than a cell.

## 5. How a five-person company plans a quarter in half a day

Half a day is enough because the thinking happens _before_ the room. [[T19-quarterly-plan-and-review|T19]] holds the four-hour agenda and the plan-doc skeleton; this section is what makes it work at three to five people.

**The prep, sent 48 hours ahead, written.** Nobody reads anything for the first time in the session — that rule alone is worth an hour.

- [ ] **COO:** the 13-week scorecard trends ([[T05-kpi-scorecard|T05]]) with one honest sentence per red or amber row; the quarter's budget vs actual ([[T20-budget-vs-actual-and-cash-forecast|T20]]); the re-scored risk register ([[T15-risk-register|T15]]); a draft of 5–8 candidate objectives to be argued down
- [ ] **Each KR owner:** their own score, 0.0–1.0, with a one-line why — self-scored before the room, never assigned in it
- [ ] **CEO:** the year's three priorities restated in three sentences, plus any change in direction, said before rocks are proposed rather than after
- [ ] **Domain-expert director:** the quarter's pipeline and partnership themes — what dealers actually said, what the IOCL and bank/gateway conversations moved to
- [ ] **Everyone:** their own list of what should be _stopped_

**The session, in four movements.** Review the quarter (scorecard trends, then OKR scores, then the retro of §7) — **review before plan, no exceptions**, because planning first turns last quarter's fictions into next quarter's assumptions. Then set the next quarter: everyone proposes, the room argues down to **3–5 rocks with one owner each and a written finish line**. Then money and people: budget changes by category, headcount triggers re-affirmed or deferred, the 13-week forecast re-based. Then decisions and cascade: the "decisions needed" list is decided in the room or parked with a date, and the plan doc is finished _before anyone leaves_.

**Two rules do most of the work at this size.** Every rock leaves the room with **its first two weeks of to-dos** — a rock without a first step is a poster, and week 1 is when quarters are actually lost. And the plan is written in the room, in the vault, at `quarters/QN-YYYY.md`, with the `OKR Tracker` tab updated before lunch; a plan doc "to be tidied up later" is a plan that never becomes Monday's agenda.

What a five-person company **does not** do: cascade OKRs into team and personal layers (there are no teams — the company is the team), grade people on scores, buy an OKR tool, or write more than one page. And name the hat: when the same person proposes a rock as CEO and owns it as the COO, say which hat is speaking — at a three-director family company that small piece of theatre prevents a surprising amount of confusion.

## 6. The planning calendar — where the half-day sits

The quarterly session sits in the **last week of the quarter** — the last weeks of June, September, December and March — and the March one is the day that also carries the annual plan and the operating-system audit. Book all four for the year in a single sitting, in the calendar, with the audits ([[08-the-operating-cadence|lesson 08]] §7's access, vendor and risk reviews) riding the same week. **A slot booked nine months out survives; a slot to be "found nearer the time" does not.**

Two other calendars run alongside, and the planning day must respect both:

| Ring                       | What lands near the quarter end                                                                                                                                                                                                                   | What that means for planning                       |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **The monthly close**      | The CA returns the month's P&L only after close inputs go across by the 5th ([[06-money-rails-and-finance-operations                                                                                                                              | lesson 06]])                                       | The last month of the quarter will **not** be closed on planning day. Plan on two closed months plus your own management numbers, and re-read the final figures at the next monthly review |
| **The statutory calendar** | Advance-tax instalments (typically 15 Jun / 15 Sep / 15 Dec / 15 Mar), quarterly TDS returns, GST returns each month or quarter, and the ROC ring after FY close — all dates **VERIFY LIVE** with the CA/CS each April ([[T16-compliance-calendar | T16]], [[13-compliance-calendar-risk-and-insurance | lesson 13]])                                                                                                                                                                               | Some quarter-ends are genuinely busy. Planning day is a **half day, not a week** partly for this reason — and compliance work is never a rock, because it is not a change you are trying to make; it is the floor |

Who prepares what is the §5 checklist; who _decides_ what is the [[T01-ceo-coo-operating-agreement|CEO–COO agreement]] — the COO facilitates and owns the artefacts, the CEO decides trade-offs when the room cannot. And the loop closes on the next working Monday: the ops meeting's Rocks segment reads the new list, the scorecard picks up whichever rows this quarter's rocks depend on, and the quarter starts being executed four days after it was planned instead of four weeks.

## 7. The retro — blameless, and the same muscle as a postmortem

The retro is the twenty minutes that decide whether the next quarter is different. Its format is three columns, everyone writing before anyone talks: **wins** (what worked — and the _mechanism_ that produced it, so it can be repeated), **misses** (what didn't — cause, not culprit), **learnings** (what you now know that you didn't 90 days ago).

**Blameless is a rule, not a mood.** It is the same discipline as the incident postmortem in [[T17-incident-postmortem|T17]], and it comes from the same place: Google's SRE practice holds that a postmortem must be written without blaming an individual or team, because the moment people fear punishment, they stop reporting the information the organisation needs ([Google SRE — postmortem culture](https://sre.google/sre-book/postmortem-culture/)). A missed quarter is an incident with a 90-day timeline. The questions are identical — what did we believe, what actually happened, which mechanism was missing — and so is the payoff: **you get the truth, which is the only input worth planning on.**

Four rules keep it honest at a family-founded company, where blame is unusually expensive and unusually easy:

1. **Cause, not culprit.** "The activation KR missed because onboarding depended on one person's evenings" is a finding; "because X was slow" is a fight.
2. **Every learning becomes an edit to an artefact within the same week** — a changed SOP ([[15-sops-and-playbooks|lesson 15]]), a new scorecard row, a revised template, a risk-register score. A learning that stays in the plan doc has been _noticed_, not learned.
3. **Performance conversations happen in 1:1s, never in the retro.** If someone genuinely under-delivered, that belongs in [[T04-one-on-one-template|T04]] with the CEO or COO — separately, privately, and with specifics.
4. **Score the mechanism, not the effort.** Everyone worked hard; that is not information. The useful question is which mechanism was missing, and whether it now exists.

One more retro habit, borrowed from the same postmortem practice: **read last quarter's learnings out loud at the start of this quarter's retro.** Repeating a learning three quarters running is itself the finding — the mechanism was never actually built.

## 8. Money in the room — budget vs actual and the forecast re-base

Rocks cost money, and a quarter planned without the money in the room is a wish list. Two instruments come to the session, both already running monthly and weekly from [[T20-budget-vs-actual-and-cash-forecast|T20]] — the quarterly is where they get _re-based_ rather than merely read.

**Budget vs actual, three months at a glance.** Lay the quarter's three BvA months side by side by category (People · Cloud/infra · SaaS/tools · Sales & marketing · Legal/compliance/CA · Office/admin · Other) and read the **pattern**, not the month. A single spike is a one-off with a sentence beside it; the same line over budget three months running is a wrong budget, and the fix is to change the number rather than to keep writing the same commentary. Three quarterly decisions come out of that reading:

- **Re-base the category budgets** that reality has overtaken — up or down, with the reason recorded, so next quarter's variances mean something again.
- **Split anything hiding in "Other"** that has grown two months running ([[T20-budget-vs-actual-and-cash-forecast|T20]]'s rule 4). At this size "Other" is usually two real categories in a trench coat.
- **Price each proposed rock** before it becomes a rock: what it adds to which category, this quarter and after. "Roll out Frappe Helpdesk" is a hosting line and a training week; "hire the Support/Ops Associate" is a People line that never goes away. A rock with no cost line has not been thought about.

**The 13-week forecast, re-based to the new quarter.** The Monday habit rolls the grid a week at a time; the quarterly session re-lays it across the _next_ thirteen weeks with the annual meteors put in their real weeks from [[T14-vendor-and-tool-register|T14]]'s renewal dates — audit fee, insurance premiums, store memberships, the advance-tax instalment — plus whatever the new rocks add. Then read the two numbers aloud: **the trough and the week it falls in.** If a rock pushes the trough below the red line ([[finance/07-phase-7-bootstrapping-runway-burn|Finance Phase 7]]'s rule), the quarter is over-committed, and the room decides _in the session_ which rock moves — not in week 9 when the bank balance decides for you.

Two rules keep this from becoming a finance meeting. **Timing beats totals:** the year's budget can be affordable while a single week is not, which is exactly why the grid rather than the average is in the room ([[06-money-rails-and-finance-operations|lesson 06]]). And **the last month of the quarter is not closed yet** (§6) — plan on two closed months plus your own management numbers, mark the third as provisional in the plan doc, and re-read it at the next monthly review rather than pretending to precision you do not have.

## 9. The monthly update — written even when nobody is asking for it

VSYST is bootstrapped. There is no board demanding a deck, no investor asking for numbers by the 10th. **Write the update anyway** — one page, every month, in [[T23-board-and-investor-update|T23]]'s shape: TL;DR, KPIs against plan, cash and runway, wins, misses, asks, hiring, product, risks, next month. It takes forty minutes when the scorecard, the BvA and the plan doc already exist, which by now they do.

It earns its forty minutes four times over:

| What it buys            | How it works                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------- |
| **Discipline**          | Writing "activation was 38% against a 60% target" to someone outside the room is a different act from noticing it on a sheet. A number you must explain in prose is a number you understand. The written form is the mechanism; the meeting is not ([[16-metrics-dashboards-and-scorecards                                | lesson 16]]'s "numbers before narrative" again)                                                               |
| **Outside eyes**        | Send it to the CA, a mentor, an advisor, the domain contacts who know fuel retail. People who see twelve companies spot the thing you have stared past for a month — and unlike a conversation, a written update lets them answer at leisure, with specifics                                                              |
| **The archive**         | Twelve honest monthly updates _are_ the company's history: what you believed, what happened, what you did about it. Reconstructing that from WhatsApp in month 18 is impossible; it is also exactly what a diligence process asks for                                                                                     |
| **Fundraise readiness** | The day you decide to raise — the Startup India Seed Fund route, an angel, a strategic — the first thing asked for is traction over time and a founder who can narrate it. Twelve updates written _before_ anyone asked are far more convincing than a deck written after ([[finance/08-phase-8-fundraising-and-cap-table | Finance Phase 8]]; the path from pre-revenue to profitable is [[finance/10-phase-10-pre-revenue-to-profitable | Finance Phase 10]]) |

Four rules, all learned the same way. **One page, one screen** — an update nobody finishes is a file, not a communication. **Misses before wins**, or at minimum in the same size type: an update that only reports wins trains its readers to stop reading, and trains you to stop noticing. **Every update carries an ask**, even a small one — an introduction to a transporter fleet, a view on a contract clause, a name for a support hire; the ask is what converts a broadcast into a relationship. And **send it by a fixed date** — the 10th, once the close pack has landed — because a monthly artefact without a date drifts to quarterly and then to never.

The update is also the quarterly's raw material: three monthly updates read end to end at the start of the planning session is the most efficient quarter review that exists, and the misses column, read three months at a time, usually names next quarter's rocks before anyone proposes one.

## 10. Strategy hygiene — diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent action

The CEO owns strategy; the COO owns whether the plan is one. That is a narrow, useful job, and it needs exactly one framework. Richard Rumelt's **kernel** holds that a good strategy has three parts — a **diagnosis** that names what is actually going on, a **guiding policy** for dealing with it, and a set of **coherent actions** that carry the policy out — and that a list of goals, however ambitious, is not a strategy ([Alex Murrell — Good Strategy Bad Strategy summary](https://www.alexmurrell.co.uk/summaries/richard-rumelt-good-strategy-bad-strategy); [Lenny's Newsletter — Good Strategy, Bad Strategy](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/good-strategy-bad-strategy-richard)). [[02-how-a-coo-thinks|Lesson 02]]'s reading canon put the book here for a reason: **this is the section where the OKR machinery gets its sanity check.**

Run the three parts over the annual plan before it becomes four quarters of rocks:

| Part of the kernel   | The question to ask in the room                                                                                       | What a bad answer sounds like                                                                                                                                               |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Diagnosis**        | What is the _one_ thing standing between us and the next stage — named as an obstacle, not an aspiration?             | "We need to grow." That is a wish. "Dealers sign up and never reach their first invoice, so nothing compounds" is a diagnosis                                               |
| **Guiding policy**   | Given that, what is our approach — and what does it rule _out_?                                                       | A policy that forbids nothing is not a policy. "Win one district completely before opening a second" rules out scattering the domain director across three cities           |
| **Coherent actions** | Do this quarter's rocks all serve the policy, and do they support each other rather than compete for the same person? | Five rocks pulling three directors in five directions is a list, not a strategy — the actions must be _coherent_, which at this size mostly means "few, and they reinforce" |

Two failure modes are worth naming because a small company hits them constantly. **Goals-as-strategy** is the OKR system's own occupational disease: a beautifully formatted sheet of objectives with no diagnosis behind it, where "3× revenue" stands in for a theory of why revenue would triple. Ask of any objective: _what does this assume is true?_ If nobody can answer, the objective is a target with no mechanism, and it will be missed politely for four quarters. **Fluff** is the second — strategy statements assembled from words that survive because they cannot be tested. The test is Rumelt's: could a competent competitor state the _opposite_ as their approach? If not, you have written a platitude.

The COO's move here is small and repeatable: **the plan doc's first line is the diagnosis, in one sentence, in plain words.** Every rock beneath it must trace to that sentence — and the tracing happens out loud, in the session, before the rocks are agreed. It costs ten minutes a quarter and it is the difference between a company that gets things done and a company that gets the _right_ things done.

## 11. Saying no — the quarter is where things get killed

Everything above is addition — priorities, rocks, key results, budget lines. A planning system that only adds is a system that runs a three-person company into the ground by month nine. **The quarterly session is the company's only scheduled opportunity to subtract**, and it is the half of planning that founders skip.

The mechanism is one sentence: **one in, one out.** A new rock proposed mid-quarter does not join the list; it displaces a named rock, whose owner says out loud what stops. The same rule scales down to everything else the operating system accumulates — a new weekly meeting retires one, a new scorecard row retires one ([[16-metrics-dashboards-and-scorecards|lesson 16]]'s 5–15 band is a budget, not a suggestion), a new tool retires one ([[14-vendors-procurement-and-cost-control|lesson 14]]'s audit), a new SOP retires or absorbs one ([[15-sops-and-playbooks|lesson 15]] §6). Without the displacement clause every list in this course grows monotonically, and a list that only grows is eventually ignored in full.

So the session carries a **stop list** alongside the rock list. Everyone brings their own candidates (§5's last prep line), and four questions find them:

- **What did we do every week this quarter that changed nothing?** A report nobody read, a meeting with no decision, a metric nobody acted on.
- **What is here because of a decision that is no longer true?** Re-read the last quarter's [[T09-decision-log-and-adr|decision log]] — decisions have review dates for exactly this.
- **Which rock is 30% done for the second quarter running?** That is not a rock behind schedule; it is a rock the company has already decided against and has not said so. Kill it explicitly, and note what you learned.
- **What would we not start today?** The sunk cost is gone either way; the only question is whether it earns next quarter's hours.

Then say no in a way that survives contact. Three ways that work at VSYST's size: **"not this quarter" with a date** — a real parking lot in the plan doc, reviewed next session, which is a promise rather than a brush-off; **"yes, if" with the trade named** — "yes to the IOCL pilot work, if the billing rock moves to Q3", which converts an argument about enthusiasm into a decision about capacity; and **"no, and here is the diagnosis"** — the §10 sentence is the most powerful no available, because it makes the refusal about the company's theory rather than about the person asking.

Two rules make the no stick. **Every kill is logged** ([[T09-decision-log-and-adr|T09]]) with its reason and a review date — otherwise the same idea returns in three quarters, freshly enthusiastic, and nobody remembers why it lost. And **the CEO says the no on strategy, the COO says the no on capacity.** Confusing those two is how a COO ends up appearing to veto the founder's ambitions; naming them keeps the disagreement where it belongs, which is the [[T01-ceo-coo-operating-agreement|operating agreement]]'s territory.

## 12. At VSYST — applying this now

- **Start with a quarter, not a year.** Do not wait for March. Book the next quarter-end half-day now, run it from [[T19-quarterly-plan-and-review|T19]], and treat this first one as the practice run — the annual plan will be better in March for having had two quarterlies behind it. The first session's honest output is usually three rocks and a shock at how few fit.
- **The first quarter's rocks pick themselves from what this course has half-built.** Illustrative, and exactly the shape they should take — a deliverable in the room, a number in the sheet: _"Support runs without the founders"_ (KR: 90% of tickets first-answered by Support/Ops within SLA, founders on escalation only); _"Dealer activation becomes a mechanism"_ (KR: activation rate — ≥1 customer linked and ≥1 invoice in 14 days — from its measured baseline to a target you can defend); _"The compliance calendar has never been late"_ (KR: 13 of 13 items on or before the due date — a committed KR, so 1.0 or a real miss). Note what is _not_ a rock: filings, the weekly meeting, the scorecard fill. Those are the floor.
- **Pricing is a decision, not a KR.** Monetisation direction is set — company-level subscription per GSTIN, users unlimited, billing on the web, the app gated server-side — but the prices are not. A revenue KR that depends on prices nobody has set is a KR that will be renegotiated in week 6. Make the _decision_ the rock ("subscription pricing decided and logged in [[T09-decision-log-and-adr|T09]] by week 4"), and let revenue KRs start the quarter after that, from the [[app-store-economics/11-cost-model-worksheet|cost-to-serve worksheet]] and the [[app-store-economics/08-dzzlo-subscription-strategy|subscription strategy]] rather than from hope. **Never quote a price as a fact until that decision exists.**
- **Partnerships get next-step KRs, not outcome KRs.** The IOCL and bank/gateway conversations are in discussion, and their timing belongs to other organisations. A KR like "IOCL agreement signed" hands your quarter's score to someone else's committee; "2 written next-step commitments recorded in the partnership tracker, each with a date" measures the only thing you actually control ([[11-revenue-operations-and-partnerships|lesson 11]]'s discipline).
- **Plan on the Indian financial year, and around the CA.** The annual day is in **March**, before the April–March year turns; the quarterly days fall in the last weeks of June, September, December and March, all four booked in one sitting alongside the access, vendor and risk audits ([[08-the-operating-cadence|lesson 08]] §7). Advance-tax instalments, quarterly TDS returns and the post-year-end ROC ring sit near those same dates — **VERIFY LIVE** with the CA/CS each April ([[T16-compliance-calendar|T16]]) — which is precisely why the session is a half day and compliance work is never a rock.
- **Start the monthly update this month, addressed to the CA and one mentor.** Twelve of them written before anyone asks is the artefact that makes a Startup India Seed Fund application, an angel conversation or a mentor's introduction credible. Bootstrapped is a funding state, not an excuse for an unaudited memory.
- **What not to do:** no OKR software (the `OKR Tracker` tab and [[T06-okr-planning-sheet|T06]] are the tool); no cascade into personal OKRs at three to five people; no scores in anyone's pay conversation this year; no second plan running beside the rocks; and no quarter that ends without a written retro — the retro is the only part of this lesson that compounds.

## 13. Exercises

**13.1 — Book the four sessions and write the prep list (20 min, calendar open).** Put the four quarterly half-days into the shared calendar for the next twelve months — last week of June, September, December and March — with the March one blocked as a full day (planning plus the operating-system audit of [[20-the-autopilot-test-and-scaling-the-machine|lesson 20]]). Attach the access, vendor and risk audits to the same weeks ([[08-the-operating-cadence|lesson 08]] §7). Then paste §5's prep checklist into the invite body with a name against each line and the 48-hour rule stated. Invitations without the prep list produce sessions where people read for the first hour.

**13.2 — Run the first quarterly and leave with a plan doc (4 hours, this quarter-end).** Follow [[T19-quarterly-plan-and-review|T19]]'s agenda in order — review, then plan, then money and people, then decisions — and do not let planning start before the retro finishes. Leave the room with: **3–5 rocks**, one owner each and a written finish line; each rock's finish line entered as a key result in [[T06-okr-planning-sheet|T06]] and the `OKR Tracker` tab of [the workbook](toolkit/vsyst-coo-workbook.xlsx) with baseline, target and confidence; **the first two weeks of to-dos per rock**; the re-based category budgets and the re-laid 13-week grid with the trough named ([[T20-budget-vs-actual-and-cash-forecast|T20]]); the stop list; and the plan doc saved at `quarters/QN-YYYY.md` **before anyone leaves**. Add the plan's one-sentence diagnosis (§10) as the doc's first line. On the next working Monday, read the rocks in the ops meeting's Rocks segment — that is the loop closing.

**13.3 — Write the first monthly update (45 min, after the close pack lands).** One page in [[T23-board-and-investor-update|T23]]'s shape: TL;DR, KPIs against plan from the scorecard, cash and runway from the `Runway & 13-Week Cash` tab, wins, **misses first**, hiring, product, risks, next month, and one real ask. File it at `updates/YYYY-MM.md` and send it to the CA and one mentor or advisor. Put the fixed send date — the 10th — in the calendar as a recurring task with your name on it; the second update is the one that makes it a mechanism.

**13.4 — Score last quarter honestly, if you have one (30 min, before 13.2).** If a quarter of rocks already exists in any form — even an informal list — score each finish line 0.0–1.0 with a one-line why, self-scored by its owner, and mark which were committed and which were stretch. Then write the three-column retro (wins with the mechanism that produced them, misses with causes, learnings) and turn **every learning into one edit to a real artefact within the same week** — an SOP, a scorecard row, a risk score, a template. A retro whose learnings do not change a document has not happened.
