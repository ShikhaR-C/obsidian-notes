# 18 — Automation and AI in Operations

_Phase 4 · Autopilot · Months 6–18. After this lesson you can apply the eliminate → simplify → standardise → automate → delegate ladder to any recurring task, score an automation candidate on hours saved per year against build-and-maintain cost, keep an automation backlog that says what gets built next and by whom, choose between a cron job, an ERPNext rule, an Apps Script, an n8n flow, a WhatsApp template and an AI agent, write the guardrails that keep agents away from money and customer inboxes, and ship VSYST's first two automations without automating a mess._

## 1. The ladder — eliminate, simplify, standardise, automate, delegate

The instinct on meeting a painful recurring task is to automate it. That instinct is right about the task and wrong about the order. The ladder from [[02-how-a-coo-thinks|lesson 02]] §11 has five rungs, and they run in one direction only:

| Rung               | The question to ask                                                            | Done looks like                                                               | A VSYST example                                                                                                |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **1. Eliminate**   | Does this need to exist at all? Who reads it, and what breaks if it stops?     | The task is gone — zero build cost, zero maintenance, forever                 | The weekly summary nobody opened; four of twelve columns on a sheet; a report duplicated in WhatsApp and email |
| **2. Simplify**    | What is the smallest version that still does the job?                          | Fewer steps, fewer inputs, fewer people touching it                           | Seven numbers instead of twenty; one channel instead of three ([[10-customer-operations-support-and-success    | lesson 10]] §2)                                                             |
| **3. Standardise** | Is it done the same way, at the same time, by the same rule, every time?       | A written SOP with a trigger, steps, checks and an owner ([[T08-sop-template  | T08]])                                                                                                         | SOP-OPS-002 weekly scorecard update; SOP-CUS-002 dealer onboarding          |
| **4. Automate**    | Can a script, an ERPNext rule, a formula or an agent run the standard version? | A machine performs the steps; a human reads exceptions                        | The digest, the dunning ladder, the compliance alerts — §8                                                     |
| **5. Delegate**    | Now that it is small, boring and documented — who owns it, not you?            | A named owner on the ladder of [[17-delegation-decision-rights-and-org-design | lesson 17]] §1                                                                                                 | Support/Ops owns the onboarding bot's outputs; the COO reads the exceptions |

**Never automate a mess.** Automating a bad process buys you a fast bad process, plus a permanent maintenance bill, plus a new problem: the mess is now inside a script where nobody can see it. Every hour spent on rungs 1–3 removes work that rung 4 would otherwise have to encode; the two cheapest rungs cost nothing to run and nothing to maintain, which no automation can ever match.

The [[index|landing page]]'s rule runs in exactly that order — _twice, write it down; weekly, put it on the scorecard; daily, automate it_ — because **the SOP is the specification**. If you cannot hand someone a document saying what happens, in what order, with what checks, you do not yet know what you are asking the machine to do, and it will encode your confusion faithfully. Hence the test before any build: _show me the SOP id._ No id, no build.

**Delegate sits after automate deliberately** — you hand over the automated, documented, boring version of a job, not the manual one. And the ladder is re-run rather than run once: every SOP review ([[15-sops-and-playbooks|lesson 15]] §6) is a chance to eliminate a step you have merely been tolerating.

## 2. What to automate first

The first automations are not the clever ones. They are **frequent, rule-shaped, low-judgement and cheap to check** — six families cover almost everything a company this size should build in its first year:

| Family                     | What it is                                                | At VSYST                                                                                                                | Why it is safe to start here                                                              |
| -------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Reminders and nudges**   | A machine remembers so no human has to                    | Rate-window nudges; compliance alerts at 30/7/1 days ([[T16-compliance-calendar                                         | T16]]); renewal alerts 60 days out ([[14-vendors-procurement-and-cost-control             | lesson 14]])                                                                                              | The output is a message a human reads; a wrong reminder costs a moment, not money |
| **Reports and digests**    | Assembling numbers whose definitions already exist        | The Monday scorecard fill from the saved Mongo queries ([[16-metrics-dashboards-and-scorecards                          | lesson 16]] §5); the Friday cash snapshot                                                 | The queries are already written and version-controlled — you are automating the copying, not the thinking |
| **Reconciliations**        | Comparing two lists, reporting only the differences       | Bank statement against ERPNext payments; invoices raised against vouchers posted                                        | Machines are simply better at this than people, and the output is a short exceptions list |
| **Data entry and copying** | Moving the same fact from system A to system B            | New tenant into the onboarding sheet; ticket counts into the scorecard; lead into the CRM                               | The most error-prone human task there is, and every error is silent                       |
| **Triage and routing**     | Labelling and assigning, so the right human sees it first | Support tickets by severity and topic ([[10-customer-operations-support-and-success                                     | lesson 10]] §4); alerts to the on-call engineer                                           | The machine sorts; the human still answers, so a bad sort costs minutes                                   |
| **Onboarding steps**       | A checklist that creates and chases itself                | New-tenant day-1/3/7/14 nudges and the no-invoice-by-day-14 flag; new-employee access tasks ([[T12-onboarding-checklist | T12]])                                                                                    | The steps are already written, dated and owned                                                            |

The screening question is the landing page's: **does it happen daily?** Daily work is where the hours hide — fifteen minutes a day is over sixty hours a year, and nobody notices them leaving. Weekly work qualifies when a single run is long (the ninety-minute scorecard fill); monthly work rarely qualifies on hours alone, and gets built for a different reason (§3).

The **not yet** list is worth writing down so the argument happens once rather than every quarter: **judgement-heavy work** (a churn-save call, a price negotiation) gets a playbook, not a script ([[15-sops-and-playbooks|lesson 15]] §2); **anything still changing weekly** would only be a draft encoded and rebuilt next month; **anything that runs a few times a year** gets a reminder rather than an action, unless missing it carries a penalty; **anything with no owner** is by definition unmonitored; and **anything whose failure you could not detect** is a liability, not a saving (§7's fail-loud rule).

## 3. The ROI rule — hours saved × frequency versus build plus maintain

Automation is a capital decision: you spend hours once to save minutes many times. The arithmetic is small enough to do in your head — and doing it there is what stops a month disappearing into a clever build nobody needed.

```
runs per year        daily ≈ 250 (working days) · weekly 52 · monthly 12 · quarterly 4 · yearly 1
hours saved per year = minutes per run × runs per year ÷ 60
true cost            = build hours + maintenance (budget ~20% of build hours every year) + any vendor cost
ROI                  = hours saved per year ÷ build hours
```

Those are the formulas behind the `Automation Backlog` tab of [the workbook](toolkit/vsyst-coo-workbook.xlsx): enter frequency and minutes per run, and the sheet computes runs per year, hours saved and ROI. Two illustrative comparisons, both from §8:

- **Rate-window reminder digest:** 15 minutes a day × 250 days = **62.5 hours a year**, against 12 build hours → **ROI ≈ 5.2**. Build it this quarter.
- **Incident status broadcast:** 40 minutes × 12 runs = **8 hours a year**, against 10 build hours → **ROI ≈ 0.8**. On hours alone it fails — and you build it anyway, for the reasons below.

Hours are not the only currency, and three other returns belong in the Notes column when they apply. **Consistency under stress:** the incident broadcast exists so the worst message of the quarter is written calmly in advance, not at 2 AM by whoever is most frightened. **Latency:** a nightly reconciliation finds a problem eleven days earlier than a monthly one. **The error avoided:** a missed GST or ROC date costs interest, late fees and your standing with the CA ([[13-compliance-calendar-risk-and-insurance|lesson 13]]) — orders of magnitude more than the six hours a year the reminder saves.

The decision rule VSYST uses: **build when ROI ≥ 3 and risk is low or medium with a guardrail; build regardless of ROI when the error avoided is statutory or customer-visible; park everything else in the backlog** — parked, not refused, because next quarter's volumes change the arithmetic.

One honest line to keep in view: **every automation is a small permanent liability.** APIs change, credentials expire, formats drift, and the person who built it moves on. That is what the 20% maintenance budget and the owner column are for — and it is also the strongest argument for rungs 1 and 2 of §1, which create no liability at all.

## 4. The automation backlog

Candidates arrive at random — in the ops meeting, in the support queue, at 11 PM when something annoys you. Without one list, either all of them get built or none do. The **automation backlog** is that list, in the `Automation Backlog` tab of [the workbook](toolkit/vsyst-coo-workbook.xlsx):

| Column                 | What goes in it                                                                                                |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Task**               | One line, concrete: what happens, to whom, when                                                                |
| **Frequency**          | Daily / Weekly / Monthly / Quarterly / Yearly — the sheet turns it into runs per year                          |
| **Minutes per run**    | The honest current cost — finding, copying and chasing included                                                |
| **Hours / yr saved**   | Computed (§3)                                                                                                  |
| **Risk**               | Low / Medium / High by blast radius, not difficulty: does it touch money, personal data or a customer's phone? |
| **Human in loop?**     | Y/N — mandatory Y for anything in §7's first guardrail                                                         |
| **Tool**               | The §5 choice, decided before the build starts                                                                 |
| **Owner**              | One name. The person who fixes it at 7 AM when it breaks                                                       |
| **Status**             | Idea → Backlog → Building → Live → Retired                                                                     |
| **Build effort (hrs)** | Your estimate, doubled if it involves a vendor API you have not used                                           |
| **ROI**                | Computed; the sort key                                                                                         |
| **Notes**              | The SOP id it automates, the scorecard row it should move, and any non-hours reason from §3                    |

Five rules keep the list a mechanism rather than a wish-board:

- **No row without an SOP id.** If the process is not standardised, the row's real next action is to write the SOP (§1).
- **One automation in `Building` at a time.** Two half-built scripts save nothing and break twice.
- **Score before you build.** Ninety seconds of arithmetic kills half of what feels urgent at 11 PM.
- **The quarterly plan picks the next two.** Automation is a rock, not a hobby ([[19-planning-okrs-and-the-quarterly-rhythm|lesson 19]]); `Building` rows carry an owner and a date into the weekly ops meeting.
- **Retire deliberately.** When the process dies the automation is switched off and the row marked Retired — an orphaned script messaging people nobody is reading is the worst artefact in this lesson.

## 6. AI agents in operations — what they are actually good at

An **AI agent** is a language model given tools, a task and permission to take several steps on its own. The difference from a script is the difference between a rule and a judgement: a script needs the input to be shaped exactly as expected; an agent copes with a messy ticket, a scanned invoice or a rambling voice note — and gives you an answer that is _usually_ right, which is a different kind of promise.

That distinction is the whole of the 2026 advice. BCG frames the role as the COO **becoming the AI orchestrator**, partnering with the CTO on platforms and make-versus-buy ([BCG, 2026](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2026/coo-role-becomes-ai-orchestrator)). Bain's 2025 technology report puts agents running multi-step workflows at the top of the list and warns that within twelve months agentic AI "will require centralized control planes providing visibility and governance across human and agent workflows" ([Bain, 2025](https://www.bain.com/insights/state-of-the-art-of-agentic-ai-transformation-technology-report-2025/)) — at your size that control plane is §4's backlog and §7's register, not a product. IMD tells COOs to aim agents at high-value problems, build a small cross-functional "nerve center", and champion **human-in-the-loop**, because generative AI's non-determinism "can feel alien in an environment founded on repeatability" ([IMD, 2026](https://www.imd.org/ibyimd/artificial-intelligence/ai-and-the-coo-operational-excellence-in-the-ai-era/)). And the adoption numbers make this table stakes rather than frontier work: COO involvement in AI strategy rose from 2% in 2024 to 41% in 2025 ([Larridin, 2026](https://larridin.com/blog/coo-ai-strategy)); 54% of CFOs call integrating AI agents in finance a transformation priority and 87% call AI extremely or very important to finance operations in 2026 ([Deloitte Q4 2025 CFO Signals](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/about/press-room/deloitte-q4-2025-cfo-signals-survey.html)); McKinsey finds around 10% of enterprise functions using agents ([Forbes, 2026](https://www.forbes.com/sites/josipamajic/2026/03/22/10-of-enterprise-functions-use-ai-agents-mckinsey-finds/)); roughly 80% of early-stage SaaS startups already used AI tools in 2024 ([HubSpot](https://www.hubspot.com/startups/ai/ai-stats-for-startups)); and the bootstrapped version of the thesis is that back-office automation is the quiet leverage that stops the team growing linearly with revenue ([Bolder Apps, 2026](https://www.bolderapps.com/blog-posts/back-office-automation-apps-2026)).

Six jobs are worth handing to an agent at VSYST today. Note what every row has in common:

| Job                                           | What the agent produces                                                                             | What the human still does                                                                               |
| --------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------- |
| **Weekly report drafting**                    | A first draft of the WBR ([[T28-weekly-business-review                                              | T28]]) from the filled scorecard plus the week's notes: what moved, what is red, candidate explanations | Checks every number against the sheet, replaces the guessed "why" with the real one, decides |
| **Support-ticket triage and FAQ drafts**      | Severity and topic labels, a suggested macro, a draft reply in the dealer's language                | Reads and sends. Always ([[10-customer-operations-support-and-success                                   | lesson 10]] §4)                                                                              |
| **Document checks**                           | "This vendor invoice has no GSTIN"; contract read against [[14-vendors-procurement-and-cost-control | lesson 14]]'s checklist — term, auto-renew, liability cap, data, exit                                   | Signs, pays, negotiates — and gets the legal read                                            |
| **Meeting notes → action items**              | Owner, action and date extracted from the ops-meeting notes into the to-do list                     | Confirms each owner accepted it, in the room                                                            |
| **SOP drafting from a walkthrough recording** | A first-draft SOP in [[T08-sop-template                                                             | T08]]'s shape from a screen recording or transcript of someone doing the task                           | Runs it, fixes the missing steps, signs it off ([[15-sops-and-playbooks                      | lesson 15]] §7) |
| **Code-review assist**                        | A first pass on a pull request — tests, obvious defects, checklist items                            | The CEO/CTO still approves and merges ([[tasks_12_tdd_testing/00-overview                               | tasks_12]])                                                                                  |

Every row is a **draft**, and every row ends with a human. That is the rule in six words: **an agent drafts; a human sends.** What agents are reliably bad at is worth stating with equal clarity: arithmetic on money without a source query behind it; anything that requires a guarantee rather than a probability; deciding; and any task where a confident wrong answer costs more than no answer at all — which is most of compliance, all of legal, and every message a dealer will act on.

## 7. Guardrails

Six guardrails, written once and applied to every automation and every agent. None takes more than an hour to set up at three people; all are expensive to retrofit at thirty.

1. **Human-in-the-loop for money, legal and customer-facing sends.** Nothing that moves money, signs anything, files anything or reaches a dealer or customer goes out without a person approving that specific send. This is the rule [[07-tools-and-it-foundation|lesson 07]] §9 already set, and it is the one IMD tells COOs to champion. Maker–checker on the bank ([[06-money-rails-and-finance-operations|lesson 06]]) is the same principle with a longer history.
2. **The data-classification policy decides what an agent may see.** Lesson 07 §9's four classes, unchanged: public and internal data in company-paid tools; confidential only where the vendor's terms exclude training on your data (**VERIFY LIVE**); **restricted personal data — dealer and customer records, phone numbers, ledgers, credentials — never**, absent a written processor agreement with DPDP clauses, and then only the minimum. Your dealers' customer data is not yours to paste anywhere: under the DPDP Act 2023 and its 2025 Rules you are a processor for it — security safeguards, retained logs, a 72-hour detailed breach report — while CERT-In's 6-hour incident reporting and 180-day log retention apply in parallel; duties and dates **VERIFY LIVE** with the lawyer ([[13-compliance-calendar-risk-and-insurance|lesson 13]]; [Scrut — DPDP Rules](https://www.scrut.io/post/dpdp-rules); [CERT-In 6-hour mandate](https://sirilawllp.com/a-comprehensive-guide-to-indias-cert-in-6-hour-cyber-incident-reporting-mandate/)).
3. **Least privilege, one credential per automation.** Read-only wherever reading is enough — the scorecard filler never needs write access to production. Separate credentials per job are what make the kill switch real.
4. **The automation register.** One row per automation, kept beside [[T14-vendor-and-tool-register|T14]]: what it does, trigger, systems and credentials it uses, data class it touches, human-in-the-loop yes/no, owner, kill switch, review date. This is the "control plane" a company of three actually needs.
5. **Audit log, and fail loud.** Every run records what it did, to whom, and with which template; every failure alerts a human in the same channel as an incident. If you cannot answer "what did it send last Tuesday, and to how many dealers?", it is not ready to send. A silent failure is worse than a manual process.
6. **Kill switch and caps.** One named person can disable every automation and revoke every agent credential in five minutes, and has practised it. Every sender carries a daily cap and a test mode; the first live run goes to your own number.

Governance is where most organisations are behind — 77% report an AI-governance adoption gap, and only 43% of CFOs in Deloitte's Q2 2026 CFO Signals said they were very confident in their AI governance ([Larridin, 2026](https://larridin.com/blog/coo-ai-strategy)). Three people is the cheapest moment in the company's life to write a one-page policy and mean it. Log the decision — which tools, which data classes, which approvals — in [[T09-decision-log-and-adr|T09]], with a review date.

## 8. Five worked VSYST automations

All five sit pre-filled in the `Automation Backlog` tab of [the workbook](toolkit/vsyst-coo-workbook.xlsx). Every minute and hour below is **illustrative** — replace them with measured numbers before sorting by ROI.

**1. Rate-window reminder digest** — _daily · 15 min/run · ≈62 hrs/yr · low risk · ROI ≈ 5 · Developer._
Trigger: 11 PM, inside the 10 PM–6 AM confirmation window. Steps: query tenants with no confirmed rate for tomorrow → push via OneSignal plus a WhatsApp utility template → at 6 AM, a digest of the still-unconfirmed to the Support/Ops channel. Tool: cron on the existing EC2 box. Guardrail: pre-approved templates, one nudge per dealer per window, no sends between 6 AM and 9 PM, kill switch = disable the cron. Why it pays: it moves the rate-confirmation rate, the earliest churn signal on the scorecard ([[16-metrics-dashboards-and-scorecards|lesson 16]] §3).

**2. Weekly scorecard auto-fill from Mongo** — _weekly · 90 min/run · ≈78 hrs/yr · low risk · ROI ≈ 5 · Developer._
Trigger: Sunday night. Steps: run the saved aggregations (active tenants, invoices and GMV, activation cohort, churn) → write them into the `KPI Scorecard` tab via the Sheets API → post a "ready for review" note. Tool: a Node script plus Apps Script. Guardrail: **human-in-the-loop** — the owner still reads and confirms each row before Monday 10:00, so "one owner per number" survives automation; read-only Atlas credentials. Why it pays: it retires the mechanical half of the Monday fill without retiring the reading.

**3. AR dunning sequence** — _weekly · 60 min/run · ≈52 hrs/yr · medium risk · ROI ≈ 6.5 · COO._
Trigger: invoice due date minus 3, due date, +7, +14 days; stops the moment payment is recorded. Steps: ERPNext notification rules send an escalating-tone reminder by email and WhatsApp; day 14 creates a task for the COO to call. Tool: ERPNext auto-reminders and notifications — the [[ERPNext-Implementation-Guide|ERPNext guide]] lists overdue-invoice alerts as a Phase 4 automation. Guardrail: wording approved by the domain-expert director once, then frozen; a hard stop on anything already disputed; no automated legal language, ever. Only switches on once invoicing starts ([[11-revenue-operations-and-partnerships|lesson 11]]).

**4. Incident status broadcast** — _monthly · 40 min/run · ≈8 hrs/yr · medium risk · ROI ≈ 0.8 · Support/Ops._
Trigger: a declared P1 or P2. Steps: one command fills the [[T17-incident-postmortem|T17]] comms template — what is broken, what still works, next update at HH:MM — and posts it to the dealer WhatsApp broadcast, the status page and the internal channel. Tool: an n8n flow. Guardrail: a human approves the wording every time; the template is pre-written; no customer numbers leave the BSP. Built despite the ROI, for §3's second and third returns: the worst message of the quarter is written calmly in advance ([[12-product-and-engineering-operations|lesson 12]] §5).

**5. New-tenant onboarding checklist bot** — _weekly · 45 min/run · ≈39 hrs/yr · low risk · ROI ≈ 3 · Support/Ops._
Trigger: a new tenant is created. Steps: generate the day-stamped onboarding checklist as ERPNext to-dos → schedule day-1/3/7/14 nudges to the owner → flag any tenant with no invoice by day 14 into the weekly ops meeting. Tool: Apps Script or n8n against ERPNext. Guardrail: nudges go to your own team, not the dealer, so a bug is embarrassing rather than public. Why it pays: it feeds the activation rate — ≥1 customer linked and ≥1 invoice within 14 days ([[10-customer-operations-support-and-success|lesson 10]] §7).

A sixth sits in `Building` because it is the cheapest of all: **monthly compliance reminders** — 30/7/1-day alerts from the `Compliance Calendar` tab to the COO and the CA, four build hours in Apps Script, with the annual **VERIFY LIVE** confirmation of dates staying firmly a human step ([[T16-compliance-calendar|T16]]).

## 9. At VSYST — applying this now

- **Start with the compliance reminders, not the clever one.** Four build hours in Apps Script, no customer contact, no money, and it fails safe. Shipping something small teaches the team that automations get owners, registers and review dates — a habit far more valuable than the six hours it saves.
- **Automate only what has an SOP id.** The [[15-sops-and-playbooks|lesson 15]] index is the queue: the processes written first are the processes automatable first, which is another reason the Wednesday build block writes one SOP a week.
- **Buy nothing.** cron, Apps Script, GitHub Actions and ERPNext rules are already paid for; n8n self-hosts on the EC2 box you already run. The only new spend is the WhatsApp BSP you were choosing anyway in [[14-vendors-procurement-and-cost-control|lesson 14]] — **VERIFY LIVE** on message rates before you assume a volume cost.
- **Give the CEO/CTO the kill switch and the register.** Every automation gets a row, a credential of its own and a review date; the technical founder can disable all of them in five minutes. Write that sentence into the register itself.
- **What not to do:** no agent with write access to production or the bank; no AI-generated message reaching a dealer unread by a human; no automation of the dealer relationship or the sales conversation ([[11-revenue-operations-and-partnerships|lesson 11]] — a field-sales business does not scale by broadcast); no scripts on a personal laptop; and nothing built during an incident.
