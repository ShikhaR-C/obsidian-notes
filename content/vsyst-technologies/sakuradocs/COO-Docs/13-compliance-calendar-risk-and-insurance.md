# 13 — Compliance Calendar, Risk and Insurance

_Phase 3 · Build the Machine · Months 2–6 (start the calendar in month one — the first GST/TDS date does not wait). After this lesson you can run VSYST's full-year compliance calendar with the CA/CS as a mechanism with owners and statuses, keep a scored risk register of the twelve ways the company could die, prove the business survives a bad day (backups, credential escrow, a second person per critical task), and buy the right insurance at the right trigger._

## 1. One lesson, three registers

A company can be killed by three kinds of event: the ones **with a date** (a filing falls due, a policy lapses, a renewal passes unnoticed), the ones **without a date** (the server dies, the key person is unreachable, the founders fall out), and the rare ones **too big to absorb** (a lawsuit, a breach, a hospital bill). The COO's answer is one register for each: a **compliance calendar** for the dated obligations, a **risk register** for the undated threats, and **insurance** for the residue that no mitigation can shrink enough. All three are the same discipline — _good intentions don't work; mechanisms do_ — applied to the downside instead of the upside.

Draw the boundary with what you already built. [[05-legal-and-governance-foundation|Lesson 05]] assembled the binder, explained the ROC forms and produced a **governance-only calendar v0**; the registration decisions and depth on GST/TDS mechanics live there, in [[06-money-rails-and-finance-operations|lesson 06]] and in [[finance/05-phase-5-indian-accounting-system|Finance Phase 5]]. This lesson is the **recurring machine**: the full-year calendar that merges governance with tax, labour, data-protection and renewal dates — and, more importantly, the operating routine around it (who watches it, weekly, with what status discipline), because a calendar nobody reads is a list of future fines.

One reality to hold while reading: the syllabus places this lesson in months 2–6, but the [[COO-Docs/index|landing page]] already warned you to read it early — **the first GST or TDS date arrives whether or not you are ready**, and a Pvt Ltd accumulates ROC obligations at ₹100 a day with no cap ([Razorpay Rize — ROC compliance calendar](https://razorpay.com/rize/blogs/roc-compliance-calendar/)). Build the calendar in month one; refine it forever.

## 2. The annual compliance calendar — built with the CA, confirmed every April

Three rules govern everything in this section. **Every date below is "typically"** — written for a 31 March year-end, a 30 September AGM and the law as of August 2026. **Every date is VERIFY LIVE** — deadlines move by notification, and 2026 alone produced a DPT-3 extension after the MCA data-centre fire and a wholesale renumbering of the TDS forms. And the rule that makes the first two safe: **confirm the whole calendar with the CA (and the CS for the ROC rows) every April**, when the financial year opens and the Union Budget's changes have settled. The April confirmation is a calendar row itself, owned by you.

A second framing rule keeps the table honest for a pre-revenue company: several rows are **dormant until a trigger fires** — GST rows activate on registration (voluntary or on crossing ₹20 lakh; the decision memo is in Finance Phase 5), PF at 20 employees, ESI at 10, GSTR-9 above ₹2 crore turnover. Build the row now with its trigger written in; a dormant row costs nothing and a missing one costs a penalty.

## 3. Operating the calendar — who watches it, and how

The tables in §2 become a mechanism only when they live somewhere, carry statuses and get read on a rhythm. The master lives in [[T16-compliance-calendar|T16]] with a working copy in the `Compliance Calendar` tab of [the workbook](toolkit/vsyst-coo-workbook.xlsx); the operating rules are short:

- **One owner per row — an internal name.** "CA" in the owner column means the CA _files_; a VSYST name still _owns_ the row (chases, supplies inputs, checks the acknowledgement). At today's headcount that name is usually yours; the point is that it is never nobody's.
- **Four statuses, nothing fancier:** `upcoming` → `in prep` (inputs being gathered) → `filed` (with the SRN / challan / acknowledgement number typed into the row) → `verified` (acknowledgement PDF stored in the binder, [[T02-company-binder-checklist|T02]]). A row is not done when the CA says "done"; it is done when you hold the proof.
- **The weekly 45-day check.** Five minutes in the weekly ops meeting ([[08-the-operating-cadence|lesson 08]], [[T03-weekly-ops-meeting-agenda|T03]]): read every row due in the next 45 days, say its status aloud, flag anything still `upcoming` inside 15 days as **amber** — amber is an issue for the issues list, not a note to self.
- **The CA and CS are on the calendar, not just in your contacts.** Recurring dates carry a "CA reminded on…" habit; the April sitting (§2) is a booked meeting, and any correction the CA makes is written back into T16 the same day — the calendar is only as good as its last confirmation.
- **A missed or at-risk date is a no-surprises event (§7):** the CEO hears it from you, in writing, the day you know — with the penalty exposure and the recovery plan, not after the late fee arrives.

## 4. The risk register — the twelve ways VSYST dies

A **risk register** is the list of things that could seriously hurt or kill the company, each scored, owned and paired with a mitigation and an **early-warning trigger** — the observable signal that says the risk is turning real. It lives in [[T15-risk-register|T15]] and the `Risk Register` tab of [the workbook](toolkit/vsyst-coo-workbook.xlsx). The scoring is deliberately crude: **likelihood 1–5** (rare → almost certain within a year) × **impact 1–5** (annoyance → kills the company) = a score out of 25. Crude is the feature — the register exists to force a ranking conversation, not to simulate probabilities.

The twelve below are the classic startup risks mapped onto VSYST. Scores are **illustrative** — exercise 9.2 makes you argue them with the other directors, which is where the value is.

The register also feeds two neighbours: risks you can transfer go to §6 (insurance), and risks that fire become incidents with blameless postmortems ([[T17-incident-postmortem|T17]]) whose action items flow back as mitigations.

## 5. Business continuity — surviving the bad day

Insurance pays money after a disaster; **continuity is what keeps the company operating through one.** For a three-to-eight-person company it reduces to three controls you have already met, now held to a standard:

1. **Backups that have been restored.** Lesson 07 §5 built the backup map and lesson 12 §7 gave it a cadence; the continuity standard is simply that the quarterly restore drill is _logged_ — date, who ran it, time to restore — and that a missed drill is an amber on the risk register (risk 6). A backup you have never restored is a hope, not a backup.
2. **Credentials escrow.** If any one person is unreachable, the other two directors can still reach everything: the break-glass Workspace account, Bitwarden emergency access, the sealed quarterly Bitwarden export, signing keys, DSC tokens and the register of who holds what — all per lesson 07 §4 and filed in [[T02-company-binder-checklist|T02]]. Test it the way you test backups: once, deliberately, with the primary person sitting on their hands.
3. **A second person per critical task.** The [[startup-operations-plan|Startup Operations Plan]]'s key-person rule — "make sure every critical task has a second person who can do it, even badly" — turned into a table. For each critical task: primary, second person, and where the written how-to lives. The second person's competence bar is deliberately low; the bar that matters is _has actually done it once, from the notes_.

| Critical task                        | Primary (role) | Second                            | Written how-to                                  |
| ------------------------------------ | -------------- | --------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Deploy / roll back the API           | CEO/CTO        | Dev contractor or COO             | Deploy notes (lesson 12)                        |
| Restore the database                 | CEO/CTO        | COO (drove one drill)             | Restore runbook                                 |
| Pay salaries and vendors             | COO            | CEO (checker role already exists) | Lesson 06 payments calendar                     |
| Answer the support channel           | Support/Ops    | COO                               | Support SOP ([[T25-customer-support-sop\|T25]]) |
| File with the CA/CS                  | COO            | CEO                               | T16 + binder                                    |
| Operate the WhatsApp Business number | Support/Ops    | COO (backup per lesson 07)        | Channel rules (lesson 10)                       |

That table _is_ the continuity plan. Review it at the same quarterly slot as the risk register; the two-week holiday test ([[20-the-autopilot-test-and-scaling-the-machine|lesson 20]]) is its final exam.

## 6. Insurance — transferring what you cannot mitigate

Mitigation shrinks a risk; insurance **transfers the residue** to someone with a bigger balance sheet. The discipline for a bootstrapped company is to buy **by trigger, not by fear** — each cover has a moment when it stops being optional. How to buy, in five rules:

- **Use a broker, not a form.** Startup-focused brokers (Pazcare, Plum, Mitigata, Onsurity) place with the large general insurers (Tata AIG, ICICI Lombard, HDFC Ergo, Bajaj Allianz) and translate exclusions into English. Two quotes minimum (exercise 9.3).
- **Read the exclusions before the premium.** A cyber policy that excludes "failure to maintain security standards" pays nothing if your access reviews lapsed — the controls in lesson 07 §6 are also your claims file.
- **Match E&O limits to your contracts.** If your MSA caps liability at fees paid (lesson 05 §6), tell the broker — it shrinks the premium; if a partner contract demands a minimum cover, that number goes in the quote request.
- **Renewals go on the compliance calendar** (§2, March sweep) with a 60-day-before alert, same as vendors in [[14-vendors-procurement-and-cost-control|lesson 14]]. A lapsed policy discovered during a claim is the worst version of a missed filing.
- **Document as if you will claim.** Incident timelines ([[T17-incident-postmortem|T17]]), the risk register, board minutes — insurers pay on evidence.

## 7. The "no surprises" reporting rule

Lesson 03 §5.6 set the rule between the CEO and COO: **neither of you learns anything material from a third party, a dashboard or a deadline notice** — bad news travels first, fast and in writing. This lesson extends it from the pair to the whole downside of the company, with two mechanical hooks:

- **The weekly business review carries a standing "compliance & risk" line** ([[T28-weekly-business-review|T28]]): anything due in 45 days that is not on track, any risk whose early-warning trigger fired, any policy inside 60 days of renewal. Most weeks the line reads "nothing" — the discipline is that it is _read aloud anyway_, because the week it stops reading "nothing" is the week the rule earns its keep.
- **A fired trigger is reported when it fires, not when it resolves.** A GST date at risk, an OTP delivery-rate slide, a partner asking for IP deeds you don't have — the message goes to the other directors the same day, with your recovery plan attached. The [[startup-operations-plan|ops plan]]'s line about clients — "most clients forgive problems; almost none forgive surprises" — is just as true of co-directors, auditors and insurers.

The deeper point: a COO who reports only good news is hiding the one thing this lesson exists to manage. The registers make honesty cheap — the status column does the confessing for you.
