# 17 — Delegation, Decision Rights and Org Design

_Phase 4 · Autopilot · Months 6–18. After this lesson you can place any task on a five-level delegation ladder and pick the level that matches the person's maturity at that task, name one owner per outcome, fill a RACI for the operating rhythm and a DoA matrix with ₹ thresholds the board has agreed, draw the org tree VSYST will have at 8, 15 and 30 people, write a role charter before you write a job ad, defend the order of the first four hires, and hand a founder's task over one SOP at a time without becoming the new bottleneck._

## Explain-it-like-I'm-5

Back to the dhaba from [[01-what-is-a-coo|lesson 01]]. You hire a boy to help. On day one you say "chop these onions, exactly this thin, and show me." A month later you say "make the dal — tell me before you serve it." A year later you say "you run the kitchen on Tuesdays; I don't need to hear about it." Same boy, same kitchen, three completely different instructions — and the reason they changed is not that you started trusting him as a person. It is that he got good **at that particular task**.

That is delegation: not a switch you flip, but a **ladder you move a task up**, one rung at a time, as the person's skill at that task grows. Two things must be written down for the ladder to work at all. **Who owns this outcome** — exactly one name, so nothing falls between two people. And **what that person may decide alone** — how much money they can spend, what they can sign, which discount they can give — because "ask me first" for everything means you are still the kitchen.

Do that for every task and the shape of the company appears by itself: a list of outcomes with owners is an **org chart**, and a list of what each owner may decide is a **delegation-of-authority matrix**. This lesson builds both, and then plans the next three sizes of the kitchen.

## 1. The delegation ladder — five levels

Most delegation arguments are really a level mismatch. One person thought they were being asked to _do exactly this_; the other thought they had handed over the _whole outcome_. Naming the level in the sentence kills the ambiguity in four words.

| Level                                       | The instruction, in your words                                 | The person may…              | You find out…                            | Use it when                                                                                                                        |
| ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **1 — Do exactly this**                     | "Do it this way, then show me the result."                     | Follow the SOP, nothing else | Immediately, every time                  | The task is new to them, or an error is expensive and irreversible: a GST filing, a payroll run, a rate-master change              |
| **2 — Recommend, I decide**                 | "Look into it and bring me two options with a recommendation." | Investigate, form a view     | Before anything happens                  | You want their judgement to grow on decisions you still own: vendor choice, a refund above the limit, a support policy change      |
| **3 — Decide, then tell me before you act** | "Decide it, tell me, then go — unless I stop you."             | Decide and act after a pause | Just before it happens                   | Their judgement is good but the blast radius is wide: a message to all dealers, a price quoted to a new dealer                     |
| **4 — Act, then tell me**                   | "Do it, then put it in the Monday note."                       | Decide and act at once       | After the fact, routinely                | Routine, reversible, and they have done it right several times: onboarding a dealer, resolving a P2 ticket, paying a listed vendor |
| **5 — Decide and don't tell me**            | "This is yours. I'll see it on the scorecard."                 | Own the outcome entirely     | Only through the number, or an exception | The outcome has an owner, an SOP, a metric and a review; you are reading, not supervising                                          |

Four rules make the ladder work rather than merely look tidy.

- **Say the level out loud when you hand the task over.** "Level 2 on this one" takes a second and prevents the week where you rewrote their work and they stopped trying.
- **Levels belong to tasks, not people.** The same person is level 5 on dealer onboarding and level 1 on a TDS challan. Anyone who is level 5 on _everything_ is either a co-founder or an accident waiting.
- **Move one rung at a time, on evidence.** The evidence is a clean run: the task done from the SOP, the output correct, on the agreed date. Two clean runs is a promotion. Jumping level 1 to level 5 is not trust, it is abandonment — and when it goes wrong the person, not the system, gets blamed.
- **Level 5 is where autopilot lives.** Count your level-5 outcomes each quarter; that count _is_ your progress toward the two-week holiday test ([[20-the-autopilot-test-and-scaling-the-machine|lesson 20]]). Nothing reaches level 5 without a written SOP ([[15-sops-and-playbooks|lesson 15]]) and a number on the scorecard ([[16-metrics-dashboards-and-scorecards|lesson 16]]) — which is exactly why those two lessons come first.

Versions of this ladder appear in most management systems under different names; the five levels above are the ones this course uses, and the only thing that matters is that VSYST uses one vocabulary consistently.

## 2. Task-relevant maturity — the person, the task, the level (Grove)

Which rung a task sits on is not a mood. Andy Grove gives the rule: manage each person according to their **task-relevant maturity (TRM)** — how experienced they are at _this specific task_, not their seniority, their age, or how much you like them. The same person can be high-TRM on React Native and low-TRM on a TDS return, and the right management style is different for each ([High Output Management — notes](https://www.nateliason.com/notes/high-output-management-andy-grove); [summary](https://readingraphics.com/book-summary-high-output-management/)). [[02-how-a-coo-thinks|Lesson 02]] §4 introduced the idea; here it becomes the dial that sets the rung.

| TRM at this task                                                  | Ladder level | How you manage it                                                      | What you check              |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| **Low** — never done it, or done it once with help                | 1–2          | Structured and close: the SOP, a walkthrough, frequent short checks    | The output, every time      |
| **Medium** — done it a few times, clean, with known gaps          | 3–4          | Goals and checks: agree the outcome and the exceptions, then step back | The exceptions and a sample |
| **High** — done it many times, handles the edge cases, teaches it | 5            | Goals and space: the outcome, the number, the review date              | The number on the scorecard |

Three things the dial does that a general "how much do I trust them" feeling cannot. It **explains why close supervision is not an insult** — you are managing the task, not judging the person, and saying so out loud ("you're new to this one, so level 1 for the first two runs") removes the sting. It **resets on change**: a promotion, a new tool, a tenfold jump in volume, or a shift from ten dealers to a hundred all knock TRM down a step at the same task, and the level should follow it down without anyone treating that as a demotion. And it makes the **1:1 the place where levels move** ([[T04-one-on-one-template|T04]]; [[09-people-operations|lesson 09]] §9) — a standing agenda item asking, for one task, whether the evidence supports a rung.

## 3. One owner per outcome — the DRI rule

**Every outcome has exactly one owner — a person, by name.** The abbreviation this course uses is **DRI: directly responsible individual.** The [[COO-Docs/index|landing page]] states the law in five words: _shared ownership is no ownership._ Two names on a row means each waits for the other; a team name on a row means nobody's evening gets ruined when it slips, which is precisely the mechanism that makes things get done.

Four rules make DRIs real rather than decorative:

- **Outcomes, not activities.** "Support" is an activity; "**first response inside SLA and no ticket older than seven days**" is an outcome. The test is whether you could tell, from the scorecard, that the owner had a bad month.
- **The DRI answers for it; they need not do all of it.** The DRI of monthly close does not personally do the CA's job — they own that the close happens by the date, chase what is missing, and escalate early.
- **Write the name where the work lives, not in a separate document.** The owner field in every SOP ([[T08-sop-template|T08]]), the owner column on every scorecard row ([[T05-kpi-scorecard|T05]]), the owner on every risk ([[T15-risk-register|T15]]), vendor ([[T14-vendor-and-tool-register|T14]]) and project charter ([[T18-project-charter|T18]]). A central "who owns what" page goes stale in a month; the field beside the work does not.
- **A DRI is not a blame target.** They are who convenes, decides and reports. When an outcome fails, the postmortem is blameless ([[T17-incident-postmortem|T17]]) — but there is still one person who owed you the news first.

The five-second test: pick any recurring outcome at random — the GST filing, the Play Console account, the rate-master change process, the WhatsApp number — and ask who owns it. If the answer takes longer than five seconds or comes out as two names, you have found this week's fix.

## 4. RACI and DACI at 3–8 people — when to reach for which

DRIs cover outcomes. Two grids cover the rest — the recurring work with several hands in it, and the one-off decision that needs a clean call.

- **RACI** maps a _recurring activity_ to roles: **R**esponsible (does the work), **A**ccountable (one person, owns the result), **C**onsulted (two-way, before), **I**nformed (one-way, after).
- **DACI** maps a _single decision_: **D**river (runs the process), **A**pprover (decides — one), **C**ontributors (supply input), **I**nformed. Use RACI for how work runs; DACI when a specific choice needs making by a date ([Routine — RACI, DACI and RAPID](https://routine.co/blog/posts/raci-daci-rapid-decision-framework); [Argumentree — RAPID vs DACI vs RACI](https://www.argumentree.com/compare/rapid-vs-daci-vs-raci/)).

At three to eight people the grid is not the point — **the argument you have while filling it is the point.** Half the rows will be obvious and half will produce a "wait, I thought _you_ were doing that", which is exactly the value. Fill it once with all three directors in the room, in under an hour.

The rules that keep it usable at this size:

1. **Exactly one A per row, exactly one Approver per decision.** Two A's is the failure the grid exists to prevent.
2. **C is expensive; I is cheap.** Every Consulted is a person who can block by being busy — keep it to two per row and put everyone else in Informed.
3. **One page, ~25 activities, the ones that recur.** [[T07-raci-matrix|T07]] carries VSYST's starter matrix across ops, people, customer, revenue, engineering, finance and compliance, and the `RACI` tab of [the workbook](toolkit/vsyst-coo-workbook.xlsx) holds the live copy. Anything one person does end to end does not need a row — it needs a DRI.
4. **Review it quarterly and after every hire.** A new person is a new column, and half the R's should move into it over their first two quarters — that migration _is_ the delegation programme, visible on one page.

Reach for DACI when a decision has been drifting for weeks: the WhatsApp BSP choice ([[14-vendors-procurement-and-cost-control|lesson 14]]), the first pricing tiers, whether to self-host or use Frappe Cloud. Name a Driver, name one Approver, set the date, and record the outcome in [[T09-decision-log-and-adr|T09]].

## 5. The DoA matrix — who may spend, sign and commit

A **delegation-of-authority matrix (DoA)** answers one question for every kind of commitment: **who may decide this alone, up to what limit, and who must be told.** It is the single most effective anti-bottleneck device in this lesson — without it, "check with me" is the default for everything, and the COO becomes the queue ([[02-how-a-coo-thinks|lesson 02]] §4's negative leverage). The standard shape is spend category × threshold × approver × second approver × what must be documented ([Tallyfy — delegation-of-authority matrix](https://tallyfy.com/delegation-of-authority-matrix-template/); [approval limits](https://tallyfy.com/approval-limits-matrix-template/)).

The VSYST starter, illustrative in every rupee figure — **these numbers are a proposal for the board to ratify, not facts** ([[05-legal-and-governance-foundation|lesson 05]] §on board resolutions; the live copy lives in [[T22-delegation-of-authority-matrix|T22]] and the `DoA Matrix` tab of [the workbook](toolkit/vsyst-coo-workbook.xlsx)):

| Decision                                                                     | Threshold (illustrative)          | Decides alone                                                                           | Second signature           | Informed                                                |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Operating spend (tools, vendors)                                             | ≤ ₹5,000/month                    | COO                                                                                     | —                          | CEO, in the weekly note                                 |
| Operating spend                                                              | ₹5,000–25,000/month               | COO                                                                                     | CEO                        | Board at the next meeting                               |
| Operating spend                                                              | > ₹25,000/month or > ₹3 lakh/year | —                                                                                       | CEO + COO, minuted         | Board decision                                          |
| Capital purchase (laptops, test phones)                                      | ≤ ₹50,000 per item                | COO                                                                                     | CEO                        | —                                                       |
| Any payment out of the bank                                                  | Any amount                        | Nobody alone — **maker–checker** ([[06-money-rails-and-finance-operations\|lesson 06]]) | COO prepares, CEO releases | —                                                       |
| Any payment out of the bank                                                  | > ₹1 lakh                         | —                                                                                       | Two directors              | Board at the next meeting                               |
| Hire a contractor already in [[T21-headcount-plan\|T21]]                     | Within the planned monthly cost   | COO                                                                                     | —                          | CEO                                                     |
| Hire an employee, or any role not in T21                                     | Any                               | —                                                                                       | CEO + COO                  | Board (headcount is a board matter)                     |
| Discount off the quoted rate                                                 | ≤ 10%                             | COO / field sales                                                                       | —                          | Logged in the CRM                                       |
| Discount, free pilot beyond 60 days, or any non-standard term                | > 10%                             | —                                                                                       | CEO                        | Logged in [[T09-decision-log-and-adr\|T09]]             |
| Refund or credit note                                                        | ≤ ₹10,000                         | Support/Ops                                                                             | —                          | COO weekly                                              |
| Refund or credit note                                                        | > ₹10,000                         | —                                                                                       | COO                        | CEO                                                     |
| Vendor contract, standard terms                                              | < ₹1 lakh/year                    | COO                                                                                     | —                          | CEO                                                     |
| Any contract with liability, IP, data or exclusivity clauses                 | Any                               | —                                                                                       | CEO + a legal read         | Board                                                   |
| MoU or partnership (IOCL, bank, gateway)                                     | Any                               | —                                                                                       | Board resolution           | All directors                                           |
| Production deploy                                                            | Planned release                   | CEO/CTO                                                                                 | —                          | COO before the dealer note                              |
| Emergency hotfix during an incident                                          | Any                               | On-call engineer                                                                        | —                          | Everyone within 24 hours, postmortem after              |
| Production data access                                                       | Read-only                         | CEO/CTO grants                                                                          | —                          | Logged in the access review                             |
| Restricted personal data (dealer/customer records)                           | Any                               | Nobody by default                                                                       | CEO + written need         | Recorded ([[07-tools-and-it-foundation\|lesson 07]] §9) |
| Dealer-wide broadcast about pricing, an outage over 1 hour, or a partnership | Any                               | —                                                                                       | CEO approves the wording   | Board                                                   |
| Statutory filing, legal notice, anything signed for the company              | Any                               | Never delegated                                                                         | CEO with CA/CS             | Board                                                   |

Five rules travel with the matrix:

- **Two signatures above the line, always for money leaving the company.** Below the line, one person moves fast; above it, two people are on the hook. At a three-director family company this is not distrust — it is what makes it possible to say "the matrix says so" instead of having a personal argument.
- **The thresholds are deliberately generous at the bottom.** A limit so low that everything crosses it is a matrix that does nothing. Set the small-spend line where you would genuinely not want to be asked, then raise it once a quarter as evidence accumulates.
- **Ratify it in a board meeting and minute it.** Until it is a board resolution it is a suggestion; once minuted it is the company's rule, and it is what your bank, your auditor and any future investor will expect to see ([[05-legal-and-governance-foundation|lesson 05]]).
- **One emergency clause.** Anyone may act outside the matrix to stop harm — a live outage, a safety issue, a payment about to bounce — provided they say so within 24 hours and it is logged in [[T09-decision-log-and-adr|T09]]. Without the clause, people either freeze or ignore the matrix entirely.
- **Review annually, and whenever headcount doubles or a new role appears.** Put the review date in the file. **VERIFY LIVE** any threshold that interacts with statutory limits (cash payments, TDS applicability, board-approval requirements) with the CA/CS before ratifying.

## 6. Org design as the team grows — 3 → 8 → 15 → 30

An org chart is not a hierarchy diagram; it is **the list of outcomes with owners, drawn as a tree**. EOS calls it the accountability chart and insists you design _seats_ before you think about people — a seat is a set of roles someone owns, and at a small company one person holds several ([EOS Toolbox](https://www.rarebirdinc.com/the-eos-toolbox-six-components-that-work-together/)). Draw the next size, not the size after next. The trees live in [[T29-org-chart-and-role-charters|T29]].

**3 people — today. No managers; three peers, seven functions.**

```
CEO / CTO (technical founder)    COO (this seat)                Domain-expert director
└─ product, code, releases,      └─ operations, finance,        └─ dealer relationships,
   infrastructure, app stores       people, compliance,            field sales, industry
                                    customer ops, vendors          and OMC channels
   external: CA/CS, lawyer, freelance designers/testers
```

The risk at this size is not span; it is that one person holds five seats and the boundaries live in nobody's head but theirs. The fix is the whole of Phase 4: write the seats down, then start moving them.

**8 people — the plan you are actually building toward (roughly months 9–18).** The first managers appear here, and all three of them are new at managing.

```
CEO / CTO ─── Developer / QA
COO ─┬─ Support/Ops Associate ── tickets, dealer onboarding, scorecard fill
     └─ Accounts / Admin (part-time, or the CA's staff)
Domain-expert director ─── Field Sales (one district)
```

Span rule at this stage: **three to five reports, maximum** — not because more is theoretically impossible, but because nobody manages more people than they can hold a real weekly 1:1 with ([[T04-one-on-one-template|T04]]), and first-time managers should be at the low end.

**15 people — functions split and a thin layer of leads appears.**

```
CEO ─── Engineering Lead ─┬─ 2–3 developers
                          └─ QA
COO ─┬─ Ops Manager / Chief of Staff ─┬─ Support (2)
     │                                └─ Ops / Admin (1)
     └─ Finance & Compliance (1, with the CA outside)
Domain-expert director (Revenue) ─┬─ Field Sales (2–3, two districts)
                                  └─ Customer Success (1)
```

**Split a function when one person is carrying two jobs that each need full attention** — the tell is a scorecard row that goes red for capacity reasons rather than skill reasons, two months running. Not before: a premature split creates handoffs and an org chart with empty boxes.

**30 people — you manage managers, and stop filling any scorecard row yourself.**

```
CEO ─┬─ CTO / Engineering (8–10)
     ├─ COO ─┬─ Ops & Support (6–8)
     │       ├─ Finance & Compliance (2)
     │       └─ People (1)
     └─ Revenue — Sales + Customer Success (8–10)
```

Two rules for every step. **Add a layer when a manager's exceptions start getting missed**, not when the headcount crosses a number — the symptom is a week where something red went unnoticed for five days. And **every reorg is a logged decision** ([[T09-decision-log-and-adr|T09]]) with the trigger that caused it written down, because the second-order effects of moving a reporting line ([[02-how-a-coo-thinks|lesson 02]] §on second-order thinking) show up two quarters later and you will want the reasoning.

## 7. Role charters — write the seat before you meet the person

**Write the seat before you meet the person** ([[09-people-operations|lesson 09]] §2). A **role charter** is one page — mission, three to five measurable outcomes for the next twelve months, the KPIs (which must be existing scorecard rows), decision rights (which must be lines in the DoA), who they report to, and a named backup. [[T29-org-chart-and-role-charters|T29]] holds the template and filled charters for the COO, Support/Ops Associate, Field Sales and Developer seats.

The charter earns its hour twice. Writing it is **the test of whether the role is real**: if you cannot name three measurable outcomes and point at the scorecard rows they move, you have a pile of chores rather than a seat — split it differently, or wait. And reading it later prevents the small-company failure where a hire "helps everywhere and owns nothing", which ends in a good person leaving because they could never tell whether they were succeeding.

Two habits keep charters honest: the job ad is the charter in public language (never the reverse), and the charter is re-read at the 90-day review and at every quarterly plan, with outcomes updated rather than quietly forgotten.

## 8. The hiring order for a SaaS like DZZLO

The order is **Support/Ops Associate → Field Sales → Developer/QA → Ops Manager or PM/CS**, with the trigger metric for each already written in [[09-people-operations|lesson 09]] §2's table and the costs in [[T21-headcount-plan|T21]] and the `Headcount Plan` tab of [the workbook](toolkit/vsyst-coo-workbook.xlsx). The order is not a convention borrowed from a SaaS blog; it follows the constraint.

- **Support/Ops first, because it frees the scarcest person.** The technical founder currently answers tickets and onboards dealers — hours that come straight out of the only person who can ship. That work is also the most documentable ([[10-customer-operations-support-and-success|lesson 10]]'s SOPs already exist), which makes it the cheapest thing to hand over well.
- **Field sales second, because the constraint moves to demand.** Once onboarding no longer eats the founders, the binding limit is dealer conversations per week in a business that is sold at the counter, one district at a time.
- **Developer third, not first.** Hiring engineering before support means the founder still does tickets _and_ now reviews someone else's code. The honest trigger is cognitive load and slipping cadence (§9), not a wish list.
- **An ops manager or chief of staff last** — the hire that frees _you_, triggered by team size around 8–10 or a calendar audit showing more than half your week in the "doing" column. Kruze's benchmark for the first dedicated ops hire is roughly 10–12 employees ([Kruze](https://kruzeconsulting.com/blog/startup-operations-hire/)); until then the COO seat is the ops function.

The founding-operator's own loop, stated by Andy Sparks, is the same idea pointed at yourself: do the most important thing nobody has been hired for, hire or delegate someone into your place, then move to the next thing ([Startup Grind](https://medium.com/startup-grind/what-s-a-coo-do-bbeb0f8648ee)). If the constraint changes — a large partnership landing, a compliance load arriving — the order changes with it, and the change gets a line in [[T09-decision-log-and-adr|T09]].

## 9. Team Topologies — when engineering is more than two people

When engineering passes two people, borrow one idea and skip the vocabulary. _Team Topologies_ defines four team types — **stream-aligned** (owns a flow of work end to end), **enabling** (helps others get better), **complicated-subsystem** and **platform** — plus three interaction modes (collaboration, X-as-a-service, facilitating), and its organising principle is **designing teams around cognitive load** ([Team Topologies — key concepts](https://teamtopologies.com/key-concepts)).

The applicable part today is cognitive load, and it is a hiring argument rather than an org argument: three repositories (`dzzlo_oms_api`, `dzzlo_oms_app`, `dip-web`), two app stores, the release gate ([[tasks_12_tdd_testing/00-overview|tasks_12]]), the infrastructure and the on-call phone are already more than one person can hold well. That — not ticket volume — is the real case for the developer hire, and it is the number to watch as DZZLO's surface area grows.

When engineering does reach four or five, the first split should be **stream-aligned** (a team that owns the dealer experience end to end, another that owns the customer/DIP side) with the API and infrastructure treated as a thin internal platform consumed as a service. What to avoid is the intuitive split — "frontend team" and "backend team" — which manufactures a handoff for every feature and puts a queue between the dealer and the fix. Until then, VSYST is one stream-aligned team of everyone; say that plainly rather than adopting the language of a structure you do not have.

## 10. Who not how — handing the founders' tasks over, one SOP at a time

The ladder from [[02-how-a-coo-thinks|lesson 02]] §11 — eliminate, simplify, standardise, automate, **delegate** — ends at this section, and its last rung has a name. Dan Sullivan's **"who, not how"**: when work lands, the expensive reflex is "how do I get this done?" and the cheap one is "**who** can own this?" ([Who Not How — summary](https://readingraphics.com/book-summary-who-not-how/)). Cameron Herold puts the identical shift at the centre of the second-in-command's job — from "how quickly can I get this done?" to "who else could do this work?" ([summary](https://www.befreed.ai/book/the-second-in-command-by-cameron-herold)). At three people the honest answer to "who" is often a document plus a part-timer, or a script ([[18-automation-and-ai-in-operations|lesson 18]]) — but it stops being you, which is the entire point.

**The unit of handover is one SOP, not one job.** "Take over support" is a wish; "own SOP-CUS-001, ticket handling, at level 3, from the 14th" is a handover. It is small enough to finish inside a fortnight, small enough that when it wobbles you know exactly which document is at fault, and small enough that the person's confidence compounds instead of collapsing.

Run each one through the three stages [[15-sops-and-playbooks|lesson 15]] §7 already installed — **shadow → run with review → own** — and record it in six columns beside the SOP index:

| Field                                 | What goes in it                                                                            |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Task & SOP id**                     | The process and the document that describes it. No SOP, no handover                        |
| **From → to**                         | Names. "From the CEO/CTO" is the row that matters most in year one                         |
| **Stage & dates**                     | Shadowed on the 3rd, run-with-review for two cycles, owned from the 17th                   |
| **Ladder level, now → target**        | §1's levels: most handovers run 1 → 3 within a month, 3 → 5 over a quarter                 |
| **Decision rights that move with it** | The DoA lines from §5. A task handed over without its decisions comes straight back to you |
| **Proof it works**                    | The scorecard row or SOP metric that must hold for two months before you stop watching     |

Four rules decide the order:

- **Take the hours off the scarcest person first.** At VSYST that is the technical founder: tickets, dealer onboarding and the chasing around them are hours taken from the only person who ships code — the same argument that puts Support/Ops first in §8.
- **Start with the least emotionally loaded domain.** The standard advice for taking operations off a founder is to observe and document before changing anything, and to take over the least emotionally charged areas first ([Alder Koten — COO in founder-led companies](https://charliesolorzano.me/2026/03/17/coo-founder-led-company-failure/)). Vendor payments, filings and registers move easily; the dealer relationships the domain-expert director has held for thirty years do not, and need not yet.
- **Ask the CEO the direct question, in writing, and log the answer** — _which operational decisions will you stop making?_ ([[03-the-coo-core-value-and-the-ceo-coo-contract|lesson 03]] §5's contract). A task whose decisions never transfer is theatre; within a fortnight everyone learns to route around you, back to the founder.
- **One a week, forever — not a handover project.** That rate is fifty transfers a year. A "handover project" produces a spreadsheet, a workshop and no transfers.

A handover is complete when three things are true: the SOP is Live and signed off; the owner column has changed in the SOP index, on the scorecard and in the RACI; and **you have not touched the task for two full cycles**. Until all three hold, it is a loan, not a transfer.

## 11. The COO-as-bottleneck trap

The failure this lesson exists to prevent looks exactly like diligence. You take work off the founders, write the SOPs, hold the registers, review everything — and two quarters later every approval, answer and decision routes through you. The company has swapped a founder bottleneck for a COO bottleneck and gained a job title. Grove's arithmetic is unforgiving: a bottleneck is **negative leverage** — your one-hour delay multiplies into everyone else's waiting ([[02-how-a-coo-thinks|lesson 02]] §4).

Six symptoms, each with a measurement you can take this week and a fix you have already built:

| Symptom                                     | Measure it                                                                                                               | The fix                                                                                               |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Everything waits for your approval          | Count last week's approvals the DoA already permitted without you                                                        | Raise the threshold (§5), or publish the default and stop being asked                                 |
| Your week is mostly doing                   | The calendar audit ([[01-what-is-a-coo\|lesson 01]] exercise 10.3): hours in doing versus deciding, training and reading | Hand the largest doing-block over as a §10 handover                                                   |
| You enter scorecard rows that are not yours | Count the rows you personally filled on Monday                                                                           | Move the row to its owner; automate the query ([[18-automation-and-ai-in-operations\|lesson 18]])     |
| The same question keeps arriving            | Tally repeat questions for one week                                                                                      | The second identical question is an SOP edit, not an answer ([[15-sops-and-playbooks\|lesson 15]] §8) |
| You are in every meeting                    | Count the meetings where you made no decision and produced no artefact                                                   | Leave; read the notes ([[T03-weekly-ops-meeting-agenda\|T03]])                                        |
| Nothing sits at level 5                     | Count level-5 outcomes (§1)                                                                                              | Move exactly one outcome up one rung this quarter                                                     |

Three habits stop the queue re-forming:

- **Answer with the rule, not the answer.** "Can I spend ₹8,000 on this?" gets "what does the DoA say?" Answering the question teaches people to ask you; answering with the mechanism teaches them the mechanism, and the second question never comes.
- **Decide in public.** Every decision written into [[T09-decision-log-and-adr|T09]] is one nobody has to re-ask, and a precedent the next person can apply without you in the room.
- **Batch the interruptions.** A single 30-minute window a day, in which anything may be asked, turns twenty interruptions into one conversation and makes the cost of "quick question" visible — including to you.

The founder-operator's version of the trap deserves naming, because it is not laziness but speed: **you are usually the fastest person at the task you just documented.** Doing it yourself saves twenty minutes today and costs the company its autopilot, every single time. The escape is to treat your own speed at routine work as a defect in the system rather than a virtue in you — which is precisely what the two-week holiday test measures ([[20-the-autopilot-test-and-scaling-the-machine|lesson 20]]).

## 12. At VSYST — applying this now

- **Fill the RACI once, with all three directors in the room.** One hour, roughly 25 recurring activities, exactly one A per row ([[T07-raci-matrix|T07]] and the `RACI` tab of [the workbook](toolkit/vsyst-coo-workbook.xlsx)). At a family company the grid is not the value — the four or five rows where two people each assumed the other was doing it are. Log those as decisions in [[T09-decision-log-and-adr|T09]], because otherwise they get re-litigated at the next family dinner.
- **Ratify the DoA before you need it.** Take §5's table to the next board meeting, change the thresholds you genuinely disagree with, and get it minuted ([[05-legal-and-governance-foundation|lesson 05]]). A matrix ratified in a calm month is what lets you say "the matrix says so" in a tense one. Every rupee figure stays illustrative until the board fixes it, and any line touching a statutory limit is **VERIFY LIVE** with the CA/CS first.
- **Draw the eight-person tree, not the thirty-person one.** §6's second diagram is the plan for the next twelve months; the 15- and 30-person trees exist to show which way the branches grow, not to recruit against. Keep the tree and the role charters in [[T29-org-chart-and-role-charters|T29]], the costs and hiring triggers in [[T21-headcount-plan|T21]] and the `Headcount Plan` tab.
- **Name the first five handovers off the technical founder.** Support tickets, dealer onboarding, the vendor register and payment run, compliance chasing, and the Monday scorecard fill — each with an SOP id from [[15-sops-and-playbooks|lesson 15]] §4, a target level and a date. Worked one item a week, that list is the highest-leverage artefact in this whole phase.
- **What not to do:** no titles that outrun the team (a "Head of" over nobody is a promise you must later break); no boxes on the chart for roles you cannot fund — hire on triggers, not hope ([[T21-headcount-plan|T21]]); no two-manager reporting lines at eight people; no DoA that has not been ratified, and no threshold set so low that everything crosses it; and never delegate a task without its decision rights — that is not delegation, it is homework.

## 13. Exercises

**13.1 — Fill the RACI and find the gaps (60 min, all three directors, one sitting).** Take [[T07-raci-matrix|T07]]'s starter list into the `RACI` tab of [the workbook](toolkit/vsyst-coo-workbook.xlsx): every recurring activity gets exactly one A, at most two C's, everyone else in I. Do it live, not over email — the disagreements are the deliverable. Write each surprise into [[T09-decision-log-and-adr|T09]] as a one-line decision with a name and a date, and put the re-read on the quarterly agenda.

**13.2 — Draft the DoA matrix and take it to the board (45 min to draft, then the meeting).** Copy §5's table into [[T22-delegation-of-authority-matrix|T22]] and the `DoA Matrix` tab, adjust the thresholds you disagree with, and mark **VERIFY LIVE** on every line that touches a statutory limit. Circulate it two days before the board meeting, get it minuted, and write the annual review date into the file. The artefact is a minuted resolution, not a spreadsheet.

**13.3 — Write one role charter and the tree behind it (45 min).** Using [[T29-org-chart-and-role-charters|T29]]: draw §6's eight-person tree as it will actually look, then write the **Support/Ops Associate** charter — mission, three to five measurable outcomes for twelve months, the KPI rows it moves (they must already exist on the scorecard, [[T05-kpi-scorecard|T05]]), the DoA lines it holds, who it reports to, and a named backup. If you cannot name three outcomes that map to existing rows, the seat is not ready — write that sentence in the file and move on.

**13.4 — Start the first handover (30 min to set up, two cycles to finish).** Pick one task from §12's list of five. Write the handover row (§10's six columns), name the level it sits at today and the level you want in a month, book the shadow session in the calendar, and say the level out loud to the person. After two cycles, check the proof metric and either move the rung or fix the SOP — and record which in [[T09-decision-log-and-adr|T09]].
