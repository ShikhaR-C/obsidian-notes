# 03 — The COO's Core Value and the CEO–COO Contract

_Phase 1 · Understand the Seat · Week 0. After this lesson you can say, in plain words, what a COO is worth to a company and how that worth is measured; describe what "good" looks like at 6, 12 and 18 months; name the ways the seat fails; and — the artefact that matters — sit down with the CEO and write the CEO–COO contract: who owns what, who decides what, how you disagree, and how you keep each other from being surprised._

## Explain-it-like-I'm-5

Two people are building a house. One draws the plan and decides where the rooms go; the other makes sure the bricks arrive, the mason comes, nobody falls off the scaffold, the bills get paid, and the municipal inspector finds the papers in order. If only the first person exists, the house is beautiful on paper and late in life. If only the second exists, the house is finished on time and nobody wants to live in it. **A company needs both, and the second one is the COO.**

The catch: the two of them will argue — about money, about hiring, about what to do first. If they argue about the same thing every week, the house stops. So before laying a brick they write down **who decides what**, and agree that once a decision is made they both push in the same direction. That page — one sheet, signed — is the CEO–COO contract, and this lesson ends with you drafting it.

## 1. Why a company needs a COO — six kinds of value

Lesson 01 defined the seat; this section says what it is _for_. A COO earns their place in six ways, each with a plain meaning and a concrete form at a three-director company that has nothing set up yet.

| Value                                   | What it means                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             | At VSYST in the first year                                                                                                              |
| --------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| **1. Converts strategy into execution** | The CEO says "sell DZZLO to dealers one district at a time, as a subscription per GSTIN." The COO turns that sentence into a pipeline, an onboarding checklist, an invoicing routine, a collections calendar and a scorecard row. McKinsey: "delivering the company's strategy isn't just part of the job — it _is_ the job" ([McKinsey — Productivity at the core](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/operations/our-insights/productivity-at-the-core-how-coos-deliver-strategy)).                                                   | Lessons 10–11: the dealer onboarding SOP, the pipeline stages in ERPNext, the dunning ladder — each a mechanism the strategy runs on.   |
| **2. Scales the CEO**                   | Every hour the CEO spends chasing a vendor invoice is an hour not spent on the product or a partner conversation. Lemkin's case for a COO: manage the things "where you do not have special value" so the CEO gets creative time back — "a COO isn't a crutch… a COO is leverage" ([SaaStr](https://www.saastr.com/ready-cro-coo-earlier-days/)). Kruze names the symptom the COO removes: "if the founder isn't available, things come to a halt" ([Kruze — first ops hire](https://kruzeconsulting.com/blog/startup-operations-hire/)). | The technical founder ships code and holds the founder-only relationships; everything else moves to the COO seat as a mechanism (§5.1). |
| **3. Builds the operating system**      | Cadence, owners, numbers, documents. A great COO is "a builder of systems, not a manager of maintenance" ([Forbes, Oct 2025](https://www.forbes.com/sites/abdoriani/2025/10/25/the-trap-of-hiring-a-coo-for-your-startup-too-early/)).                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                    | The company binder (05), the tool map (07), the weekly ops meeting (08), the SOP index (15), the scorecard (16).                        |
| **4. Reduces variance**                 | Invoices go out on the same day every month; support replies within the same hour; releases follow one checklist; the GST return never depends on memory. Variance is what makes a small company feel chaotic even when it is busy.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       | The rate-confirmation window already removes variance for dealers; the COO does the same for the company's own routines.                |
| **5. Creates capacity**                 | Every task that becomes an SOP with an owner is a task a hire, a contractor or a script can absorb — more dealers without more founder hours. Grove: a manager's output is the output of the teams they run and influence ([High Output Management notes](https://www.nateliason.com/notes/high-output-management-andy-grove)).                                                                                                                                                                                                           | Contractors before employees; the hire that frees the founder; automating the daily grind (lessons 09, 17, 18).                         |
| **6. Protects the company**             | Compliance (ROC, GST, TDS, PF/ESI, POSH, DPDP), risk (backups, access, insurance, key-person) and **institutional memory** — how the company works is written down, not carried in one head.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              | The compliance calendar ([[T16-compliance-calendar                                                                                      | T16]]), the risk register ([[T15-risk-register | T15]]), the vault as source of truth. |

None of the six is "does the work." At a five-person company you will do plenty of the work — but the _value_ is what remains after you stop doing it. That is the [[index|landing page]]'s sentence again: **your product is the operating system, not the output.**

## 2. What "good" looks like at 6, 12 and 18 months

The COO's value is slow to show and easy to argue about, so agree in advance what "good" means at three checkpoints. Illustrative, calibrated to VSYST's stage; the [[00-course-map-and-timeline|master timeline]] gives the week-by-week version.

| Horizon                                               | The machine                                                                                                                                                           | The numbers                                                                                                                                                                                                      | The people                                                                                                                                      | What the CEO notices                                                                                     |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **6 months** — foundation done, first functions built | Company binder complete ([[T02-company-binder-checklist                                                                                                               | T02]]); tool map fixed, every credential in a shared vault; the weekly ops meeting has run ~20 weeks without a skip; support and dealer-onboarding SOPs live; first policies (POSH, leave, expenses, IT) written | Scorecard v0 (5–7 numbers) filled every Monday; 13-week cash forecast owned jointly with the CA; zero missed filings since the calendar existed | Hiring process used once end to end (a contractor or first hire); role charters for the seats that exist | Nobody asks them where a document is; support and vendors no longer land in their WhatsApp                                                 |
| **12 months** — every function on a checklist         | Every recurring process has an owner, an SOP and a number (~25 SOPs, lesson 15); DoA matrix in force ([[T22-delegation-of-authority-matrix                            | T22]]); risk register reviewed quarterly; two quarterly planning sessions done; monthly budget-vs-actual with the CA                                                                                             | KPI tree v1 (lesson 16); collections and DSO weekly; incident count and MTTR known; first automations have retired a daily task                 | A second person for every critical task; the first ops/support person handed a function on an SOP        | Their calendar shows product, customers and partners — not admin. The decisions they said they would stop making, they have stopped making |
| **18 months** — autopilot                             | Two-week holiday test passed and debriefed (lesson 20); annual operating-system audit done (SOP, access, vendor, risk, compliance reviews); alerts surface exceptions | One sheet or dashboard read by exception; the COO's own scorecard (§3) green two quarters running                                                                                                                | An owner for each function; the COO's time mostly on design, hiring and the next quarter                                                        | The company shipped, sold, supported and complied for two weeks while both of you were away              |

Two cautions. "Good" at 6 months is mostly _foundation_, which looks unglamorous — resist skipping to dashboards. And the seat is judged fast: COO tenure is the shortest in the C-suite (3.2 years in the Fortune 500, per [Spencer Stuart](https://www.spencerstuart.com/-/media/2025/02/f500-snapshot/fortune-500-c-suite-snapshot-2024-profiles-in-functional-leadership.pdf)) — one more reason to make the checkpoints explicit rather than felt.

## 3. How COO performance is measured — the COO scorecard

Because the seat varies so much, the metrics must be agreed up front. Executive-search firms say exactly this — "you need to be sure of the role your COO plays before you can appraise him/her" — and recommend agreeing metrics in advance and evaluating quarterly plus annually ([Cowen Partners — COO performance metrics](https://cowenpartners.com/coo-performance-metrics-how-to-measure-the-effectiveness-of-your-coo/)). At scale the standard COO KPIs are employee turnover, labour utilisation, operating margin, operating cash flow and the cash conversion cycle (same source); a scaled-startup version is "six numbers on one page" — revenue attainment and forecast accuracy, margin trajectory, sales efficiency, operating-cadence health (% of OKRs shipped on time, % of weekly reviews producing a documented decision) and regrettable senior attrition ([TheHireHub — How to hire a COO in India](https://www.thehirehub.ai/blog/how-to-hire-a-coo-india-2026)).

Neither fits a pre-revenue three-director company yet. The version that does is below. Put it in [[T05-kpi-scorecard|T05]] as a block separate from the company scorecard: this one measures _you_.

| #   | Metric                           | Definition                                                                                                                | Source of truth                           | Illustrative target                                                       | Reviewed                                                                      |
| --- | -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | --------- |
| 1   | **On-time delivery of the plan** | % of quarterly rocks/OKRs shipped by quarter end                                                                          | [[T06-okr-planning-sheet                  | T06]], [[T19-quarterly-plan-and-review                                    | T19]]                                                                         | ≥ 80% shipped; the rest consciously dropped, not forgotten | Quarterly |
| 2   | **Scorecard health**             | % of Mondays the company scorecard was complete by 10:00; % of metrics green; % of weekly meetings that logged a decision | T05, [[T09-decision-log-and-adr           | T09]]                                                                     | 100% filled; ≥ 70% green; every meeting logs a decision or an explicit "none" | Weekly / monthly                                           |
| 3   | **Cash and collections**         | Runway vs the red line; DSO; % of receivables over 30 days; 13-week trough predicted within an agreed band                | [[T20-budget-vs-actual-and-cash-forecast  | T20]], ERPNext AR ageing                                                  | Never below the red line without a written plan; over-30-day AR trending down | Weekly                                                     |
| 4   | **Hiring plan hit rate**         | Roles filled within ± 1 month of the headcount plan; % of hires passing 90 days                                           | [[T21-headcount-plan                      | T21]], hiring tracker                                                     | ≥ 75% on time; ≥ 80% pass 90 days                                             | Quarterly                                                  |
| 5   | **Incidents and SLA**            | P1 incidents per quarter; MTTR; % of tickets first-answered within SLA                                                    | Incident log ([[T17-incident-postmortem   | T17]]), helpdesk                                                          | Trend down; SLA ≥ 90%                                                         | Monthly                                                    |
| 6   | **Compliance zero-misses**       | Statutory filings and renewals completed on or before the due date                                                        | [[T16-compliance-calendar                 | T16]]                                                                     | 100%; any miss triggers a postmortem                                          | Monthly                                                    |
| 7   | **Founder hours freed**          | Hours per week the CEO spends on non-product operations (support, vendors, admin, filings), from a monthly calendar audit | Calendar audit (lesson 01, exercise 10.3) | Trending to a floor agreed in T01 (e.g. under 4 hours a week by month 12) | Monthly                                                                       |
| 8   | **The autopilot test**           | Maturity level per function (lesson 20) and the two-week holiday test result                                              | Maturity checklist                        | Level 3+ everywhere by month 12; test passed by month 18                  | Quarterly                                                                     |

Three rules. **Numbers before narrative** — the review starts with the table, not with how the quarter felt. **The COO fills it, the CEO reads it** — the discipline you will ask of every owner. **It is a contract, not a report card** — if a target was wrong, change it in the 1:1 and log why in T09.

## 4. How the seat fails

Most COO failures are structural, not competence failures, and each has a mechanism that prevents it. Learn the list now; every item on it will be offered to you within a year.

| Failure mode                                 | What it looks like                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           | Early warning                                                                                            | The mechanism that prevents it                                                                                    |
| -------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Glorified project manager**                | Your week is status updates on other people's work; you track, you don't design                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              | You cannot name a system you built this month                                                            | The "not my job" list (lesson 01); a monthly count of mechanisms shipped                                          |
| **No decision rights**                       | Authority "formally promised and informally withdrawn"; the team keeps escalating to the founder; within three months "the COO has the title, and the founder has the authority" ([Alder Koten, 2026](https://charliesolorzano.me/2026/03/17/coo-founder-led-company-failure/))                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              | People check with the CEO after you have decided                                                         | Written decision rights ([[T01-ceo-coo-operating-agreement                                                        | T01]], [[T22-delegation-of-authority-matrix                                                        | T22]]) and a CEO who says "talk to the COO" and means it |
| **Shadow CEO**                               | You quietly re-decide strategy or run a parallel agenda; the team hears two directions                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       | Disagreements leak into meetings instead of the 1:1                                                      | The disagreement protocol (§5.5); one position in public                                                          |
| **The COO becomes the bottleneck**           | Everything routes through you; you replaced the founder as the single point of failure                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       | Your inbox is the company's queue; holidays are impossible                                               | Delegation ladder and DoA (lesson 17); "if it happens twice, write it down"; the two-week test                    |
| **Process for its own sake**                 | Templates nobody uses; meetings with no decisions; the vault as a museum                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     | SOPs with no owner and no last-reviewed date                                                             | Every process has a metric and a review date; kill SOPs (lesson 15); "no meeting without an artefact" (lesson 08) |
| **Hides bad news**                           | Problems arrive as surprises — a missed filing, a churned dealer, a cash trough                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              | You are drafting the message instead of sending it                                                       | The "no surprises" rule (§5.6); blameless postmortems ([[T17-incident-postmortem                                  | T17]])                                                                                             |
| **Hired too early, or for the wrong reason** | A COO title where an ops manager or chief of staff would solve 80% of the problem — "you trade creative chaos for organized stagnation" ([Forbes, 2025](https://www.forbes.com/sites/abdoriani/2025/10/25/the-trap-of-hiring-a-coo-for-your-startup-too-early/)); or hiring "for what the CEO doesn't want to do" — your exact opposite — Intercom's Karen Peacock's number-one reason COO hires fail ([CodeScience — Peacock at SaaStr](https://codescience.com/blog/2018/intercoms-karen-peacock-scaling-saas-businesses)) | The role was created to dump tasks, not to own outcomes; the "why now" cannot be written in one sentence | A written charter before the seat is filled ([[T30-coo-charter-and-90-day-plan                                    | T30]]). At VSYST the seat is founder-held, so the live risk is the reverse — leaving it _unfilled_ |

The base rate is sobering: HubSpot's Brian Halligan has estimated that half or more of startup C-level hires are gone within 18 months (secondary citation via [startupradio](https://startupradio.substack.com/p/why-startup-exec-hires-fail-and-how)); undesigned COO roles default to "fixer instead of strategist… handed problems but not authority," and turn "politically fragile" when board, CEO and COO have not agreed decision rights and public authority ([Bright Arrow](https://www.brightarrowcoaching.com/the-coo-role-isnt-what-you-think-it-is/); [JRG Partners](https://www.jrgpartners.com/ceo-coo-partnership-how-forge-ultimate-alliance-execution/)). The thread through all of it is what COO Alliance names: failures are "rarely about skill… almost always about clarity" — unclear roles, weak communication, no shared rhythm ([COO Alliance](https://cooalliance.com/how-to-build-a-ceo-coo-partnership-that-actually-works/)). Clarity is a document. That document is §5.

## 5. The CEO–COO contract

One page (two at most), written together, signed, and reviewed on a date. Its template is [[T01-ceo-coo-operating-agreement|T01 — CEO–COO Operating Agreement]]; the spend and approval thresholds it points to live in [[T22-delegation-of-authority-matrix|T22 — Delegation of Authority Matrix]]. The design brief, from a16z's framing: successful COO arrangements "involve clear delineation between CEO and COO responsibilities and high trust; the most-troubled involve ambiguity, competition for credit, or different views on priorities" ([Startups.com — COO](https://www.startups.com/lexicon/coo)). Seven parts.

### 5.1 Division of labour — who owns which function

Not "who helps with" — who is the **DRI** (directly responsible individual: the one name that answers for the outcome). At VSYST today, an honest first draft:

| Function                                                                                          | Owner (DRI)                                                                     | Notes                                                                                              |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Product direction, architecture, engineering, releases                                            | **CEO/CTO**                                                                     | The COO owns the _process_ around releases (checklist, incident comms — lesson 12), never the code |
| Partner and founder-only relationships (the IOCL and bank/gateway conversations, "in discussion") | **CEO**; the COO tracks                                                         | Tracker, next steps, MoU/NDA hygiene (lesson 11)                                                   |
| Dealer domain knowledge, field credibility, association and OMC channels                          | **Domain-expert director**                                                      | The COO gets this knowledge _written down_ — onboarding SOP, FAQ, objection handling               |
| Legal, statutory and the compliance calendar (with the CS/CA)                                     | **COO**                                                                         | Filings go through the CA/CS; the COO owns the calendar and the binder                             |
| Money operations: bank, approvals, vendor payments, invoicing, collections, 13-week cash          | **COO** (jointly with the CEO on the forecast)                                  | Depth: [[finance/00_README                                                                         | Finance for Founders]] |
| People: contracts, onboarding, policies, payroll, the 1:1 system                                  | **COO**                                                                         |                                                                                                    |
| Customer support and dealer onboarding                                                            | **COO** (the founder answers tickets at first, then hands over via SOP)         |                                                                                                    |
| Sales pipeline, quoting, invoicing handoff                                                        | **COO** for the process; the domain-expert director and the CEO for the selling |                                                                                                    |
| Vendors, tools, IT/security basics, backups                                                       | **COO**                                                                         |                                                                                                    |
| The operating cadence itself (meetings, scorecard, decision log)                                  | **COO**                                                                         | The CEO attends; the COO runs                                                                      |

### 5.2 Decision rights — four levels

For every recurring decision, the contract says which level applies. Four is enough:

| Level                    | Meaning                                                                      | Examples at VSYST (illustrative — set yours in T01/T22)                                                                                    |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Decide alone**         | The owner decides and acts; informs in the weekly meeting                    | COO: vendor spend under the DoA threshold, support SLA wording, meeting cadence, SOP changes. CEO: what ships in a release, the tech stack |
| **Consult, then decide** | The owner decides after hearing the other, and may overrule them             | COO: the first contractor hire, policy wording. CEO: roadmap priorities after hearing the complaint log                                    |
| **Joint**                | Both must agree; if not, escalate                                            | Annual plan and budget; any hire above a salary threshold; pricing (once decided); anything touching the cap table; entering a partnership |
| **Escalate**             | Goes to the board (all three directors) or an outside adviser (CA/CS/lawyer) | Legal exposure; spend above the two-signature line; related-party matters; anything a director has an interest in                          |

Two ideas make the levels workable. **One-way vs two-way doors** (Bezos): irreversible decisions — a partnership MoU, a hire, a price list — get the slow, joint treatment; reversible ones — a support macro, a vendor trial — get decided alone and fast. Most decisions are two-way doors, and treating them as one-way is how small companies get slow ([Growth Method — two-way doors](https://growthmethod.com/two-way-doors/)). **Spend thresholds by role**: a delegation-of-authority matrix lists spend category × threshold × approver × second approver, and a US\$5,000 single-signature ceiling "makes sense for a bootstrapped startup" ([Tallyfy — DoA matrix](https://tallyfy.com/delegation-of-authority-matrix-template/)); set VSYST's ₹ lines in T22 and review them quarterly.

### 5.3 Escalation

Write the path: **owner → the other of CEO/COO (same day) → the three directors as a board (next weekly meeting, or within 48 hours if money or legal) → outside adviser.** And write the always-escalate list, regardless of amount: anything legal or regulatory; anything touching a director's interest (MBP-1 interest disclosures belong on the first board agenda — confirm the exact set with your CS, **VERIFY LIVE**); any public, customer-facing statement; any security incident (CERT-In's six-hour reporting clock has no size exemption — lessons 12–13, **VERIFY LIVE**); any cash event that moves runway past the red line.

### 5.4 The weekly 1:1

Non-negotiable, the same slot every week, 45 minutes, _outside_ the operational meeting. Cameron Herold calls the CEO–COO version a weekly "date night" — a fixed meeting away from the noise where the pair aligns before anything reaches the team ([The Second in Command](https://cameronherold.com/thesecondincommand/)). Mochary's rule improves it: issues arrive **written, with a proposed solution**, before the meeting — "writing vs talking" ([The Great CEO Within notes](https://blas.com/the-great-ceo-within/)). Skeleton (full template: [[T04-one-on-one-template|T04]]):

```
CEO–COO weekly 1:1 (45 min, fixed slot)
 1. Scorecard exceptions (5)     — only reds/ambers, from T05
 2. Decisions needed (15)        — pre-written: context, options, recommendation
 3. Founder-hours check (5)      — what still lands on the CEO that shouldn't?
 4. People & partners (10)       — hires, contractors, partner-tracker next steps
 5. Disagreements parked (5)     — anything either of you is sitting on
 6. Actions with owner + date (5) → decision log (T09)
```

### 5.5 The disagreement protocol — disagree and commit

You will disagree; the protocol makes it safe. **Argue in the 1:1, in writing where possible; decide by the level in §5.2; once decided, both commit and present one position to the team; revisit only on new information, at a review date logged in T09.** Herold's "united front" is the behavioural rule — the COO absorbs the tough calls, the CEO delivers the good news and backs the COO publicly ([The Second in Command](https://cameronherold.com/thesecondincommand/)). "Disagree and commit" is Amazon's phrase for the same discipline; it works only when the decision, the decider and the review date are written down — which is why the decision log exists.

### 5.6 The "no surprises" rule

Neither of you learns anything material from a third party, a dashboard or a deadline notice. Bad news travels _first, fast and in writing_: a missed date, a cash trough, an angry dealer, an incident, a filing at risk. Horowitz's version: a leader's job includes sharing bad news with the top team so the whole team can work the problem ([The Hard Thing About Hard Things](https://www.blinkist.com/en/books/the-hard-thing-about-hard-things-en)). The [[startup-operations-plan|Startup Operations Plan]] says it for customers — "most clients forgive problems; almost none forgive surprises" — and it is equally true between two directors. Make it mechanical: a "risks and surprises" line in every weekly business review ([[T28-weekly-business-review|T28]]) and a standing question in the 1:1.

### 5.7 When one person wears both hats — VSYST's three-director reality

At VSYST the CEO is also the CTO — the technical founder who ships the apps; the domain-expert director holds thirty years of dealer knowledge; and the operations hat sits with a director who may, on some days, also be making CEO-type calls. **The seat exists even when it is shared** — but a shared seat needs rituals that force the switch, or operations silently loses every priority contest with product and partners. The T01 variant for this case:

- **Name the hat when deciding.** Every decision-log entry is tagged CEO-hat or COO-hat; the decision-rights level applies by hat, not by person.
- **Protected COO time.** A fixed weekly block (Monday morning: scorecard, ops meeting, exceptions) that product and partner work cannot book over — Mochary's rule of scheduling the top-goal time first, applied to the seat.
- **The domain-expert director is a formal input, not an occasional one.** A standing 30 minutes a week where dealer knowledge is extracted into SOPs, FAQs and the risk register — otherwise the company's most valuable institutional memory stays oral.
- **The board is three people; use it.** "Joint" and "escalate" decisions go to all three directors, minuted — which is also what the Companies Act expects of a Pvt Ltd (board-meeting minimums for a small company: **VERIFY LIVE** with the CS; lesson 05).
- **The two-hat test.** Once a quarter: which decisions did the CEO-hat make that the COO-hat should have owned? The answer becomes an edit to T01.

## 6. At VSYST — applying this now

- **The contract before the calendar.** Before lesson 05's binder or lesson 08's cadence, spend one sitting with the CEO on T01: the division of labour above, the four decision-rights levels, ₹ thresholds in T22 (an illustrative first pass, reviewed in 90 days), the fixed 1:1 slot, the disagreement and no-surprises rules. Sign it. A signature between family founders feels odd and is exactly the point: the [[startup-operations-plan|Startup Operations Plan]] warns that "co-founder conflict kills more startups than competition does" and that the founder agreement "is the mechanism that lets you disagree safely."
- **Ask the predictive question in writing.** "Which operational decisions will you stop making once the COO seat is running?" ([Alder Koten, 2026](https://charliesolorzano.me/2026/03/17/coo-founder-led-company-failure/)). Log the answer verbatim in T09; revisit at day 90. If the honest first answer is "none yet," the contract's first job is to change that — starting, as the same source advises, with the least emotionally loaded domains (vendors, finance ops, compliance) before the ones close to the founder's heart (product, key relationships).
- **Measure yourself from week one.** Put the eight-row COO scorecard into T05 now, even with mostly empty cells; the founder-hours row starts with the calendar audit from lesson 01. Nothing makes the seat real faster than a number the CEO reads every month.
- **Protect the domain knowledge.** VSYST's most valuable institutional memory is not in the repo — it is how petrol-pump dealers actually run credit, rates and drivers, held by the domain-expert director. Make its extraction a scheduled mechanism, not a favour.
- **Failure modes to watch, in order of likelihood here:** becoming the bottleneck (a small team makes it easy), process for its own sake (an engineer's temptation), and hiding bad news inside a family (the hardest of all — hence the rule).
