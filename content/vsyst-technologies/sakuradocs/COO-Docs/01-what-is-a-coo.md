# 01 — What Is a COO?

_Phase 1 · Understand the Seat · Week 0. After this lesson you can explain, in one paragraph, what the Chief Operating Officer seat is at VSYST, which of the seven classic COO types you are, what is and isn't your job, and how the seat differs from the CEO, the CFO, a general manager, a chief of staff and a head of operations._

## Explain-it-like-I'm-5

Imagine two people running a dhaba on the highway. One stands at the front deciding what to cook, what to charge, which trucks to wave in and whether to open a second dhaba in the next town. The other makes sure the gas cylinder never runs out, the cook comes on time, the plates get washed, the money in the drawer matches the plates served, and the licence on the wall hasn't expired. Both are running the dhaba. **The first is the CEO. The second is the COO.**

When the dhaba is tiny, one person does both jobs — but they are still two jobs, and the second one is the one that quietly kills small businesses when nobody owns it. This course exists because VSYST has decided the second job needs a name, an owner and a method.

## 1. The definition, and why nobody agrees on it

The cleanest definition is McKinsey's: the COO is the executive who **translates strategy into execution** — historically a back-office role that has since become "a catalyst for technology-driven growth, strategic expansion, and employee empowerment" ([McKinsey — Why do organizations have COOs?](https://www.mckinsey.com/featured-insights/mckinsey-explainers/why-do-organizations-have-coos)). The important part of that definition is what it does _not_ say: it does not list functions. That is deliberate. **The COO role is defined relative to what the CEO needs**, which is why it varies more from company to company than any other C-suite seat.

Two honest quotes to keep you humble about the title:

- "Ask ten COOs what they do and you will get ten different answers." — a founding COO whose remit was described as "the entire business minus engineering" ([Between Two COOs — What does a COO do at a startup?](https://betweentwocoos.com/blog/what-does-a-coo-do-early-stage-startup-allie-fleder/), 2026).
- "COO is a bit of a made-up job… there is no organizational need for a COO per se… It is an elective, extra hire… until you are huuuge." — Jason Lemkin ([SaaStr — What does a COO typically do at a SaaS startup?](https://www.saastr.com/dear-saastr-what-does-a-coo-typically-do-at-a-saas-startup), 2024).

Both are right, and neither is a reason to skip the seat. Lemkin's own follow-up is the point: the useful split is not "internal vs external" but **"things only the CEO can do" versus "things someone really smart and driven, but not the CEO, can manage."** At a newly formed company that second bucket is enormous — legal, money, people, customers, vendors, compliance, tools, rhythm — and it is exactly the bucket this course teaches you to own.

The one-line version you will use in your own charter (lesson 04, [[T30-coo-charter-and-90-day-plan|T30]]): **the COO builds and runs the operating system of the company so that the CEO can steer it.** Your product is the operating system, not the output.

## 2. The seven kinds of COO (and which one you are)

The classic taxonomy is Bennett & Miles' HBR article _Second in Command: The Misunderstood Role of the Chief Operating Officer_ ([HBR, 2006](https://store.hbr.org/product/second-in-command-the-misunderstood-role-of-the-chief-operating-officer/R0605C)). They found seven reasons companies create the seat, and each produces a different job. A COO can be more than one at once, and move between them over time.

| Archetype         | Why the seat exists                               | What the COO actually does all day                           | Fit at VSYST today                                                                     |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Executor**      | The strategy is set; someone must run it          | Owns day-to-day operations, cadence, delivery, numbers       | **Yes — primary.** The product exists; the operating system doesn't.                   |
| **Change agent**  | A specific transformation is needed               | Leads one big programme (turnaround, integration, new model) | Later — e.g. the move from free to paid subscriptions                                  |
| **Mentor**        | The CEO is young or first-time                    | Seasoned operator coaching the CEO                           | No — VSYST's founders are peers                                                        |
| **Other half**    | The CEO is strong in some areas, absent in others | Owns whatever the CEO doesn't — often people, money, process | **Yes — secondary.** A technical CEO who ships code needs the non-building work owned. |
| **Partner**       | Genuine two-in-a-box leadership                   | Co-runs the company; shared decisions                        | Partly — a family founding team behaves like this; lesson 03 makes the split explicit  |
| **Heir apparent** | Succession being tested                           | Runs increasing scope as a proving ground                    | Not now                                                                                |
| **MVP**           | Retain someone too valuable to lose               | Title as reward                                              | No                                                                                     |

Why the labels matter: **most COO failures are archetype mismatches, not competence failures.** A CEO who wanted an Executor but hired an Other Half (or the reverse) will fight the COO within a quarter. Intercom's long-time COO Karen Peacock names "hiring for what the CEO doesn't want to do" — i.e. hiring your exact opposite — as the number-one reason COO hires fail ([SaaStr podcast with Karen Peacock](https://www.saastr.com/saastr-podcast-223-intercom-coo-karen-peacock-on-scaling-from-1mm-to-500mm-arr-5-strategies-to-drive-your-next-wave-of-growth-with-intercom/)). Write your archetype down and get the CEO to agree to it (exercise 1 below). At VSYST the honest answer is **Executor first, Other Half second** — you build the machine, and you take the non-engineering half of the company off the technical founder's desk.

A live 2026 example of the "heir apparent" type, so the table doesn't feel academic: Coca-Cola's COO Henrique Braun was named successor to CEO James Quincey, effective 31 March 2026 ([The Coca-Cola Company](https://www.coca-colacompany.com/media-center/the-coca-cola-company-announces-ceo-succession-plan)).

## 3. COO versus the neighbours

Half the confusion about the seat comes from mixing it up with roles that sit next to it. Use this table when someone (including you) asks "isn't that the CFO's job?"

| Role                        | Looks at                                                   | Owns                                                           | Has line authority?             | How it differs from the COO                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| --------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **CEO**                     | Outward and forward — customers, capital, market, vision   | The _what_ and _why_; final call on strategy, culture, capital | Yes — everyone                  | "The CEO's job is to drive vision and culture. The COO's job is to make that vision real — through people, process, and performance." ([COO Alliance](https://cooalliance.com/how-to-build-a-ceo-coo-partnership-that-actually-works/))                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| **COO**                     | Inward and near-term — systems, teams, cadence, cost, risk | The _how_ and _when_; the operating system                     | Yes — operating functions       | Translates strategy into execution; measured on the machine's health                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         |
| **CFO**                     | Money — books, cash, forecast, controls, capital structure | Accounting, treasury, reporting, fundraising mechanics         | Finance team                    | The boundary is blurring: in a 2025 L.E.K. survey nearly two-thirds of CFOs said their duties overlap the COO's and about 10% of companies had merged the roles; August 2026 alone saw Zoetis, Zillow and Owens Corning give CFOs COO duties ([Fortune, Aug 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/08/10/boards-expanding-cfo-mandate-coo-duties/)). At VSYST there is no CFO — the founder-CFO course covers the books; the COO owns the _operational_ causes of cash (collections, cost, vendor terms).                            |
| **General Manager**         | One business unit or geography                             | P&L of that unit                                               | Yes — within the unit           | A GM runs one business; the COO runs cross-functional operations for the whole company ([Expert Sapiens — COO vs GM](https://expertsapiens.com/en/compare/coo-vs-general-manager/))                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| **Chief of Staff**          | The CEO's capacity                                         | Projects, prep, follow-through on the CEO's behalf             | **No** — individual contributor | A CoS "maximises a leader's capacity" without owning teams; a COO runs teams with decision power over hiring, budgets and execution ([HireChore — COO vs Chief of Staff](https://www.hirechore.com/startups/coo-vs-chief-of-staff)). Some advisers argue founder-led scale-ups should hire a CoS first because the first constraint is CEO leverage, not operational ownership ([Monkhouse & Co](https://www.monkhouseandcompany.com/resources/insight/4-reasons-why-successful-ceos-need-a-chief-of-staff-and-not-a-coo/)). |
| **VP / Head of Operations** | Existing processes                                         | Executing inside the founder's operating system                | Yes — ops team                  | A VP Ops doubles down on the strategy that exists; a COO is brought in to build or transform the operating system and needs the founder to _cede_ decisions ([Alder Koten — COO in founder-led companies](https://charliesolorzano.me/2026/03/17/coo-founder-led-company-failure/); [Bowdoin Group](https://www.bowdoingroup.com/blog/coo-vs-vp-of-operations/))                                                                                                                                                             |

The practical use of this table at a 3-person company: **you will wear several of these hats on the same day.** That is fine. What is not fine is being unable to say which hat you are wearing when you make a decision — because each hat has different decision rights (lesson 03).

## 4. What the seat covers at each stage

The COO's job changes shape with the company. Knowing which stage you are in stops you importing the wrong playbook.

| Stage                                               | Who holds the seat                             | What the COO owns                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 | Evidence                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| --------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Newly formed / seed (3–15 people)** — _VSYST now_ | A founder wearing the hat, or a "founding COO" | The operational generalist who fills every gap the CEO and CTO can't: accounting & finance ops, HR, customer support, recruiting, legal, vendors, sometimes product and BD. One founding COO's role changed "every three to four weeks" — de-facto head of SEO one month, running support seven days a week the next — with finance, recruiting and legal as constants ([Between Two COOs](https://betweentwocoos.com/blog/what-does-a-coo-do-early-stage-startup-allie-fleder/)). Andy Sparks' rule of thumb: an early-stage COO covers ~7 functions and the job is "to do the most important things the company hasn't hired for yet, hire or delegate to someone in your place, and move on" ([Startup Grind](https://medium.com/startup-grind/what-s-a-coo-do-bbeb0f8648ee)). | Kruze (a startup CPA firm) describes the startup VP Ops/COO as owning "pretty much anything that touches money" — close, tax, payroll, insurance, IT, policy ([Kruze — What does a VP Ops or COO do?](https://kruzeconsulting.com/blog/what-vp-ops-coo-startup-do/))                                                                                 |
| **Scale-up (₹8–80 Cr revenue, 30–150 people)**      | Usually a hired COO                            | Everything the CEO cannot _uniquely_ manage: sales-as-engine, customer success, marketing, ops; the CEO drops to ≤4 direct reports and gets creative time back. Lemkin: start the conversation as you approach ~\$10M ARR; a COO title at \$2M ARR "is a sign of weakness"; "A COO isn't a crutch… A COO is leverage" ([SaaStr — ready for a CRO or COO earlier](https://www.saastr.com/ready-cro-coo-earlier-days/))                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             | Forbes (Oct 2025): hire a COO only when moving from "searching for a model" to "scaling a model"; ask first whether an ops manager, chief of staff or project lead solves 80% of the problem ([Forbes — the trap of hiring a COO too early](https://www.forbes.com/sites/abdoriani/2025/10/25/the-trap-of-hiring-a-coo-for-your-startup-too-early/)) |
| **Enterprise**                                      | Career COO, often CEO-in-waiting               | Whole-enterprise operations: AI-driven operations, resilient supply/service chains, workforce strategy, ecosystem alignment, customer experience — PwC's five COO priorities for 2026 ([PwC — What's important to the COO in 2026](https://www.pwc.com/us/en/executive-leadership-hub/coo.html))                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  | In India most founders hire a COO between roughly 150 and 500 employees, or past ~₹100 Cr annualised revenue with two revenue lines ([TheHireHub — How to hire a COO in India, 2026](https://www.thehirehub.ai/blog/how-to-hire-a-coo-india-2026)) — **VERIFY LIVE**, recruiter data, not a survey                                                   |

Read the first row again. It is your job description for the next 18 months — with one crucial addition this course insists on: **you do not just _do_ the seven functions; you turn each into a mechanism (owner, cadence, number, document) so it can be handed off.** Doing without systematising is how a founding COO becomes the company's bottleneck.

## 5. What a COO is _not_

Write these on the first page of your charter, because each one is a trap you will be invited into weekly:

1. **Not the CEO's personal assistant.** You take work _off_ the CEO by owning outcomes, not by fetching them.
2. **Not a project manager with a title.** A PM tracks tasks inside someone else's system; you _design_ the system. If your week is all status updates, the seat has collapsed into PM.
3. **Not the person who personally does every task.** You will do many tasks in month one — that's the founding-COO reality — but each one you do twice must leave behind a checklist and a name that isn't yours.
4. **Not the CFO** (though you sit closest to the money of anyone but the CFO course). You own the operational causes of cash; the books belong to the [[finance/00_README|Finance for Founders]] discipline and the CA.
5. **Not a shadow CEO.** You don't set the vision, and you don't overrule it in private. If you disagree, lesson 03's disagreement protocol applies: argue hard, decide once, present a united front.
6. **Not the fixer who is handed problems without authority.** Bright Arrow's warning: undesigned COO roles default to "fixer instead of strategist… handed problems but not authority" ([Bright Arrow — The COO role isn't what you think it is](https://www.brightarrowcoaching.com/the-coo-role-isnt-what-you-think-it-is/)). Authority is written down (lesson 03, [[T22-delegation-of-authority-matrix|T22]]) or it doesn't exist.

## 6. Visionary and Integrator — the framing that fits a founding team

Gino Wickman's Entrepreneurial Operating System (EOS) uses plainer words for the CEO–COO pair: the **Visionary** (big ideas, relationships, culture, the future) and the **Integrator** (harmony, execution, accountability, the present) ([EOS Worldwide — Traction component](https://www.eosworldwide.com/traction-component)). Almost every strong small company has both, whether or not the titles exist; the trouble starts when one person tries to be both at full intensity, or when two people are both Visionaries and nobody integrates.

Cameron Herold's version for the second-in-command adds the behavioural rule: the CEO and COO operate as **a united front** — the COO absorbs the tough calls, the CEO delivers the good news and backs the COO publicly, and the pair holds a fixed weekly meeting outside the noise ([The Second in Command — Cameron Herold](https://cameronherold.com/thesecondincommand/)). Whatever titles VSYST's three directors use on the MCA forms, this Visionary/Integrator split is the working relationship to design — and lesson 03 turns it into a signed page ([[T01-ceo-coo-operating-agreement|T01]]).

## 7. The COO's calendar at 5 people versus 50

The role's _shape_ is best seen in a week's calendar. Illustrative — yours will differ, but not in kind.

| Block               | 5-person company (VSYST now)                                                               | 50-person company                                                         |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Weekly ops meeting  | You run it (30 min, three people, scorecard + issues)                                      | You run it (90 min, seven function heads)                                 |
| 1:1s                | Two or three, 20 min each                                                                  | Six to eight, 45 min each                                                 |
| Doing the work      | ~50% — you personally answer support, chase an invoice, file with the CA, onboard a dealer | ~10% — only what no owner exists for yet                                  |
| Building mechanisms | ~25% — writing the SOP, the register, the calendar, the checklist                          | ~30% — redesigning them as they break at scale                            |
| Numbers             | Monday morning: fill the scorecard from Mongo/ERPNext/Sheets yourself                      | Monday morning: read the scorecard someone else filled; act on exceptions |
| People              | Hiring the first contractor; onboarding                                                    | Hiring managers; org design; performance system                           |
| CEO time            | Daily hallway + one 45-min weekly 1:1                                                      | One 60-min weekly 1:1 + monthly business review                           |
| External            | Vendors, CA/CS, bank, insurance broker                                                     | Partners, key customers, auditors, board                                  |
| Firefighting        | Frequent, small (an OTP vendor blip, a store rejection)                                    | Rare, large — because mechanisms absorb the small ones                    |

The pattern to notice: **the "doing" column shrinks as the "mechanisms" column does its job.** That migration — from doing to designing to reading dashboards — is the whole arc of this course, and the two-week holiday test in lesson 20 is how you know it worked.

## 8. The seat in numbers, 2024–2026

A few data points, so you know the role you are stepping into is real, growing, and demanding — and so you can quote them to a sceptical co-director:

- **Prevalence swung and recovered.** 48% of Fortune 500 / S&P 500 companies had a COO in 2000, 32% at the 2018 low, 40% by 2022 ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/featured-insights/mckinsey-explainers/why-do-organizations-have-coos); [IA Magazine — 2026 is the year of the COO](https://www.iamagazine.com/2026/01/01/2026-is-the-year-of-the-coo/)). Spencer Stuart counted 234 COOs across the Fortune 500 as of mid-2024 (~47%), 79% promoted from inside, only 17% with prior COO experience, and the shortest average tenure in the C-suite at 3.2 years ([Spencer Stuart — Fortune 500 C-Suite Snapshot 2024](https://www.spencerstuart.com/-/media/2025/02/f500-snapshot/fortune-500-c-suite-snapshot-2024-profiles-in-functional-leadership.pdf)); its 2025 S&P 500 cut shows 3.3-year tenure versus 7.6 for CEOs, 80% internal, only 12% with prior COO experience ([Hunt Scanlon, 2026](https://huntscanlon.com/how-functional-c-suite-roles-are-changing/)).
- **The comeback is measurable.** zero100's analysis of 570 COO appointments found a 671% increase in COO positions between 2006 and 2025, with internal promotions rising from 34% to 54% of appointments; Microsoft hired its first COO in eight years in September 2024 ([zero100 — The COO comeback](https://zero100.com/insights/the-coo-comeback-and-the-roles-new-look/)).
- **It is the most common road to CEO.** 40% of new CEOs in 2024 had held COO or president titles immediately before ([McKinsey — COO excellence](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/operations/our-insights/coo-excellence-the-next-generation-of-leadership)).
- **Experience is deepening, and the job is being redefined around AI.** Oliver Wyman's April 2026 study of 20,000+ European COOs found an average 17 years' experience before appointment and 6-year tenures, and frames the modern COO as running a "COO operating system" of value propositions, operating models, capability engines and ecosystem guardrails ([Oliver Wyman, 2026](https://www.oliverwyman.com/our-expertise/insights/2026/apr/new-next-generation-chief-operating-officer.html)); BCG's 2026 line is blunter — "agentic AI transforms the COO from a technical expert into an enterprise-wide orchestrator" ([BCG, 2026](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2026/coo-role-becomes-ai-orchestrator)). Lesson 18 is where that lands for a 5-person company.

None of those numbers is about a Raipur startup. The reason to know them is subtler: **the seat is a real discipline with a real body of practice**, not a vanity title — and the practices (cadence, scorecards, mechanisms, decision rights) scale _down_ to three people better than most founders expect.

## 9. At VSYST — applying this now

VSYST Technologies has three founder-directors, one product in production, partner conversations in flight, and — for the purposes of this course — **no operating system yet**. Here is what the seat looks like on day one:

- **The seat exists even when it's shared.** If one director wears both CEO and COO hats on different days, the hats are still different jobs with different decision rights. Name the seat, even if the nameplate moves.
- **Archetype: Executor + Other Half.** The strategy (fuel-distribution SaaS, dealer-first, India-only, sales-assisted, subscription per GSTIN — see [[00_OVERVIEW|the product overview]] and [[company/README|the company playbook]]) is set. The COO's job is to make it _run_: get the company legally and financially tidy (lesson 05–06), fix the tool map (07), install the heartbeat (08), and then build customer ops, revenue ops, people ops, compliance and vendor management one at a time (09–14) — taking each off the technical founder's desk as a mechanism, not a favour.
- **The founding-COO remit at VSYST, written plainly:** finance operations with the CA (not the accounting itself), legal and statutory compliance with the CS, people (contracts, onboarding, policies, payroll), customer support and dealer onboarding, the sales pipeline and collections, partnership tracking (IOCL, the bank/gateway conversation), vendors and tools, security and data-protection basics, and the operating cadence itself. **Stays with the CEO/CTO:** product direction, engineering, architecture, releases (you own the _process_ around releases — lesson 12 — not the code), and the relationships only a founder can hold. **The domain-expert director** is the COO's most valuable internal customer and source of truth on how dealers actually work; design the cadence so that knowledge gets written down.
- **The two traps to name on day one:** becoming the fixer with no authority (fix: [[T01-ceo-coo-operating-agreement|T01]] and [[T22-delegation-of-authority-matrix|T22]] in the first fortnight), and becoming the bottleneck by doing instead of systematising (fix: the "if it happens twice, write it down" rule from the [[COO-Docs/index|landing page]], enforced from week one).

**10.2 — The "not my job" list (10 min).** From §5 and §9, list ten things the COO explicitly does **not** own at VSYST (product roadmap, code review, architecture, the founder-only relationships, the CA's filings…). This becomes the "What the COO doesn't own" column of [[T01-ceo-coo-operating-agreement|T01]]. A seat with no edges is a seat that swallows you.

**10.3 — Calendar audit (20 min).** Reconstruct last week (or your best guess of a typical week) in the categories of the §7 table: doing / building mechanisms / numbers / people / CEO time / external / firefighting. Write the hours next to each. Circle the two categories you will deliberately shift over the next 90 days, and note them in T30 under "Month 3 target calendar".

**10.4 — The question that predicts success (15 min).** Ask the CEO, in writing, the question executive-search firms say decides whether founder-led COO arrangements work: _"Which operational decisions will you stop making once the COO seat is running?"_ ([Alder Koten, 2026](https://charliesolorzano.me/2026/03/17/coo-founder-led-company-failure/)). Record the answer verbatim in the decision-rights section of T01. If the honest answer is "none yet," that is useful data — lesson 03 is where you fix it.
