# 02 — How a COO Thinks

_Phase 1 · Understand the Seat · Week 0. After this lesson you can name the mental models operators run on, apply three of them (the constraint, the door type, leverage) to a real VSYST problem this week, say which book fixes which gap, and describe a COO's week from the inside — so that lessons 05–20, which turn these models into artefacts, read as one system rather than twenty checklists._

## Explain-it-like-I'm-5

VSYST's office sits inside a petrol pump, so use the pump. The dealer who runs it well does not fill every tank himself. The pump runs on a **timetable** (shifts change at fixed hours), a **register** (the Daily Sales Register every attendant fills in), **a few numbers checked every night** (litres sold against the tank dip; cash in the drawer against the register), and **people who already know what to do** when the tanker arrives or the card machine fails. When something goes wrong twice, a good dealer does not just scold the attendant — he changes the routine so it cannot happen the same way again. That is operator thinking: not "how do I do this task?" but **"what routine, number and owner would make this task take care of itself?"** Everything below is that one question, given the vocabulary that lets you use it on purpose.

## 1. The business is a machine — and you are its designer

Ray Dalio's framing is the cleanest start: an organisation is "a machine engineered to achieve specific goals"; its outcomes come from its **design** and its **people**, and whoever runs it must alternate between being the **Designer**, who changes the machine, and the **Doer**, who works inside it ([Principles — notes](https://www.nateliason.com/notes/principles-ray-dalio)). Michael Gerber's _The E-Myth Revisited_ says the same to small-business owners: **work _on_ the business, not only _in_ it**, and build the "franchise prototype" — a business that depends on systems, not on particular people ([summary](https://readingraphics.com/book-summary-the-e-myth-revisited/)).

The habit that follows is **systems over goals**. A goal says where you want to be ("50 paying dealer tenants by March" — illustrative). A system is what, run every week, produces that outcome as its normal output — a Tuesday pipeline review, a dealer-onboarding checklist, an activation number on the scorecard. The CEO sets goals; **you build the systems that make the goal the expected result instead of a hope.** Your first question to any goal is: _what, running weekly and owned by whom, would produce this?_ You already think this way as an engineer — you don't compute a value by hand each time, you write the function. **Your product is the operating system, not the output.**

## 2. Good intentions don't work; mechanisms do

The most useful sentence in this course is Jeff Bezos's: **"Good intentions don't work, mechanisms do."** When a well-intentioned person trips, "there's probably a process we need to fix", and the question to ask after every failure is "do we have a mechanism in place so it doesn't happen again?" ([CNBC on the Bezos quote](https://www.cnbc.com/2022/10/06/how-this-popular-jeff-bezos-quote-drives-amazons-climate-goals.html); [AWS Well-Architected — Building mechanisms](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/operational-readiness-reviews/building-mechanisms.html)).

In this course a **mechanism** has exactly four parts — **an owner** (one named person: the _DRI_, directly responsible individual), **a cadence**, **a metric** and **a document**. Missing any one and it is still an intention. String enough mechanisms together and you have **autopilot**: the company runs on documented mechanisms, owned by named people, measured weekly, with exceptions surfacing automatically — so the founders can be away two weeks and it still ships, sells, supports and complies.

| Recurring problem (illustrative)                       | The intention — fails           | The mechanism — works                                                                                                         |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| A GST return was nearly missed                         | "We'll be more careful"         | Compliance-calendar row: due date, owner, 7-day reminder; the CA confirms filing in the weekly meeting (T16)                  |
| Dealers stop confirming rates in the 10 PM–6 AM window | "Keep an eye on it"             | "Rate-confirmation rate" on the Monday scorecard, owner: support; below threshold triggers a WhatsApp check-in SOP (T05, T25) |
| The technical founder answers every support message    | "We'll hire someone eventually" | Support SOP with triage levels, a named first responder and a named backup (T25)                                              |
| Two directors remember a decision differently          | "Let's be clearer next time"    | Decision-log entry with options, decider and review date (T09)                                                                |

The [[COO-Docs/index|landing page]] compresses this into the rule that runs the course — **if it happens twice, write it down; if it happens weekly, put it on the scorecard; if it happens daily, automate it; and in every case give it one owner.** Runbook writers reach the same rule from the other side: "if you've troubleshot the same problem twice, future responders will benefit from documented steps" ([Upstat](https://upstat.io/blog/runbook-vs-sop)).

## 3. Cadence is the operating system

**Cadence** is the fixed rhythm at which the company looks at itself and decides. It is the operating system because every other mechanism plugs into it: the scorecard is read weekly, the budget monthly, the plan quarterly. Each frequency owns a decision class — "daily for triage, weekly for diagnosis and action assignment, monthly for resource reallocation, and quarterly for strategy reset" — and the weekly review is "the heartbeat" ([RedHub](https://blog.redhub.ai/business-operating-cadence); [Fairview](https://getfairview.com/blog/operating-cadence)).

| Frequency | Decision class                                                               | VSYST ritual ([[08-the-operating-cadence\|lesson 08]])                                                                                       | Templates     |
| --------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------- |
| Daily     | Triage — what broke, who is blocked                                          | Async check-in in chat; support triage                                                                                                       | T25           |
| Weekly    | Diagnosis and action — what the numbers say, who does what by when           | Weekly ops meeting (30 min at three people; the 90-minute EOS "Level 10" later — [EOS](https://www.eosworldwide.com/level-10-meeting)); 1:1s | T03, T04, T28 |
| Monthly   | Resource reallocation — budget vs actual, close, all-hands, mentor/CA update | Monthly business review                                                                                                                      | T20, T23, T24 |
| Quarterly | Strategy reset — 3–5 rocks, retro, access/vendor/risk audits                 | Half-day quarterly planning                                                                                                                  | T19, T06      |
| Annual    | Plan, budget, AGM and statutory calendar, insurance renewals                 | Annual planning day                                                                                                                          | T16           |

The mental effect is the point: **cadence converts worry into appointments.** If collections are reviewed every Monday, you do not carry them in your head on Wednesday. Problems surface on schedule instead of by accident — which is what lets you be calm (§12).

## 4. Leverage and task-relevant maturity (Grove)

Andy Grove's _High Output Management_ gives the arithmetic of the seat: **a manager's output = the output of their team + the output of the teams they influence.** Nothing you personally produce counts except through those two terms, so spend time on **high-leverage activities** — where an hour of yours changes many hours of others': a decision that affects everyone, training, a well-run 1:1, information that lets people act ([notes](https://www.nateliason.com/notes/high-output-management-andy-grove); [summary](https://readingraphics.com/book-summary-high-output-management/)). At VSYST: an hour onboarding one dealer produces one onboarded dealer; an hour writing the onboarding checklist produces every future onboarding, done by anyone. A meeting with no agenda has **negative leverage**; so does a bottleneck — if every rupee above a threshold waits for you, everyone downstream slows. The delegation-of-authority matrix ([[T22-delegation-of-authority-matrix|T22]]; _DoA_: who may spend or sign what) fixes that.

Grove's second idea, **task-relevant maturity (TRM)**, is how you delegate without dropping things. TRM is how experienced someone is at _this specific task_, not their seniority — the same person can be high-TRM on React Native and low-TRM on a TDS return.

| TRM for this task | Your style                                                      | Example                                                                      |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Low               | Structured: precise instructions, a checklist, frequent checks  | A new support associate's first week — the SOP plus a daily 10-minute review |
| Medium            | Two-way: discuss the approach, coach, check outcomes weekly     | The same person after a month, owning one ticket category                    |
| High              | Set the objective, agree how you'll monitor, get out of the way | The technical founder on a release; the CA on the filing calendar            |

Delegating a low-TRM task in a high-TRM style is abandonment; the reverse is micromanagement. [[17-delegation-decision-rights-and-org-design|Lesson 17]] turns this into a five-level delegation ladder.

## 5. Find the constraint (Goldratt)

Eliyahu Goldratt's _The Goal_: every system has **one constraint** — the step that limits total throughput — and improving anything else is "local efficiency" that does not change the output. The five focusing steps are **Identify → Exploit → Subordinate → Elevate → Repeat** ([five focusing steps](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/organization-frameworks/theory-of-constraints-five-focusing-steps/); [EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/business-and-management/theory-constraints-toc)).

| Step        | The question                                                                                             | Illustrative VSYST answer today                                                                    |
| ----------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Identify    | Which single step, given double capacity, would double the output that matters (paying, active dealers)? | The technical founder's hours — split between building, support, store submissions and admin       |
| Exploit     | How do we get the most from the constraint as it is?                                                     | Take support, vendors, filings and onboarding off that desk; protect uninterrupted build blocks    |
| Subordinate | What must everything else adjust to?                                                                     | Meetings, tool debates and nice-to-haves wait; nobody interrupts a build block for a non-P1        |
| Elevate     | Still constrained? Invest                                                                                | A support/ops associate; onboarding automation ([[18-automation-and-ai-in-operations\|lesson 18]]) |
| Repeat      | Where did the constraint move?                                                                           | Probably to demos and field sales, then activation — the scorecard tells you                       |

The discipline is negative as much as positive: **do not polish a non-constraint.** A perfect vendor register while the pipeline is empty is efficient and useless. A COO who cannot name the constraint in one sentence is guessing (exercise 16.1).

## 6. Prioritise like an operator

There is always more worth doing than hours. Four tools, each for a different situation:

| Tool                  | What it does                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     | Use it for                                                                                                                                          | The trap                                                                                                   |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Eisenhower matrix** | Sort by urgent × important: do / schedule / delegate / delete                                                                                                                                                                                                                    | Your daily inbox and WhatsApp                                                                                                                       | Urgent-not-important eats COOs; schedule the important-not-urgent (SOPs, the calendar) or it never happens |
| **ICE / RICE**        | Score Impact, Confidence, Ease; RICE adds Reach and uses Effort; rank ([comparison](https://www.productlift.dev/blog/product-prioritization-framework-comparison/); [compared](https://www.fygurs.com/blog/product-prioritization-frameworks-compared))                          | Backlogs of 10+ items: automation candidates, support improvements                                                                                  | Precision theatre — scores are estimates; use them to argue, not to abdicate                               |
| **Cost of delay**     | "The impact of time on the outcomes we hope to achieve"; rank by cost of delay ÷ duration ([Cost of delay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cost_of_delay))                                                                                                                         | Sequencing: a filing with a per-day penalty and a store rejection blocking a release both carry a real cost of delay; most nice-to-haves carry none | Treating everything as equally deferrable                                                                  |
| **The one thing**     | "What is the one thing I can do such that, by doing it, everything else becomes easier or unnecessary?" (Gary Keller's focusing question); 4DX's version is one or two wildly important goals, "from X to Y by when" ([4DX](https://www.perdoo.com/resources/online-guides/4dx)) | Your week and your quarter (EOS calls the quarterly ones Rocks, 3–7 of them — [EOS](https://www.eosworldwide.com/traction-component))               | Having five "one things"                                                                                   |

Under all four: **prioritisation is a weekly ritual with a written, ranked list — not a mood.** The bottom of the list is killed out loud, not silently starved.

## 7. Decision hygiene

**One-way and two-way doors.** Bezos's 2015 shareholder letter split decisions into Type 1 — one-way doors, irreversible or nearly, to be made "methodically, carefully, slowly" — and Type 2, two-way doors that can be reversed, which "most decisions" are and which should be made fast by small groups; large companies fail by applying Type-1 process to Type-2 doors, producing "slowness, unthoughtful risk aversion, failure to experiment" ([Two-way doors](https://growthmethod.com/two-way-doors/); [Type 1 vs Type 2](https://ashikuzzaman.com/2025/03/03/amazons-type-1-vs-type-2-decisions-a-framework-for-effective-decision-making/)). A three-person company fails the opposite way: it walks through one-way doors as if they were two-way.

| One-way doors at VSYST — slow down, write it up, sleep on it                                                                    | Two-way doors — decide today, review in 30 days                                  |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Founder equity and vesting; IP assignment; anything signed for more than a year; a partnership MoU with exclusivity             | Which chat tool; the agenda format; a helpdesk trial; the first draft of any SOP |
| Announcing a price publicly; the structure of the subscription model; hiring an employee (vs. a contractor); letting someone go | A scorecard threshold; a canned reply's wording; which day the weekly meeting is |
| A production data migration; changing the OTP or push vendor                                                                    | A new column in the vendor register                                              |

**Disagree and commit.** Argue hard before the decision — with data, in writing; once made, execute it as if it were your own. That is Amazon's phrase; Cameron Herold's version for the second-in-command is the "united front" — fight in the room, one voice outside it ([The Second in Command](https://cameronherold.com/thesecondincommand/)). Lesson 03 writes the protocol into [[T01-ceo-coo-operating-agreement|T01]].

**Pre-mortem.** Before a one-way door, spend fifteen minutes imagining it is six months later and the decision failed — and write down why. People name risks in that frame that they will not raise as objections. Top items go into the risk register ([[T15-risk-register|T15]]) or change the decision.

**The decision log.** Date, decision, context, options, who decided, door type, review date, outcome — [[T09-decision-log-and-adr|T09]] and the `Decision Log` tab of [the workbook](toolkit/vsyst-coo-workbook.xlsx). Log every one-way door, every spend above the DoA threshold, and anything two directors might remember differently in six months. _Who may decide what_ is a separate written thing — RACI governs a workstream, DACI one decision, RAPID allocates rights when authority is spread ([RACI vs DACI vs RAPID](https://routine.co/blog/posts/raci-daci-rapid-decision-framework)) — lesson 17, [[T07-raci-matrix|T07]], [[T22-delegation-of-authority-matrix|T22]].

## 8. Measure what matters

**Leading versus lagging.** Lagging numbers are results — paying dealers, cash, churn. Leading numbers are the controllable inputs that predict them — demos booked, dealers activated within 14 days, the rate-confirmation rate, tickets answered inside the SLA. Amazon's weekly business review is built on "controllable input metrics" ([Commoncog — the Amazon WBR](https://commoncog.com/the-amazon-weekly-business-review/)); 4DX's second discipline is "act on lead measures", and its scoreboard rule is that anyone can tell in five seconds whether the team is winning ([4DX](https://www.perdoo.com/resources/online-guides/4dx)). A scorecard that is all lagging numbers is a history book.

**Few numbers.** EOS's weekly scorecard is 5–15 numbers on a 13-week trailing view ([EOS Level 10](https://www.eosworldwide.com/level-10-meeting)); VSYST's v0 is 5–7 ([[16-metrics-dashboards-and-scorecards|lesson 16]], [[T05-kpi-scorecard|T05]]). Downloads and sign-ups are vanity; paying, active tenants and cash are truth.

**Thresholds and management by exception.** Every number gets an owner, a goal and a **RAG rule** (red / amber / green thresholds). Then manage by exception: people work independently and escalate only variances from plan; the manager spends time on the largest deviations ([AccountingTools](https://www.accountingtools.com/articles/what-is-management-by-exception.html); [Indeed](https://www.indeed.com/career-advice/career-development/management-by-exception)). That is what "**the COO reads dashboards, not inboxes**" means — green rows get no meeting time. **OKRs** are the quarterly cousin: an Objective is what you want to achieve, the Key Results the 2–4 measurable benchmarks that say whether you did ([OKRs and CFRs](https://quantive.com/resources/articles/okrs-and-cfrs)) — [[19-planning-okrs-and-the-quarterly-rhythm|lesson 19]], [[T06-okr-planning-sheet|T06]].

## 9. Second-order thinking

Ask "**And then what?**" — think in interactions and in time, not only in first effects ([Farnam Street](https://fs.blog/second-order-thinking/)). A discount for annual prepay brings cash now and hides churn for twelve months — so watch activation, not only payment. Hiring a support associate frees the founder and creates a new bottleneck if there is no SOP for them to run — so write the SOP before the offer letter. Automating collection reminders on WhatsApp lifts collections and can sour dealer relationships if the tone is wrong — so a human reviews the first twenty. Rule: **every mechanism you build gets one line in its document on the second-order effect you expect,** and the review date checks it.

## 10. Write it down — documents over meetings

Matt Mochary's _The Great CEO Within_ makes the operational case for **writing over talking**: issues are written up with a proposed solution _before_ the meeting, so the meeting decides instead of discovers, and "Top Goal" time is scheduled first ([notes](https://blas.com/the-great-ceo-within/); [summary](https://www.nateliason.com/notes/the-great-ceo-within-matt-mochary)). Amazon runs on the same instinct at scale — six-page narratives read in silence and the PR/FAQ written before a product is built ([Commoncog — Working Backwards](https://commoncog.com/working-backwards/)).

It matters more at three people, not less: writing is the only way to find out whether you have a thought; a document is read by five people at once and by the hire you make in month nine; and a decision that is not written is a memory — and family memories diverge. House rules: **no meeting without an artefact** (an agenda, each issue with a proposed answer); one-page memos in a fixed shape — context, options, recommendation, decision needed, by when; and one home per record — this Obsidian/Quartz vault for how we work (SOPs, policies, decisions), ERPNext for transactions, GitHub for code. The [[COO-Docs/toolkit/index|COO Toolkit]] means nothing starts blank: [[T08-sop-template|T08]] for a procedure, [[T18-project-charter|T18]] for an initiative, [[T09-decision-log-and-adr|T09]] for a choice.

## 11. Eliminate → Simplify → Standardise → Automate → Delegate

The order is the lesson. **Never automate a mess and never delegate chaos** — you only make the mess faster, and someone else's. Run every recurring piece of work down this ladder before handing it to a script, an agent or a person:

| Step        | The question                                                                                 | Illustrative: the weekly numbers report                                              |
| ----------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Eliminate   | Does this need to exist? Who reads it?                                                       | Cut the twelve columns nobody has asked about in a month                             |
| Simplify    | What is the smallest version that does the job?                                              | One sheet, seven numbers, one owner                                                  |
| Standardise | Same way, same time, same place, written down                                                | SOP: filled by 10:00 Monday from named sources (T08)                                 |
| Automate    | Can a script, an ERPNext notification, a Sheets formula or an agent do the standard version? | A query from Mongo/ERPNext fills the sheet; you get an exception summary (lesson 18) |
| Delegate    | Now that it is boring and documented — who, not you?                                         | The support/ops associate owns the run; you read the red rows                        |

The last step is Dan Sullivan's **"Who, not how"**: stop asking "how do I do this?" and ask "who can do this?" — a contractor, a tool, an agent, a colleague ([summary](https://readingraphics.com/book-summary-who-not-how/)); Herold describes the same shift for a second-in-command, from "how quickly can I get this done?" to "who else could do this work?" ([summary](https://www.befreed.ai/book/the-second-in-command-by-cameron-herold)). Lessons 17 and 18 apply the ladder function by function.

## 12. Calm over busy, and the two clocks

**Calm over busy.** Busyness is a symptom: a mechanism is missing and you are the mechanism. Frank Slootman's rule — "narrow the focus, up the quality, and increase the speed" — is pace from focus, not hours ([Amp It Up — summary](https://manassaloi.com/booksummaries/2022/08/04/amp-it-up-slootman.html)). 4DX keeps the "whirlwind" of day-to-day work under 80% of capacity so the important work has room ([4DX](https://www.perdoo.com/resources/online-guides/4dx)); Shape Up gives work a fixed "appetite" (a time budget, not an estimate) and a cool-down between cycles ([Shape Up](https://basecamp.com/shapeup)). Calm is not slow — it is the absence of avoidable surprises, which is also the "no surprises" rule you sign in lesson 03. Ben Horowitz's caveat: know whether you are in peacetime or wartime and switch deliberately ([summary](https://www.blinkist.com/en/books/the-hard-thing-about-hard-things-en)); at a bootstrapped company most weeks are peacetime for people and wartime for cash, because **cash is a fact** ([[finance/07-phase-7-bootstrapping-runway-burn|Finance Phase 7]]). The end state has a test: **the two-week holiday test** — a founder absence "booked and announced to the team as a system test" ([Stratisian](https://stratisian.com/blog/reduce-founder-dependency-business-systems)) — is the definition of done for autopilot ([[20-the-autopilot-test-and-scaling-the-machine|lesson 20]]).

**Two clocks.** EOS's Vision/Traction Organizer lays out a 10-year target, a 3-year picture, a 1-year plan and quarterly Rocks ([EOS — V/TO](https://www.eosworldwide.com/blog/9684-traction-thoughts-defining-vision-visiontraction-organizer-vto)). The split this course uses: **the CEO owns the three-year picture; the COO owns the next 90 days plus a 12-month view of capacity** — cash (13-week and 12-month), headcount ([[T21-headcount-plan|T21]]), the compliance calendar, vendor renewals. When the CEO brings a three-year idea into a 90-day plan, the answer is never simply "no"; it is "which quarter, and what has to be true first?" The clocks meet in the weekly 1:1 and on the quarterly planning day.

## 15. At VSYST — applying this now

VSYST is three founder-directors, one product in production, partner conversations in flight, and — for this course — no operating system. Not every model above is equally urgent on day one:

- **Start with the free ones this week.** Mechanisms over intentions and the twice rule cost nothing and change behaviour immediately. Cadence: a 30-minute weekly ops meeting on a fixed day and a Monday scorecard v0 of five to seven numbers ([[08-the-operating-cadence|lesson 08]], [[T03-weekly-ops-meeting-agenda|T03]], [[T05-kpi-scorecard|T05]]) — not the 90-minute version. Decision hygiene: three directors from one family will remember decisions differently within a month; open [[T09-decision-log-and-adr|T09]] in week one and classify doors as you go. Constraint thinking: write VSYST's constraint in one sentence (exercise 16.1) before you build anything, so the first mechanisms attack it.
- **Write-it-down is a head start, not a new habit.** The vault, the [[00_OVERVIEW|product overview]] and the company playbook already exist; the COO's job is to route new records into the right home (vault / ERPNext / GitHub) and to refuse decisions that live only in a WhatsApp thread.
- **Defer the ones that need a team.** Task-relevant maturity and the delegation ladder matter once there is someone to delegate to; RICE scoring waits for a backlog long enough to need it; automation waits for standardisation (§11); OKRs wait for the first quarterly planning day (lesson 19).
- **The two founders run on different clocks — by design.** The CEO/technical founder holds the three-year picture: the product roadmap, the move to a per-GSTIN subscription billed on the web, the IOCL and payment-gateway conversations, whatever comes after order management. The COO seat holds the next 90 days and the 12-month capacity picture: which mechanisms ship this quarter, what cash and people that requires, what the compliance calendar demands. The domain-expert director is a third clock — thirty years of how dealers, credit cycles and seasons actually behave — and the cadence should be designed so that pattern memory gets written down. Lesson 03's operating agreement ([[T01-ceo-coo-operating-agreement|T01]]) is where the clocks are synchronised; the weekly 1:1 is where they meet.
- **Do not import a fifty-person operating system.** Every model above scales down; the artefacts must too — one sheet, one 30-minute meeting, one log, one constraint. Models are lenses; the artefact is the point.
