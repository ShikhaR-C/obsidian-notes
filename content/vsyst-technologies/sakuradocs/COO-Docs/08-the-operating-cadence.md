# 08 — The Operating Cadence

_Phase 2 · Foundation · Days 1–30. After this lesson you can install VSYST's full operating rhythm — the daily check-in, the weekly ops meeting run from a fixed agenda, the Monday scorecard, the 1:1s, the pipeline review, the monthly, quarterly and annual rituals — put it in the calendar with an owner and an artefact for each, run meetings that end in logged decisions, and route every message to the channel where it belongs._

[[02-how-a-coo-thinks|Lesson 02]] made the argument: **cadence is the operating system** — the fixed rhythm at which the company looks at itself and decides, the thing every other mechanism plugs into. This lesson stops arguing and installs it. By the end of the fortnight the whole rhythm — **daily → weekly → monthly → quarterly → annual rituals** — sits in the calendar as recurring appointments, each with one owner and one artefact, and the first weekly ops meeting has actually run. Everything after this lesson (people, customers, revenue, compliance, vendors) gets built _inside_ this rhythm; nothing in this lesson requires money, staff or tools you don't already have.

## 1. The heartbeat, and what it replaces

A company with no cadence still reviews itself — it just does it **by accident**: when a dealer complains, when the bank balance surprises someone, when the CA calls about a date that was yesterday. The cost of accident-driven management is not only the misses; it is that every open question stays open all week, in everyone's head, on WhatsApp at 11 PM. The operating cadence replaces that with a schedule. Each frequency owns a **decision class** — "daily for triage, weekly for diagnosis and action assignment, monthly for resource reallocation, and quarterly for strategy reset" — and the weekly review is "the heartbeat" of the whole system ([RedHub — business operating cadence](https://blog.redhub.ai/business-operating-cadence); [Fairview — operating cadence](https://getfairview.com/blog/operating-cadence)).

Three design rules carry this lesson:

1. **The weekly ops meeting is the heartbeat.** Everything else synchronises to it: the scorecard is read there, issues get solved there, decisions get logged from there. Miss a month-end review and you drift for a month; break the weekly beat and the company is back to managing by accident within three weeks.
2. **A ritual is a mechanism, so it has all four parts** (lesson 02 §2): an owner who runs it, a cadence it never misses, a metric or list it reads, and an artefact it writes. A meeting that reads nothing and writes nothing is a conversation — pleasant, and not part of the operating system.
3. **Install the smallest version that will actually run.** Three directors do not need seven meetings; they need one 30-minute meeting, one sheet, one log and four calendar rings around them. Every ritual below has a "v0 at three people" form, and §13 shows how it grows.

The payoff is the one lesson 02 named: **cadence converts worry into appointments** — collections reviewed every Monday are not carried in anyone's head on Wednesday.

## 3. Daily — the async check-in

The daily ring exists for **triage and visibility**, nothing more. At three people it should not be a meeting at all: each person posts three lines in the internal-chat check-in thread by 09:30 —

```
Yesterday: <what shipped / moved>
Today:     <the one thing>
Blocked:   <what, by whom / by what — or "nothing">
```

Rules: **a "blocked" line gets a response the same morning** — unblocking is the highest-leverage minutes of the COO's day; nothing else gets discussed in the thread (a topic that grows a discussion moves to the issues list for Monday); nobody chases a missing check-in more than once — a pattern of missing check-ins is itself an issue for the meeting. If the team later wants a spoken version, the model is the Scaling Up **daily huddle**: 5–15 minutes, standing, same three questions, no problem-solving ([Growth Institute — Rockefeller Habits checklist](https://blog.growthinstitute.com/scale-up-blueprint/10-rockefeller-habits-checklist)). Keep issues out of it ruthlessly, or the huddle swallows the morning.

Two daily loops are deliberately **not** this check-in: support triage runs on its own SLA clock in the helpdesk ([[10-customer-operations-support-and-success|lesson 10]]), and the product has a daily clock of its own — the 10 PM–6 AM rate-confirmation window — whose health shows up as a scorecard number and an alert, not as a ritual for humans ([[18-automation-and-ai-in-operations|lesson 18]] automates the digest).

## 4. Weekly — the ops meeting

The weekly ops meeting is the one ritual you never skip, because it is where the other artefacts get _used_: the scorecard is read, the issues list is solved, the to-dos are checked, the decision log is written. The template is EOS's **Level 10 meeting** — 90 minutes, seven fixed segments, same day and time every week, named for the ambition that attendees rate it 10/10 ([EOS Worldwide — Level 10 Meeting](https://www.eosworldwide.com/level-10-meeting); the official agenda: [EOS L10 PDF](https://cdn2.hubspot.net/hubfs/434170/%5BOFFICIAL%5D%20EOS%20Toolbox/EOS_Tools_Level_10_Meeting.pdf)):

| Min | Segment                            | What happens                                                                                                                                                                                                           |
| --- | ---------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 5   | Segue                              | One personal + one business good news each — then heads down                                                                                                                                                           |
| 5   | Scorecard                          | Each number **on track / off track** only; off track drops to the issues list; no discussion here                                                                                                                      |
| 5   | Rocks                              | Quarterly priorities, on/off track only; off track → issues list                                                                                                                                                       |
| 5   | Customer & employee headlines      | One line each, good or bad; anything needing discussion → issues list                                                                                                                                                  |
| 5   | To-do review                       | Last week's to-dos: done / not done; target 90% done; not done twice → issues list                                                                                                                                     |
| 60  | **IDS — Identify, Discuss, Solve** | Top three issues by vote, one at a time: identify the _real_ issue (often not the stated one), discuss once — everyone speaks once, no repetition — then solve: a to-do with an owner and a date, or a decision logged |
| 5   | Conclude                           | Recap to-dos, agree cascading messages (what the team / dealers must hear), rate the meeting 1–10                                                                                                                      |

The design is two-thirds solving, one-third reporting — the reverse of the default staff meeting, which is why it works ([Strety — L10 agenda guide](https://strety.com/blog/level-10-meeting-agenda-template-guide/)). The rating at the end is not decoration: anything under 8 gets a one-line "why", and the why is usually a hygiene violation from §9 ([Ninety — running world-class L10s](https://www.ninety.io/eos/blog/lead-world-class-eos-level-10-meetings-powered-by-ninety)). The principle under the scorecard segment — numbers are _reported_, then exceptions are _worked_ — is the same one Amazon's weekly business review runs on ([Commoncog — the Amazon WBR](https://commoncog.com/the-amazon-weekly-business-review/)), and the weekly accountability rhythm is 4DX's fourth discipline: a same-time-every-week session where each person reports on commitments and makes new ones ([4DX](https://www.perdoo.com/resources/online-guides/4dx)).

**At three people, run the 30-minute variant** — same skeleton, compressed: segue 2 · scorecard 3 · rocks 2 · headlines 3 · to-dos 3 · IDS 15 (top one or two issues) · conclude 2. [[T03-weekly-ops-meeting-agenda|T03]] carries both forms, the notes skeleton, the facilitator/scribe roles and a filled VSYST example; move to the full 90 minutes when a fourth regular attendee joins, without changing the skeleton. Two rules matter more than the timings: **the meeting exists to solve, not to inform** (information is what the artefacts are for), and **the issues list is never wiped** — anyone adds to it all week, in the vault, and unsolved issues carry over until solved or consciously killed.

## 5. Weekly — the scorecard, the 1:1s and the pipeline review

**The Monday scorecard.** Filled by **Monday 10:00**, before the meeting, never during it — no scorecard, no meeting (the "no meeting without an artefact" rule, §9). Scorecard v0 is 5–7 numbers on a 13-week trailing view — EOS's weekly scorecard runs 5–15 numbers the same way ([EOS — Level 10 Meeting](https://www.eosworldwide.com/level-10-meeting)) — each with one owner, a goal and a RAG rule, entered in [[T05-kpi-scorecard|T05]] / the `KPI Scorecard` tab. Exercise 15.4 writes VSYST's seven; [[16-metrics-dashboards-and-scorecards|lesson 16]] grows it into the KPI tree. Until there is anyone to delegate to, the COO fills it — thirty minutes from Mongo, ERPNext and the support log — and lesson 18's first automation retires exactly that half hour.

**The 1:1s.** Two are already standing. The **CEO–COO 1:1** was designed in [[03-the-coo-core-value-and-the-ceo-coo-contract|lesson 03]] §5.4 — 45 minutes, fixed slot, _outside_ the ops meeting, issues arriving written with a proposed solution; this lesson only adds: put it in the calendar now and treat the slot as immovable. The **domain-knowledge 1:1** with the domain-expert director (lesson 03 §5.7) is 30 minutes with a hard output rule — one piece of dealer knowledge written down per session, into an SOP, the FAQ or the risk register. When the first hire arrives, they get a weekly 1:1 from day one: **their agenda first**, blockers, priorities, feedback both ways — the skeleton is [[T04-one-on-one-template|T04]], and Mochary's rule applies to all of them: a fixed cadence on the calendar, not "when there's time" ([The Great CEO Within — notes](https://blas.com/the-great-ceo-within/)). 1:1s are where trust and task-relevant maturity get managed ([[09-people-operations|lesson 09]] §9); the ops meeting is deliberately too public for that.

**The pipeline review.** Thirty minutes midweek, separate from the ops meeting so that selling gets undivided attention: walk every live lead through the stages (lead → contacted → demo → pilot → paid → active), agree the **next step and date for each** — a pipeline entry without a next step is a wish — and read the conversion math (leads → demos → pilots → paid) that feeds the scorecard. [[11-revenue-operations-and-partnerships|Lesson 11]] owns the stages, the CRM in ERPNext and the partnership tracker that rides in the same slot.

**The Friday WBR one-pager.** Last thing in the week, the COO writes [[T28-weekly-business-review|T28]]: scorecard snapshot, what changed and why, top three issues, decisions taken, next week's one thing — "numbers before narrative". It is read async (by the CEO, later by the team) and it is _different from the ops meeting_: the meeting solves in a room, the WBR forces one person to think in writing — the graded-scorecard → flags → priorities → decision shape that operating-cadence practice recommends for a weekly review ([Fairview — operating cadence](https://getfairview.com/blog/operating-cadence)). At three people it feels like writing to yourself; write it anyway. It is the institutional memory the monthly review reads, and the discipline the future team inherits.

## 6. Monthly — the business review, the close and the all-hands

The monthly ring owns **resource reallocation** — the decisions too slow for a week and too fast for a quarter.

- **The close.** By the 5th, the COO hands the CA the close inputs — every invoice issued, every vendor bill and contractor invoice with PAN/GST details, bank statements, the expense file ([[06-money-rails-and-finance-operations|lesson 06]] owns the checklist). The CA returns the month's P&L and the GST/TDS confirmations. The close is a supplier relationship with a deadline, not a meeting.
- **The monthly business review.** Sixty minutes once the close pack lands (target: by the 10th): **budget vs actual** line by line with a one-line commentary on every material variance, the 13-week cash forecast re-based, collections and AR ageing, and one decision — what gets more money/time next month, what gets less ([[T20-budget-vs-actual-and-cash-forecast|T20]]; depth in [[finance/07-phase-7-bootstrapping-runway-burn|Finance Phase 7]]). The CA joins by call for the first months — cheaper than a wrong guess about your own numbers.
- **The all-hands.** Forty-five minutes, last Friday, from the day headcount exceeds the directors: the numbers (the same scorecard, honestly presented), wins and a customer story, this month's priorities, people news, questions, recognition — [[T24-all-hands-agenda|T24]]. Until then the ops meeting _is_ the all-hands; start the ritual with the first hire so transparency is the default, not a retrofit.
- **The mentor/CA update.** One page a month in [[T23-board-and-investor-update|T23]] shape — TL;DR, KPIs vs plan, cash and runway, wins, misses, asks — sent to the CA and any mentor even though there is no board and no investor. [[19-planning-okrs-and-the-quarterly-rhythm|Lesson 19]] makes the case: the discipline of writing it monthly is the cheapest fundraise-readiness there is.

## 7. Quarterly — planning day and the three audits

Quarterly owns the **strategy reset**. EOS's meeting pulse pairs the weekly beat with a quarterly session (and a two-day annual) to set the next 90 days' Rocks ([EOS — Meeting Pulse](https://www.eosworldwide.com/meeting-pulse)); at VSYST's size it is a **half day, last week of the quarter**: review the quarter (scorecard trends, OKR/rock scores, wins, misses, learnings), then set 3–5 rocks for the next quarter with owners, adjust budget and headcount, and surface risks — agenda and doc in [[T19-quarterly-plan-and-review|T19]], scoring mechanics in [[T06-okr-planning-sheet|T06]], the full method in [[19-planning-okrs-and-the-quarterly-rhythm|lesson 19]].

## 8. Annual — the planning day and the statutory ring

The annual ring has two halves. The **planning half** is yours: a full day in March, before the new financial year — three priorities for the year, the budget, the headcount plan, and the operating-system audit that [[20-the-autopilot-test-and-scaling-the-machine|lesson 20]] describes (SOP review, access review, vendor review, risk review, compliance review in one sweep). The **statutory half** belongs to the calendar you built with the CA/CS: board meetings at the Companies Act minimum for a small company, the AGM in the window after FY close, the annual ROC filings that follow it, DPT-3, and the rest — every date lives in [[T16-compliance-calendar|T16]] with an owner and a lead-time alert, all **VERIFY LIVE** with the CA/CS ([[05-legal-and-governance-foundation|lesson 05]] set them up; [[13-compliance-calendar-risk-and-insurance|lesson 13]] runs them). Two renewals ride the same ring from the register: **insurance policies** and anything with an annual contract — the 60-day-before alerts in [[T14-vendor-and-tool-register|T14]] exist so that renewal is a decision, not a surprise. And one rule closes the loop: **every April, the whole compliance calendar is re-confirmed with the CA** — thresholds, dates and forms move every Budget; the cadence is what catches the moves.

## 9. Meeting hygiene — seven rules

Meetings are where a manager's leverage is highest and where it goes negative fastest ([High Output Management — notes](https://www.nateliason.com/notes/high-output-management-andy-grove)). Seven rules, on the first page of the meeting-notes folder, applying to every meeting at VSYST including the 1:1s:

1. **Every meeting has one owner** — a named facilitator who holds the agenda and the clock. No owner, no meeting.
2. **Every meeting has an agenda before it starts.** For recurring meetings the agenda is the template (T03, T04, T19, T24); for one-offs, three lines in the invite: purpose, decisions needed, pre-reading.
3. **No meeting without an artefact.** A meeting _reads_ at least one artefact (scorecard, pipeline, issues list, memo) and _writes_ at least one (notes, to-dos, a decision). Issues arrive **written, with a proposed solution** where possible — writing versus talking, so the meeting decides instead of discovers ([The Great CEO Within — notes](https://blas.com/the-great-ceo-within/)).
4. **Notes or it didn't happen.** One note per meeting, in the vault, named by date; a rotating scribe writes to-dos, decisions and the rating.
5. **Decisions are logged, actions have an owner and a date.** "We should…" is banned as an ending; the legal endings are a to-do (owner, due date), a logged decision ([[T09-decision-log-and-adr|T09]]), or an explicit "parked until <date>".
6. **Start on time, end on time, same slot every week.** Late arrivals get no recap. A ritual moved is at worst moved, never skipped (T03's rule).
7. **Async by default; meet to decide or solve.** Status is written (check-in thread, WBR); a synchronous meeting exists only where discussion changes the outcome. Before accepting any new recurring meeting, ask which artefact it reads, which it writes, and what it costs — a 60-minute meeting for five people is a person-day.

## 10. Async-first and the channel map

A three-person company does not have a communication-volume problem; it has a **routing** problem — the same question asked on three channels, decisions living in a WhatsApp scroll-back, a credential pasted where it must never be. The fix is a channel map: every kind of message has exactly one home, and everyone knows the map. [[07-tools-and-it-foundation|Lesson 07]] fixed the systems of record; this table fixes the flows:

| Channel                                                          | It is for                                                                                                                                               | Never for                                                                                        | Response norm                                                                     |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **WhatsApp (Business number)**                                   | **External only** — dealers, customers, field contacts, partner pings ([[10-customer-operations-support-and-success\|lesson 10]] runs the support side) | Internal decisions, internal coordination, credentials, anything that must be findable in a year | Per the support SLA                                                               |
| **Internal chat** (Google Chat, per lesson 07's decision record) | Daily check-in thread, coordination, alerts from tools, quick questions                                                                                 | Decisions (log them), files as source of truth, secrets                                          | Same working day; **async-first — a non-urgent message expects no instant reply** |
| **Email** (`support@`, `billing@`, `ops@` aliases)               | External formal: vendors, CA/CS, banks, stores, government portals — the paper trail                                                                    | Internal chatter                                                                                 | One business day                                                                  |
| **The vault** (Obsidian/Quartz)                                  | SOPs, policies, meeting notes, decisions, this course — how we work                                                                                     | Secrets, personal data, contracts (the published site is public — lesson 07 §7)                  | Permanent                                                                         |
| **ERPNext**                                                      | Transactions: invoices, payments, tickets, CRM — what happened                                                                                          | Drafts and debate                                                                                | System of record                                                                  |
| **A call / a meeting**                                           | Urgency, high-bandwidth decisions, 1:1s, anything emotional                                                                                             | Anything that needed a record and didn't get one — minute it after                               | Urgent = call; a call is the _only_ urgent channel                                |

Three norms make it stick. **Urgency is a phone call** — not a 11 PM WhatsApp "seen?"; if it isn't worth a call, it waits for the cadence. **Quiet hours are real**: the rate window runs 10 PM–6 AM and its exceptions are the on-call arrangement in lesson 10, not a norm of round-the-clock chat. And **WhatsApp is a channel, not a record**: anything decided, promised or complained about there gets copied the same day into its system of record — a ticket, a CRM note, a decision-log entry.

## 11. The COO's calendar — blocking the week

The cadence fails quietly if the COO's own calendar does not defend it — the seat's work (building mechanisms, writing SOPs, thinking) is important-not-urgent, and unblocked calendars fill with urgent instead. Mochary's rule: schedule the **top-goal time first**, before anyone else's requests land ([The Great CEO Within — notes](https://blas.com/the-great-ceo-within/)). A template week for the seat, matching §2:

| Block              | When                                               | What it protects                                                                                    |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Ops block**      | Monday 09:00–12:00                                 | Scorecard fill, ops meeting, exception follow-ups — the operating system's own maintenance window   |
| **People block**   | Tuesday afternoon                                  | CEO–COO 1:1, domain-knowledge 1:1, later every team 1:1                                             |
| **Build block**    | Wednesday morning, 3 hours, recurring, no meetings | Writing the mechanism of the week — an SOP, a register, a checklist, a policy. This is the top goal |
| **External block** | Thursday                                           | CA/CS, vendors, bank, partner follow-ups — batched so they don't perforate the week                 |
| **Review block**   | Friday 16:30–18:00                                 | WBR one-pager, decision log sweep, next week's one thing                                            |

Defend one more calendar than your own: **the CEO's build block** (lesson 02's week protected mornings until 13:00 for the technical founder). The fastest way a cadence dies is the CEO getting pulled into operational noise the cadence was built to absorb — every meeting the CEO does not need to attend is the COO doing the job.

## 12. The decision log as a ritual

[[02-how-a-coo-thinks|Lesson 02]] §7 introduced the decision log as hygiene; the cadence is what makes it real, because **the log is written in the meeting, not after it**. The conclude segment of the ops meeting, the decisions section of the 1:1 and the quarterly session all end with the scribe entering the row in [[T09-decision-log-and-adr|T09]] / the `Decision Log` tab before anyone leaves: date, decision, context, options considered, decided by, door type (one-way / two-way), review date, outcome (blank for now).

What gets logged — the threshold from lesson 02, now enforced by ritual: **every one-way door; every spend above the DoA threshold ([[T22-delegation-of-authority-matrix|T22]]); and anything two directors might remember differently in six months.** Technical and structural operations decisions (an OTP-provider abstraction, a hosting move, a data-retention rule) get the slightly fuller **ADR** form — context, decision, consequences — which is the lightweight standard the software world already uses ([adr.github.io](https://adr.github.io/); the MADR template: [adr.github.io/madr](https://adr.github.io/madr/)); keep them in the vault next to the decision log with grep-able IDs (lesson 07 §7's naming).

The under-used column is **review date** — and the cadence is what activates it: the ops meeting's to-do review includes "decision reviews due this week", so a two-way door decided fast in August actually gets re-examined in September instead of fossilising. A decision log without review dates is a diary; with them, it is the company's cheapest learning loop.

## 13. How the cadence grows — 3 → 8 → 15 people

The skeleton never changes; the instances multiply and the COO's role in each shifts from _doing_ to _reading_. What changes at each size:

| Ritual             | 3 people (now)                   | 8 people                                                                | 15 people                                                                                                                                           |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Daily check-in     | One chat thread, everyone        | One thread per function (support, eng, sales) + the company thread dies | Function threads or 10-min huddles; the COO reads exceptions only                                                                                   |
| Weekly ops meeting | One, 30 min, all three directors | One, **90 min L10**, 5–7 attendees — the leadership meeting             | Leadership L10 (function owners) + 30–60-min department meetings on the same skeleton that feed issues _up_                                         |
| Scorecard          | 5–7 numbers, COO fills           | 10–15 numbers, **each owner enters their own row** by Monday 10:00      | Function scorecards roll up; the fill is automated (lesson 18), humans only annotate                                                                |
| 1:1s               | CEO–COO + domain-knowledge slot  | Everyone 1:1s with the COO or CEO weekly, 20–30 min                     | First managers appear; 1:1s cascade — the COO 1:1s managers, managers 1:1 their teams ([[17-delegation-decision-rights-and-org-design\|lesson 17]]) |
| Pipeline review    | 30 min, COO + whoever sells      | Weekly with the field-sales hire; visit logs feed it                    | A sales cadence of its own; COO attends monthly                                                                                                     |
| WBR                | COO writes it, CEO reads it      | Function owners contribute lines; still one page                        | The written WBR becomes the management artefact; meetings shrink because it exists                                                                  |
| All-hands          | Is the ops meeting               | Monthly 45 min, distinct ritual, CEO fronts it                          | Monthly, with Q&A discipline and rotating demos                                                                                                     |
| Monthly review     | One sitting with the CA          | + function budget lines                                                 | + unit economics per function; Metabase dashboards replace the sheet walk                                                                           |
| Quarterly          | Half day                         | Full day, offsite if possible                                           | Two days incl. department planning (EOS's annual is two days — [EOS Meeting Pulse](https://www.eosworldwide.com/meeting-pulse))                     |

Two forces drive the growth. **Meeting cost rises linearly with headcount** — the 90-minute L10 at eight people is twelve person-hours, which is exactly why hygiene (§9) and the written WBR matter more, not less, as you grow. And **the COO must exit each ritual's mechanics**: by fifteen people you facilitate the leadership meeting and _read_ everything else — if you still fill the scorecard or scribe the notes at fifteen, the cadence scaled and you didn't ([[17-delegation-decision-rights-and-org-design|lesson 17]]).

## 14. At VSYST — applying this now

- **Install in one week, in this order:** the Monday ops meeting (30-minute form, T03) and scorecard v0 (T05) first — they are the heartbeat and its pulse-reading; then the CEO–COO 1:1 and the domain-knowledge slot; then the check-in thread; then the calendar rings (monthly review with the CA, quarterly half-day, the T16 statutory dates) as recurring invites with reminders. The first meeting will be awkward and slightly empty. Run it anyway — the fourth one won't be.
- **The shared-hat reality is the reason for fixed slots, not an excuse against them.** When one director wears CEO and COO hats on different days, an unscheduled rhythm collapses into whichever hat shouted last. The Monday ops block and Tuesday 1:1 are how the COO hat gets guaranteed hours (lesson 03 §5.7); the decision log's hat-tagging keeps the seats distinct even in a two-person conversation.
- **Use the cadence to drain the founders' heads.** The domain-expert director's thirty years of dealer patterns and the technical founder's infrastructure knowledge become SOPs, FAQ entries and risk rows one week at a time — the weekly slots exist precisely so that extraction is a mechanism, not a favour.
- **The product's daily clock is not your meeting clock.** Rate confirmations, orders and OTPs run 24 hours through the app; the company's rituals watch them through scorecard rows and alerts ([[10-customer-operations-support-and-success|lesson 10]], [[12-product-and-engineering-operations|lesson 12]]) — do not let "we should check the app more" become a standing meeting.
- **What not to do:** no daily stand-up meeting for three directors who share an office; no 90-minute meeting until there are four regulars; no separate "alignment calls" outside the map — if a topic needs a meeting, it needs an agenda, an owner and an artefact, or it needs to be a chat message; and no new recurring meeting without killing or merging an old one.
