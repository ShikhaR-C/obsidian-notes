# 15 — SOPs and Playbooks

_Phase 4 · Autopilot · Months 6–18. After this lesson you can tell a checklist, an SOP, a runbook and a playbook apart and pick the right form for any task, write a recurring process to the T08 standard, map VSYST's functions into a starter index of ~30 SOPs with owners and review dates, file them where they belong in the vault, train someone by doing and sign them off, measure whether the SOPs are actually used, and kill the ones that aren't._

## Explain-it-like-I'm-5

Think of the dhaba from [[01-what-is-a-coo|lesson 01]] again. Only one person knows how the dal is made, so the dhaba closes on that person's sick day. Now write the recipe down — the quantities, the order, and the checks ("fry the masala until the ghee separates") — and anyone competent can cook a passable dal on day one and a good one by week two. **An SOP is a recipe for a piece of company work.** A checklist is the shopping list a practised cook glances at so nothing is forgotten. And a playbook is for situations rather than dishes — what to do when a customer sends the food back: not fixed steps, but decision rules, because every send-back is different. A company reaches autopilot ([[20-the-autopilot-test-and-scaling-the-machine|lesson 20]]) when every routine dish has a recipe and every recurring situation has decision rules — because that is the day the founders can finally leave the kitchen.

## 1. The engine of autopilot: if it happens twice, write it down

Phases 2 and 3 already made you write things down: a support SOP ([[T25-customer-support-sop|T25]]), a dealer-onboarding checklist ([[10-customer-operations-support-and-success|lesson 10]]), a monthly-close checklist ([[06-money-rails-and-finance-operations|lesson 06]]), a release gate that lives in the repos ([[tasks_12_tdd_testing/00-overview|tasks_12]]). What you do not yet have is a **system of SOPs**: one standard everything follows, one index that knows what exists and when it was last true, one review loop that keeps it true, and one training method that turns a document into a person who can do the job. That system is this lesson — and it is the engine of Phase 4, because **every other autopilot mechanism stands on it**: you cannot delegate what isn't written ([[17-delegation-decision-rights-and-org-design|lesson 17]]), you must not automate what isn't standardised ([[18-automation-and-ai-in-operations|lesson 18]]'s ladder — eliminate → simplify → **standardise** → automate → delegate), and the two-week holiday test fails at the first task that lives only in a founder's head.

The trigger rule is the one from the [[index|landing page]], and it is worth restating exactly, because it is the whole scheduling algorithm for this lesson:

```
If it happens twice        → write it down          (an SOP — this lesson)
If it happens weekly       → put it on the scorecard (a number, lesson 16)
If it happens daily        → automate it            (a script or agent, lesson 18)
And in every case          → give it one owner       (a name, lesson 17)
```

**The first time is discovery; the second time is a pattern.** Runbook writers reach the same law from the technical side — "if you've troubleshot the same problem twice, future responders will benefit from documented steps" ([Upstat — runbook vs SOP](https://upstat.io/blog/runbook-vs-sop)). The deeper argument is the E-Myth's: a small business survives its founders only when it is **systems-dependent rather than people-dependent** — the business runs on documented ways of working, and people run the systems ([ReadinGraphics — The E-Myth Revisited](https://readingraphics.com/book-summary-the-e-myth-revisited/)). For a startup the practical version is blunter: SOPs are what make the company stop depending on any single person's memory ([Whale — SOPs for your startup](https://usewhale.io/blog/sops-for-your-startup/)), and reducing founder dependency is precisely a matter of building the systems that carry the knowledge ([Stratisian — reduce founder dependency](https://stratisian.com/blog/reduce-founder-dependency-business-systems)).

Do the arithmetic once and the habit pays for itself. A task that takes 20 minutes and recurs weekly costs ~17 hours a year of the only person who knows it — plus every interruption when someone must ask. The SOP costs 30–60 minutes to write **while doing the task anyway**, and from then on the task can be handed to a new hire, a backup, or eventually a script. [[T08-sop-template|T08]]'s summary line is the course's: **no SOP, no delegation; no delegation, no autopilot.**

## 2. Checklist, SOP, runbook, playbook — four tools, not one

"SOP" gets used loosely for four different documents. Using the wrong form is why most process documentation dies — a 30-step SOP for a task the veteran does daily gets ignored; a bare checklist handed to a new hire teaches nothing. Know the four apart and pick deliberately ([Glyde — runbook vs SOP vs playbook](https://glydehq.com/resources/blog/runbook-vs-sop-vs-playbook)):

| Form          | What it is                                                                                      | Right tool when                                                                                   | VSYST examples                                                             |
| ------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------ |
| **Checklist** | Memory guard for a **practised** operator — steps named, not explained                          | The person knows the task; the risk is a skipped step under routine or pressure                   | Release checklist ([[T26-release-checklist                                 | T26]]), monthly close inputs, the pre-visit onboarding kit list                                         |
| **SOP**       | The full recipe for a **routine business process** — teachable to someone who has never done it | The task recurs, judgement is low, and you want it doable by someone who isn't you                | Dealer onboarding, weekly scorecard update, payroll run, vendor onboarding |
| **Runbook**   | The SOP's technical sibling — operating a **system**, step by step, often under time pressure   | The "operator" is acting on servers, databases, stores or pipelines                               | Restore drill, production deploy, app-store submission                     |
| **Playbook**  | Decision rules for a **scenario where judgement branches** — situations, not steps              | The situation recurs but every instance differs; you standardise the _decisions_, not the actions | Incident response ([[T17-incident-postmortem                               | T17]]'s severity and comms rules), the churn-save plays of [[10-customer-operations-support-and-success | lesson 10]] §9, the price-objection conversation in a sales call ([[11-revenue-operations-and-partnerships | lesson 11]]) |

Three relationships make the taxonomy useful rather than pedantic:

1. **They nest.** A playbook calls SOPs: the incident playbook's "declare P1" branch invokes the incident-comms SOP; the churn-save playbook's "training gap" branch invokes the onboarding SOP's day-3 call script. Write the SOP once, reference it everywhere.
2. **An SOP graduates into a checklist.** The full steps train the new person; once the task is practised, the distilled checklist at the end of the SOP ([[T08-sop-template|T08]] rule 8) guards the veteran — that split between a training document and a memory guard is the core insight of the checklist literature: checklists exist because even experts skip steps, not because people are stupid ([Runn — The Checklist Manifesto](https://www.runn.io/blog/the-checklist-manifesto-summary)).
3. **A playbook is the honest form for judgement work.** Do not force decision-heavy work into steps — a churn conversation "SOP" with a fixed script produces robotic calls and hides the real skill. Write the decision tree, the principles, and the two or three worked examples; leave the sentences to the human. The minimum viable versions are small either way — a startup SOP needs only task, owner, trigger, steps and a definition of done ([Notion — SOP template for startups](https://www.notion.com/blog/sop-template-for-startups)).

## 3. The SOP standard — what every SOP contains

The standard is [[T08-sop-template|T08]], and every SOP at VSYST follows it — one shape, so a reader always knows where the trigger, the checks and the escalation live. The anatomy:

| Block                       | What it must contain                                                                      | Why it exists                                                                                                                                       |
| --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Metadata**                | id, **one named owner**, version, last reviewed, **next review (+180 days)**, **trigger** | An SOP without a trigger is a document; with one, it is a mechanism. The owner is accountable for the SOP being true, not for personally running it |
| **Purpose & scope**         | One sentence on what it produces; covers / does-not-cover                                 | Stops scope creep and duplicate SOPs                                                                                                                |
| **Roles & prerequisites**   | Who runs it, backup, escalation target; access and inputs needed before step 1            | The step-1 failure mode is always a missing login                                                                                                   |
| **Steps with checks**       | Verb-first, one action per step, **every step with an observable proof it worked**        | "The message shows two grey ticks" is a check; "make sure it went" is a hope                                                                        |
| **Exceptions & escalation** | If X → do Y; unresolved by _time_ → escalate to _role_ via _channel_                      | The exceptions are where SOPs earn their keep — routine paths rarely fail                                                                           |
| **Outputs & metrics**       | What exists when done; how we know the SOP works (on-time %, error count, minutes)        | Feeds §8's adoption measurement and lesson 16's scorecard                                                                                           |
| **Checklist & change log**  | The veteran's distilled list; dated versions with what changed and why                    | The graduation path (§2) and the audit trail (§6)                                                                                                   |

The writing rules that matter most, from T08: **write it while doing the task** (SOPs written from memory skip the steps that matter); write for a reader who has never done it — exact tool names, menu paths, the saved query, the message template; and steal the worked example — T08 carries **SOP-OPS-002 Weekly scorecard update** filled end to end, which is deliberately the first SOP you will write in exercise 11.1. One more rule from hard experience: an SOP describes **one process at one altitude**. "Run support" is not an SOP; "handle an inbound ticket to SLA" is. If your draft has more than ~12 steps, you are probably writing two SOPs, or an SOP plus the playbook that calls it.

## 5. Where SOPs live

One home, already decided in [[07-tools-and-it-foundation|lesson 07]]: **knowledge lives in the Obsidian vault, published on the Quartz site.** SOPs are the clearest case for it — plain markdown in git is versioned, diffable, greppable and portable, and the published site means anyone can read the dealer-onboarding SOP on a phone at the counter, which is where it gets used.

The conventions, so a new person finds any SOP in under a minute:

- **Path and name:** `sops/sop-<func>-<nnn>.md`, kebab-case, the id and name as the H1 — `sops/sop-cus-002.md` opens with `# SOP-CUS-002 — Dealer onboarding to activation`. Playbooks and runbooks live in the same folder under the same scheme; the form is declared in the file, not the folder.
- **The index is the source of truth for status.** The `SOP Index` tab of [the workbook](toolkit/vsyst-coo-workbook.xlsx) holds id, name, function, owner, status (Draft / Live / Retired), last reviewed, next review and the vault path; the folder holds the content. When they disagree — including on ids, if an early draft numbered itself differently — **the index wins**; renumber the file, not the index.
- **The published site is public.** The rule from lesson 07 §7 applies with force here, because SOPs are exactly where credentials want to creep in: no passwords, API keys, personal data, salary figures or partner-confidential details in any SOP. The step says _which_ Bitwarden collection holds the credential and _which_ Drive folder holds the document — by name, never by content. When in doubt, the sensitive detail stays out of the repo altogether.
- **Blank masters vs live copies.** [[T08-sop-template|T08]] stays a blank master in the toolkit; live SOPs are company records and live in the `sops/` area of the vault, not inside this course's folder.

## 6. Versioning, the 180-day review, and killing SOPs

An SOP is a claim about how work happens; claims rot. Three mechanisms keep the corpus true:

1. **Versions and the change log.** Every material edit bumps the version and adds a change-log row — date, version, what changed, why, by whom. The log is what makes "the SOP said so" a usable answer six months later, and it is the audit trail a future diligence or auditor will ask for.
2. **The 180-day review.** Next review = last reviewed + 180 days; the index computes the date and the days remaining, and flags anything overdue. A review is small: the owner runs the task once against the document (or watches the current operator run it), fixes what drifted, bumps the date. With ~30 SOPs on a 180-day cycle that is about one per week in steady state — batch them into the quarterly-audit week ([[08-the-operating-cadence|lesson 08]] §7) and most are five-minute confirmations. Any process change triggers a review immediately; the calendar is the backstop, not the trigger.
3. **Killing.** An SOP nobody has run since its last review is either an automated task (good — link the automation, retire the manual steps into an exceptions appendix), an eliminated process (better), or a document nobody believed (fix or kill). Flip the index row to `Retired`, note why; git history keeps the content. **A stale SOP is worse than none** — the first time someone follows a wrong step, they stop trusting all of them, and the corpus dies of one bad document.

The discipline to defend is small and specific: **nobody edits how a process works without editing its SOP in the same sitting.** The change is not done until the document says so — the same rule engineering already applies to code and tests.

## 7. Training-by-doing and sign-off

A written SOP is a hypothesis; **sign-off is the experiment.** T08's rule 6 is the method: the next person runs the task _from the document_ while the author watches in silence. Every question they have to ask is a missing step; every hesitation is an unclear one. Fix the document the same day, run it again if the gaps were big, and only then flip the index row from Draft to Live. The test is deliberately humbling — most first drafts fail it, because the author's hands knew things their prose didn't.

This is the same **train-by-doing** discipline the [[ERPNext-Implementation-Guide|ERPNext guide]] uses for go-live — each person enters their own real transactions, reviewed daily — and it beats classroom-style walkthroughs for the same reason: the learning happens in the doing, and the doing exposes the document. For handovers, stage it the way [[10-customer-operations-support-and-success|lesson 10]] §10 stages the support queue: **shadow** (they watch you run the SOP once), **run with review** (they run it, you check the output for a few cycles), **own** (they hold the SOP, you read the metric). The stages turn a leap of faith into three small checks.

Two rules complete the mechanism. **Every critical SOP has a named backup** who has passed the same sign-off — the key-person risk row in [[T15-risk-register|T15]] is only mitigated by a second person who has actually done the task, not by the document's existence. And **sign-off is recorded**: the index's status flip plus the date is the record; for statutory-adjacent SOPs (payroll, close, compliance runs) note who signed off in the change log, because "trained and signed off" is exactly what a labour or tax question will one day ask.

## 8. Measuring SOP adoption

"We have SOPs" is an output; adoption is the outcome, and it has numbers. Three questions, each with a cheap measurement:

| Question                                         | Measure                                                                                                                                                                                   | Where it lives                                                 |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Coverage** — is the recurring work documented? | Live vs Draft vs Retired counts (the index computes them); the honest version is _hours_: what share of a normal week's recurring work has a Live SOP?                                    | `SOP Index` tab                                                |
| **Use** — do the SOPs actually run?              | Each SOP's own metrics row: on-time % (the scorecard filled by 10:00 streak), error/rework counts, minutes taken. A metric that never moves is a metric nobody enters — itself the answer | The SOP's Metrics block; exceptions surface in the ops meeting |
| **Drift** — does practice still match the page?  | The 180-day review's re-run (§6), plus one spot-check a quarter: watch a task you did not pick in advance, diff reality against the document                                              | Review notes in the change log                                 |

Read drift generously: **if practice is better than the document, the document was wrong — update it.** The operator who found a faster path did the company a favour; an SOP regime that punishes deviation teaches people to hide improvements, and you end up with compliance theatre — documents that are "followed" on paper and ignored in fact. If practice is worse, that is a training gap or a document gap; the fix is a session (§7) or an edit, never a scolding.

Two adoption signals need no spreadsheet. **Questions asked**: when the same question about a documented task arrives twice, the SOP failed — the answer belongs in the step (T08's rule). And **the handover test**: the day a task changes hands with nothing but the SOP and a sign-off session, adoption is real. Count those days; they are the autopilot actually assembling.

## 9. Walkthrough videos — supplements, never substitutes

Some steps are faster to show than to describe — a six-tap path through the dealer app, the Helpdesk triage screen, where the rate master hides its confirmation state. For these, record a **60-second screen walkthrough with a voice-over** — Hindi where natural, since the watcher may be a dealer's accountant or your next support hire — and link it from the SOP's step. Loom is the standard tool (free tier, then Business from roughly \$12.50–18/user/month — **VERIFY LIVE** on the vendor page; [Supademo — Loom pricing](https://supademo.com/blog/loom-pricing)), but any screen recorder plus a Drive folder does the job at ₹0; the recordings double as the WhatsApp-forwardable training assets of [[10-customer-operations-support-and-success|lesson 10]] §6.

The discipline is in the word _supplement_:

- **The written steps remain the source of truth.** A video cannot be diffed, skimmed, grepped or reviewed in five minutes; a step list can. The video shows _how the screen looks_; the SOP says _what must be true_ (the checks).
- **Videos age faster than text.** Every UI change orphans them silently — so each video is listed beside its SOP in the index's link column and dies or gets re-recorded at the same 180-day review.
- **Keep them short and single-purpose.** One video per step-cluster, sixty seconds, no intros. A 12-minute walkthrough is a meeting nobody can search.
- **The public-site rule applies** (§5): record against test data, never a real dealer's ledger or phone number.

## 10. At VSYST — applying this now

- **Write the index before the corpus.** The §4 table goes into the `SOP Index` tab this week, owners confirmed with each person, every status honestly `Draft`. An index of thirty Drafts is not embarrassment; it is the process map of the company on one screen — and it converts "we should document things" into thirty named, ownable tasks.
- **Promote what already exists instead of rewriting it.** [[T25-customer-support-sop|T25]], the dealer-onboarding checklist from lesson 10, the close checklist from lesson 06, the release gate and PR checklists living in the repos ([[tasks_12_tdd_testing/00-overview|tasks_12]]) — these are SOPs in all but format. Wrap them in T08 metadata, give them index rows, and where a mechanism already lives in code (the release gate), the SOP **references it and covers what the gate cannot see** — store listings, staged rollout, the dealer announcement — rather than duplicating steps that would drift.
- **One SOP per week is the whole plan.** The Wednesday build block ([[08-the-operating-cadence|lesson 08]] §11) exists for exactly this: one process written while doing it, every week, is the full starter index inside a year with no documentation sprint and no consultant. Sequence by the twice-rule and by handover: whatever you are about to hand to the Support/Ops Associate gets written first.
- **Drain the founders' heads through the existing slots.** The weekly domain-knowledge 1:1 already has a one-written-item rule ([[08-the-operating-cadence|lesson 08]] §5) — point its output here: the domain-expert director's dealer patterns become steps and exception branches in SOP-CUS-002 and the churn playbook; the technical founder's deploy and restore knowledge becomes SOP-ENG-001 and 003. Thirty years of fuel-retail judgement will not fit in an SOP — capture the _decisions_ in playbook form and the _routines_ in steps.
- **What not to do:** no SOP software (the vault is the tool, and it is ₹0); no 30-page documents (split them); no SOPs for one-offs or for processes still changing weekly — simplify first, standardise second ([[18-automation-and-ai-in-operations|lesson 18]]'s ladder); and no ghost-written SOPs — the person who does the task writes it, or the document starts life untrue.
